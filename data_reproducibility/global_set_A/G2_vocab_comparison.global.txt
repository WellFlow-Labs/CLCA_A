## Narrative Analysis

### Recurring Partition Patterns

**The factual correctness / deliberate deception split is universal.** Every language in the dataset maintains a lexical distinction between terms evaluating whether statements correspond to facts and terms marking intentional false representation. The deception terms consistently carry stronger moral valence and tend to be more lexically elaborated on the negative pole (liars are named; truth-tellers are not). This split is not merely a matter of having separate words: the two domains show different constructional behavior, different collocational patterns, and different derivational productivity across all eight languages.

**The authenticity/genuineness domain is universally present but variably organized.** All eight languages distinguish terms for genuine, original, or authentic objects from terms for fakes, counterfeits, or imitations. However, the relationship between this domain and the factual truth domain varies. In Georgian, ნამდვილი (namdvili) participates in both authenticity and factual truth contexts. In Finnish, aito (genuine) is kept more separate from tosi (true). In Indonesian, asli (authentic) and benar (correct) are clearly distinct. In Yoruba, gidi (genuine) is a separate item from tọ́ (correct). The degree of overlap between "genuine object" and "true statement" vocabulary is a point of cross-language variation rather than a universal.

**Moral rightness and justice are present in all languages but organized differently.** All eight languages have vocabulary for moral rightness, justice, or fairness that is related to but distinct from factual truth vocabulary. However, the degree of integration varies considerably. Turkish hak- merges rights, justice, truth, and religious reference into a single root with extensive derivational elaboration. Georgian mart'l- similarly spans correctness, moral rightness, and legal justice. By contrast, Yoruba keeps òdodo (justice/righteousness) more separate from tọ́ (correct), and Korean maintains a clear distinction between 정당하다 (legitimate) and 맞다 (correct). The merger of factual correctness with moral rightness within a single root is a feature of some languages (Georgian, Turkish, Basque zuzen-) but not others (Yoruba, Korean, Finnish).

**Discourse markers derived from truth-domain roots appear in all eight languages.** Georgian მართლაც, Yoruba nitootọ́, Finnish sebenarnya/totta puhuen, Basque benetan, Tamil uṇmaiyil, Indonesian sebenarnya, Turkish gerçekten, and Korean 사실은/솔직히 all function as grammaticalized discourse markers introducing corrections, frank statements, or factual clarifications. This grammaticalization pattern is cross-linguistically robust and appears to be a general tendency rather than a contact phenomenon.

**Reliability and credibility form a distinct cluster in all languages.** Every language has vocabulary for evaluating whether sources, persons, or information can be trusted, and this cluster is consistently kept separate from vocabulary for evaluating the truth-value of specific statements. Georgian სანდო (reliable) vs. საეჭვო (doubtful); Yoruba dájú (certain) and gbàgbọ́ (believe); Finnish luotettava (reliable) and uskottava (credible); Basque fidagarria (reliable) and zalantzagarria (doubtful); Tamil nampakam (reliability) and cantēkam (doubt); Indonesian güvenilir (reliable) and şüpheli (doubtful); Turkish güvenilir and şüpheli; Korean 신빙성 (credibility). The reliability cluster is consistently gradable (credibility can be high or low) in contrast to core truth predicates, which resist degree modification in several languages.

### Where Languages Split More Finely

**Tamil splits the truth-action domain most finely.** Tamil distinguishes speech acts of truth-telling (Group 1: uṇmaiyai col), concealment and revelation (Group 2: uṇmaiyai maṟai/veḷippaṭuttu), epistemic pursuit (Group 3: uṇmaiyai tēṭu), and acceptance of truth (Group 4: uṇmaiyai ēṟṟu) as separate groupings. Other languages have vocabulary for these distinctions but do not organize them into as many separate clusters. This fine-grained splitting may reflect the extensive verb+object collocation system in Tamil, where the same noun (uṇmai) combines with different verbs to create distinct semantic groups.

**Yoruba splits investigation and verification into more sub-groups than other languages.** Yoruba's F3 analysis identifies separate groups for investigation/verification processes, explanation/clarification, record/documentation, and evidence/demonstration — all built from the productive ṣe + noun construction. Other languages have vocabulary in these areas but do not show the same degree of lexical differentiation. This may reflect the productivity of the ṣe construction in Yoruba, which generates a large number of distinct action-denoting expressions.

**Finnish splits deception and distortion into separate groups.** Finnish distinguishes deliberate deception (Group 4: valhe, valehdella, petollinen) from distortion and misrepresentation (Group 12: vääristää, vääristely, harhaanjohtava). The distinction is between creating false representations (lying, forging) and corrupting existing true representations (distorting, misrepresenting). Other languages have vocabulary for both but do not consistently organize them into separate groups.

**Korean splits practical reality into a large independent cluster.** Korean's 실- (sil-) compounds form a distinct group (Group 8) covering actual practice, implementation, real-world application, and concrete results, with no clear parallel in other languages at this level of elaboration. The distinction between 실제 (actual reality) and 진실 (truth of statements) is maintained more explicitly in Korean than in most other languages.

### Where Languages Merge Distinctions

**Indonesian merges the most dimensions within the appropriateness/suitability group.** Indonesian Group 7 combines cocok (matching/compatible), wajar (reasonable/natural), pantas (appropriate/proper/deserved), tepat (precise/exact/appropriate), and pas (exact fit) into a single group. Other languages keep some of these distinctions separate: Yoruba has a dedicated appropriateness group (yẹ) distinct from its correspondence group (bá mu); Basque keeps egokia (appropriate) separate from berdina (same/equal); Finnish keeps appropriateness terms (oikea-aikainen) peripheral. Indonesian's merger may reflect the broad semantic range of the benar root, which licenses a wide range of correctness-related evaluations.

**Turkish merges reality, genuineness, and naturalness into a single group.** Turkish Group 2 combines gerçek (real/genuine), asıl (original/essential), özgün (authentic/original), taklit (imitation), doğal (natural), and yapay (artificial) into a single authenticity/reality assessment group. Other languages tend to keep the natural/artificial distinction (Finnish bunebrivi/khelovnuri; Georgian ბუნებრივი/ხელოვნური) somewhat separate from the genuine/counterfeit distinction. Turkish's merger may reflect the semantic range of gerçek, which covers both "real" (as opposed to imaginary) and "genuine" (as opposed to fake).

**Georgian merges sincerity and emotional genuineness with authenticity.** Georgian's F3 analysis identifies a separate group for sincerity and emotional genuineness (Group 4: გულწრფელი, ლიცემერი) that overlaps with the authenticity group. Other languages have vocabulary for sincerity (Finnish vilpitön, Turkish dürüst, Korean 진심) but tend to treat it as part of the honest character group rather than as a separate cluster. Georgian's separation may reflect the productivity of the გულ- (heart) root in sincerity expressions.

### Cases Where a Language Lacks a Clear Lexical Contrast Found Elsewhere

**Yoruba lacks a dedicated lexical antonym for gidi (genuine).** While all other languages have a clear lexical pair for genuine vs. fake (Georgian ნამდვილი/ყალბი, Finnish aito/väärennös, Basque benetakoa/faltsua, Tamil acal/pōli, Indonesian asli/palsu, Turkish gerçek/sahte, Korean 진짜/가짜), Yoruba expresses inauthenticity through negation (kì í ṣe gidi "it is not genuine") rather than a dedicated lexical item. This is the clearest case of an asymmetric lexicalization in the authenticity domain.

**Korean lacks a productive inchoative for propositional truth change.** While all other languages have some way to express "becoming true" or "becoming false" for propositions (Georgian გახდა constructions, Yoruba di constructions, Finnish toteutua/osoittautua, Basque bihurtu, Tamil āku, Indonesian menjadi, Turkish olmak/çıkmak), Korean treats truth as a static property that is discovered or revealed rather than something that changes. The constructions "진실이 되다" (become truth) and "거짓이 되다" (become false) are marginal or pragmatically restricted; Korean instead uses discovery verbs (드러나다, 밝혀지다). This is the most clearly documented case of a language-specific conceptual constraint on truth-domain constructions.

**Finnish lacks a common expression for "becoming untrue."** While Finnish has productive inchoative constructions for many truth-domain terms (toteutua "come true," todellistua "be realized"), it lacks a common expression for the reverse process — something that was true becoming false. The gap is documented explicitly in F6: "Finnish lacks specific verb for 'become suddenly false.'" Other languages handle this through discovery constructions (osoittautua vääräksi "turn out to be false") rather than a true inchoative. This parallels the Korean pattern but is less absolute.

**Tamil lacks dedicated vocabulary for objectivity/subjectivity.** While Georgian explicitly groups ობიექტური/სუბიექტური as a distinct semantic cluster, and other languages have borrowed these terms, Tamil's F3 analysis notes no dedicated group for objectivity/subjectivity. The distinction exists in Tamil (borrowed terms are available) but does not generate a distinct lexical cluster in the data.

**Indonesian and Korean lack a dedicated group for fiction vs. fact.** Georgian (Group 9: ფიქტიური vs. ნამდვილი ისტორია), Finnish (Group 15: tosielämä, tositarina), and Tamil (Group 16: uṇmai nikaḻvu, uṇmai katai) show dedicated vocabulary for distinguishing real events and true stories from fictional ones. Indonesian and Korean have relevant vocabulary (Indonesian tositarina-type expressions are absent; Korean 진짜 can be used) but do not show a distinct lexical cluster for this contrast.

**Basque lacks a dedicated group for epistemic certainty/probability.** While Yoruba (dájú, ìdájú), Finnish (todennäköinen, todennäköisyys), Tamil (uṟuti, cantēkam), Indonesian (kesin, şüpheli), Turkish (kesin, şüpheli), and Korean (확실하다, 불확실성) all show distinct vocabulary for epistemic certainty and probability, Basque's F3 analysis does not identify a separate group for this domain. Basque has ziurra (certain) and zalantzagarria (doubtful) but these are grouped under reliability/credibility rather than forming an independent epistemic certainty cluster.

### Distinguishing Well-Attested Cross-Language Patterns from Single-Language Observations

**Well-attested across six or more languages:**
- Lexical separation of deliberate lying from factual error (all eight languages)
- Agentive nominalization asymmetry: "liar" lexicalized, "truth-teller" not (documented in Georgian, Yoruba, Finnish, Tamil, Indonesian, Korean; implied in Basque and Turkish)
- Productive abstract nominalization from truth-domain adjectives (all eight languages)
- Discourse markers derived from truth-domain roots (all eight languages)
- Resistance to degree modification of core truth predicates (documented in Yoruba, Korean, Finnish, Basque, Tamil; implied in others)
- Separation of authenticity/genuineness from factual truth (all eight languages)
- Proverbial expressions asserting truth's ultimate triumph or difficulty (all eight languages)

**Attested in three to five languages:**
- Taste metaphors for truth and lies (Turkish, Yoruba, and implied in Korean's "white lie" calque)
- Absence of a simple verb meaning "to tell the truth" parallel to a dedicated "to lie" verb (Finnish, Yoruba, Korean, Tamil)
- Asymmetric lexicalization of negative compounds (Finnish, Indonesian; implied in others)
- Merger of factual correctness with moral rightness within a single root (Georgian, Turkish, Basque)

**Single-language observations:**
- Extension of "lie/false" to "futile/in vain" (Georgian ტყუილი only)
- Three-way register stratification of truth vocabulary (Tamil only)
- Advancement/progress group sharing morphology with truth terms (Korean 진/실 only)
- Objectivity/subjectivity as an explicitly grouped semantic cluster (Georgian F3 analysis only — may reflect analytical choice)
- Organizational management and live broadcasting as extensions of a correctness root (Basque zuzen- only)
- Elaborated rights/justice vocabulary as a major sub-domain of the truth field (Turkish hak- most extensively; Georgian mart'l-/samart'l- secondarily)
- Elaborated practical reality vs. theory cluster (Korean 실- compounds most extensively)
- Elaborated correspondence/matching cluster (Yoruba bá mu/ìbámu/jọ most extensively)

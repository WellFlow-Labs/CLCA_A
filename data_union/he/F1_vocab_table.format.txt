| אֱמֶת | ʾemet | truth, faithfulness; root א-מ-ת (ʾ-m-t), related to firmness, reliability | truth, truthfulness, veracity, authenticity, faithfulness, reality, genuineness | The most central and widely used Hebrew term for truth, appearing across biblical, rabbinic, legal, religious, philosophical, and everyday modern contexts. It can describe a true statement, a genuine object, a reliable person, or a quality of genuine reliability. In rabbinic literature it is also treated as a foundational moral value. Appears in fixed expressions such as *ʾemet ve-ʾemuna* ("truth and faithfulness"), *ʾemet ve-shalom* ("truth and peace"), and *le-ʾamet* (to verify/confirm). Participates in extensive derivational patterns. | [CORE] |
| אמיתי | ʾamiti | true, real, genuine; adjectival form derived from *ʾemet* | true, real, genuine, authentic | The standard modern Hebrew adjective meaning "true" or "genuine," used attributively and predicatively across registers. It applies to statements, objects, feelings, and identities, and is used to describe persons, feelings, objects, or states as genuinely what they appear to be. Frequently used in everyday speech to distinguish the real from the fake or the sincere from the performative. Slightly more colloquial than *ʾemet* itself. Contrasts with *medumaʾ* ("imagined"), *meumat* ("fabricated"), and *mezuyaf* ("forged/fake"). Example: *ʾohev ʾamitʾi* ("a true/genuine friend"). | [CORE] |
| נכון | nakhon | correct, right, established; root כ-ו-נ (k-w-n), to be established, prepared, firm, correct | correct, right, accurate, true, proper | Widely used to evaluate statements, answers, facts, and procedures as accurate or correct, particularly in everyday and educational contexts. It is the standard response to confirm that something is accurate or has been done properly. Also carries a sense of moral or practical rightness in some collocations, e.g., *ze lo nakhon* ("that's not right/correct"). Less philosophically weighted than *ʾemet* but extremely frequent. Participates in derivations such as *nekhonah* (f.) and *le-nakhen* ("to correct"). | [CORE] |
| מדויק | meduyaq | precise, exact; Pu'al/Mefuʿal passive participle from root ד-י-ק (d-y-q), related to fineness, exactness | accurate, precise, exact | Used to evaluate the degree of correspondence between a representation, measurement, statement, description, or translation and the reality or standard it describes. Common in scientific, technical, journalistic, and legal contexts. Implies a higher standard of exactness than *nakhon*. Contrasts with *meʿurpal* ("vague") or *meshuʿar* ("approximate"). Example: *tiʾur meduyaq* ("an accurate description"). The root also yields *diyuq* ("precision, exactness") and *ledayeq* ("to be precise"). | [CORE] |
| אותנטי | ʾotenti | authentic; loanword from Greek/Latin *authenticus* via European languages | authentic, genuine | A borrowing used in modern Hebrew, especially in cultural, artistic, philosophical, and psychological contexts, to evaluate whether something is genuine or original rather than derivative or imitative. More restricted in register than *ʾamiti* and tends to appear in educated, academic, or journalistic discourse. Sometimes interchangeable with *meqori* in aesthetic contexts. Common in discussions of personal identity or cultural heritage. Functions as a direct parallel to English "authentic." | [CORE] |
| מקורי | meqori | original; derived from מָקוֹר (*maqor*, source, origin); root ק-ו-ר (q-w-r) | original, genuine, authentic | Describes something that is the original rather than a copy or imitation, and by extension something that is genuinely creative or not derivative. Used in aesthetic, legal (intellectual property), and commercial evaluation to evaluate whether something is an original rather than a copy. Can describe artworks, documents, ideas, or personal style. Overlaps with *ʾotenti* in some contexts but emphasizes origin rather than genuineness of character per se. Example: *yetsirah meqorit* ("an original work"). Contrasts with *ha-ʿataqah* ("the copy") or *ziyyuf* ("forgery"). | [CORE] |
| אמין | ʾamin | reliable, trustworthy; adjectival form from root א-מ-נ (ʾ-m-n), same root family as *ʾemet* | reliable, trustworthy, credible, dependable | Closely related to *mehiman* but somewhat more colloquial and broadly used. Applied to persons, sources, witnesses, systems, and information to indicate dependability and accuracy. Common in modern Hebrew in contexts such as journalism, law, and everyday evaluation of people. Contrasts with *lo ʾamin* (unreliable). The noun *ʾaminut* is the standard modern Hebrew term for "reliability" or "credibility." | [CORE] |
| אמינות | ʾaminut | reliability, trustworthiness; abstract noun from root א-מ-נ (ʾ-m-n) | reliability, credibility, trustworthiness | Refers to the quality of being believable and dependable, applied to persons, institutions, sources, and testimony. Central in legal and journalistic discourse when evaluating the weight to be given to a witness or source. A key term in legal evaluation of witnesses (*ʾaminut ha-ʿed*, "the credibility of the witness") and in journalistic and academic assessment of sources. Frequently appears in the phrase *behinat ha-ʾaminut* ("examination of credibility"). Closely related to *mehiman* and *neʾeman*. | [CORE] |
| מהימן | mehiman | trustworthy, reliable; Hif'il participial form from root א-מ-נ (ʾ-m-n), to trust, be faithful | reliable, trustworthy, credible | Evaluates persons, sources, witnesses, and information as trustworthy and credible. Used especially in legal and formal contexts to describe witnesses, testimony, or sources deemed credible. Slightly more formal than *ʾamin* and more common in written legal Hebrew. Common in legal testimony contexts (*ʿed mehiman*, "a credible witness") and in journalism. Shares a root with *ʾemet* and *ʾemunah*. The noun *mehimanut* also occurs. | [CORE] |
| אמונה | ʾemunah | faithfulness, trust, belief; from root א-מ-נ (ʾ-m-n) | faith, trust, faithfulness, reliability | Denotes both the quality of being trustworthy or faithful and the act of believing or trusting. Used in religious, moral, and interpersonal contexts; *ʾemunah* in a person implies they are reliable and honest. The root connects it to *neʾeman* ("faithful, reliable") and *maʾamin* ("believer"). | [CORE] |
| נאמן | neʾeman | faithful, loyal, trustworthy; passive/stative pattern from root א-מ-נ (ʾ-m-n), "one who is trusted" | faithful, loyal, trustworthy, reliable | Describes persons who are dependable and honest in fulfilling obligations or representing facts accurately. Used in legal contexts (*neʾeman le-ʿedut*, "competent/reliable as a witness") and in everyday moral evaluation. Also used as a title, e.g., *neʾeman bayit* ("a trusted household steward"). | [CORE] |
| כנות | kanut | sincerity, honesty; abstract noun from כָּן (*kan*, sincere, honest); root כ-נ-נ or כ-ו-נ | sincerity, honesty, genuineness, candor | Refers to the quality of being sincere or honest in one's expressions, intentions, or conduct, without concealment or pretense. Used in moral and interpersonal evaluation to evaluate whether a person's behavior or statements reflect their true inner state. Example: *medaber be-kanut* ("speaking with sincerity"). Contrasts with *sheqer* (falsehood) and *tsevʿiut* ("hypocrisy") in moral discourse. The adjective *ken* and adverb *be-kannut* ("sincerely, honestly") are closely related. | [CORE] |
| כן | ken | upright, sincere, honest; from root כ-נ-נ or כ-ו-נ | sincere, honest, candid, genuine | Describes a person or statement as straightforward and free from deception. Used in moral evaluation of persons and their speech. Example: *adam ken* ("an honest/sincere person"). Participates in derivations including *kanut* ("sincerity") and *le-hiyot ken* ("to be honest"). | [CORE] |
| שֶׁקֶר | sheqer | falsehood, lie; root ש-ק-ר (sh-q-r) | falsehood, lie, deception, fraud, untruth | The primary Hebrew term for falsehood or lying, and the primary antonym of *ʾemet*, used across moral, legal, religious, and everyday contexts. Appears in the biblical prohibition *midvar sheqer tirḥaq* ("distance yourself from falsehood") and in modern Hebrew to describe lies, false testimony, and fraudulent representations. Also used attributively, as in *ʿed sheqer* ("false witness"). Defines the negative pole against which *ʾemet* is measured. Participates in derivations such as *shakran* ("liar") and *shaqri* ("false, spurious"). | [CORE] |
| זיוף | ziyyuf | forgery, falsification; verbal noun from root ז-י-פ (z-y-f), to falsify, counterfeit | forgery, falsification, counterfeiting, fraud | Refers to the act or product of deliberately falsifying documents, artworks, currency, signatures, or other representations. A standard legal and administrative term. Example: *ziyyuf mismakh* ("document forgery"). Closely related to *mezuyaf* ("forged") and *mezayef* ("forger"). The verb *lezayef* means "to forge" or "to falsify." | [CORE] |
| מזויף | mezuyaf | forged, falsified, counterfeit; Pu'al passive participle from root ז-י-פ (z-y-f) | forged, fake, counterfeit, falsified | The adjectival/participial form describing something that has been falsified or is not genuine. Applied to documents, signatures, artworks, currency, credentials, and identities that have been deliberately made to appear genuine when they are not. Common in legal and journalistic contexts. Example: *teʿudat zehut mezuyefet* ("a forged identity document"). Directly contrasts with *meqori* (original) and *ʾotenti* (authentic). | [CORE] |
| צֶדֶק | tsedeq | justice, righteousness; root צ-ד-ק (ts-d-q), to be right, just | justice, righteousness, rightness | A foundational term in biblical, rabbinic, and modern Hebrew for justice and moral rightness, used in evaluating conduct, judgments, and social arrangements. Used in legal contexts (*mishpat u-tsedeq*, "justice and righteousness") and in evaluating whether an action or judgment is correct or fair. The related verb *tsadaq* means "to be right" or "to be justified." Participates in a large derivational family including *tsaddiq* ("righteous person") and *letsdiq* ("to justify, to vindicate"). Closely related to *tsaddiq* (righteous person) and *tsedaqa* (righteousness, charity). | [CORE] |
| צדיק | tsaddiq | righteous, just person; root צ-ד-ק (ts-d-q); intensive adjectival form | righteous, just, upright | Describes a person whose conduct conforms to moral and religious standards of rightness. A key evaluative term in biblical, rabbinic, and modern Hebrew moral discourse. Can also be used more loosely in modern Hebrew to describe someone who is blameless in a particular situation. | [CORE] |
| ישר | yashar | straight, upright, honest; root י-ש-ר (y-sh-r) | upright, honest, straightforward, just | A moral and evaluative term describing persons and conduct as morally straight, honest, and without deception or deviation. Appears extensively in biblical and rabbinic literature and remains in use in modern Hebrew in moral and legal discourse. Appears in the biblical phrase *ha-yashar be-ʿeynav* ("what is right in his eyes"). Yields derivations such as *yosher* ("uprightness, integrity") and *le-yasher* ("to straighten, to approve"). | [CORE] |
| יושר | yosher | uprightness, integrity, honesty; abstract noun from root י-ש-ר (y-sh-r) | integrity, uprightness, honesty, fairness | Refers to the quality of moral uprightness or integrity in a person's conduct or character. Used in ethical and legal discourse, sometimes approximating "equity" or "fairness." Appears in fixed expressions such as *middat ha-yosher* ("the standard of fairness/equity") and *yosher lev* ("integrity of heart"). Used in modern contract law discourse. Closely related to *yashar* but functions as an abstract quality noun rather than an attributive adjective. Example: *ʾish yosher* ("a man of integrity"). | [CORE] |
| רמאות | ramaʾut | deceit, fraud; abstract noun from root ר-מ-א (r-m-ʾ), to deceive, to cheat | deceit, fraud, cheating, trickery | Denotes the practice of deliberately misleading others, used in moral, legal, and everyday contexts to evaluate conduct as dishonest or fraudulent. Appears in legal discourse concerning commercial fraud and in everyday moral condemnation. The root yields *rammaʾi* ("deceiver, cheat") and *leramot* ("to deceive"). | [CORE] |
| הונאה | honaʾah | deception, fraud; verbal noun from root א-נ-ה in Hif'il pattern, to deceive, to defraud | deception, fraud, swindling | Used in legal and moral contexts to describe deliberate misleading of another person, particularly for material gain. Appears in contract law and consumer protection discourse. Example: *honaʾah be-miqaḥ u-memkar* ("fraud in buying and selling," a classical legal category). Yields *meʾoneh* ("deceiver") and *lehaʾot* ("to deceive"). | [CORE] |
| צביעות | tsevʿiut | hypocrisy, insincerity; from *tsavoaʿ* ("dyed, colored"); abstract noun; "being two-colored, hypocrisy" | hypocrisy, insincerity, two-facedness | Denotes the quality of presenting a false appearance of virtue or sincerity while concealing contrary intentions or conduct. Used in moral evaluation of persons and public figures. Example: *ze tsevʿiut mehuḥatsefet* ("that is blatant hypocrisy"). Contrasts with *kanut* ("sincerity") and *yosher* ("integrity"). | [CORE] |
| תקין | taqin | proper, in order, functioning correctly; root ת-ק-נ (t-q-n), to fix, correct, regulate | proper, correct, in order, functioning correctly, valid | Evaluates whether something is functioning correctly, is in proper order, or conforms to a standard — a machine, a procedure, a document, or conduct. Common in administrative, technical, and legal contexts. In legal and bureaucratic contexts it can indicate that a document or process is formally valid and correct. Implies conformity to a standard or norm rather than moral rightness per se. | [CORE] |
| תקף | taqef | valid, in force; root ת-ק-פ (t-q-p), strength, force, to be strong, valid, in force | valid, in force, effective, legally binding | Used primarily in legal and administrative contexts to describe documents, contracts, licenses, laws, or decisions that are currently in force and legally effective. Contrasts with *lo taqef* (invalid, expired) and *batel* ("null, void"). The noun *toqef* means validity or force. | [CORE] |
| בטל | batel | null, void, invalid; root ב-ט-ל (b-t-l), to be null, cease, be annulled | null, void, invalid, annulled | The standard legal and everyday term for something that is null, void, or without effect. Used in legal discourse (contracts, rulings) and in rabbinic law. The verb *levatel* means "to annul, cancel, nullify." | [CORE] |
| חוקי | ḥuqi | legal, lawful; derived from חוֹק (*ḥoq*, law, statute); root ח-ק-ק (ḥ-q-q) | legal, lawful, legitimate | Evaluates whether an action, document, or status conforms to law. Used in legal and administrative contexts to distinguish what is permitted or valid under law from what is not. Contrasts with *bilti ḥuqi* (illegal, unlawful). | [CORE] |
| לגיטימי | legitimi | legitimate; loanword from Latin/European *legitimus* | legitimate, valid, justified | Used in political, legal, and moral discourse to evaluate whether a claim, authority, action, or representation is justified or valid according to recognized standards. Functions as a direct parallel to English "legitimate" and is common in modern Hebrew public discourse. | [CORE] |
| כשר | kasher | fit, proper, valid; root כ-ש-ר (k-sh-r), fitness, suitability | fit, proper, valid, legitimate, kosher (in religious law) | Evaluates whether something meets the relevant standard of fitness or validity. In religious law, evaluates whether food, objects, or persons meet the required standards of fitness. Used broadly in rabbinic and legal contexts to mean "valid" or "fit," e.g., *ʿed kasher* ("a valid/competent witness"), *get kasher* ("a valid bill of divorce"). In broader modern Hebrew usage, *kasher* can describe any person or thing that is legitimate, proper, or above suspicion. Contrasts directly with *pasul* ("invalid, disqualified"). The noun *kashrut* refers to the state of being fit or valid as well as the dietary system. | [CORE] |
| פסול | pasul | disqualified, invalid, unfit; Qal passive participle from root פ-ס-ל (p-s-l), to disqualify, to hew away, to invalidate | invalid, disqualified, unfit, inadmissible | Evaluates persons, evidence, documents, or ritual objects as disqualified or invalid according to a relevant standard. Used in legal, religious, and general contexts to evaluate something or someone as failing to meet required standards and therefore invalid or inadmissible. Widely used in rabbinic law, e.g., a witness who is *pasul le-ʿedut* ("disqualified from testimony"), and in modern Israeli legal and administrative contexts. Example: *edut pesula* ("inadmissible testimony"). Contrasts directly with *kasher*. | [CORE] |
| סביר | savir | reasonable, plausible; root ס-ב-ר (s-b-r), to think, suppose, consider plausible | reasonable, plausible, credible | Evaluates whether a claim, explanation, or course of action is reasonable or plausible given available evidence or norms. Central in legal discourse, e.g., *ha-ʾadam ha-savir* ("the reasonable person" standard). Also used in everyday contexts to assess whether something makes sense or is believable. The noun *sevirut* denotes reasonableness. | [CORE] |
| מהות | mahut | essence, nature; derived from מָה (*ma*, what) + abstract suffix; "whatness, essence" | essence, nature, substance, what something fundamentally is | A philosophical and general-purpose term for the fundamental nature or defining character of something, used in contexts where one evaluates what something truly or essentially is. | עצם | ʿetsem | bone, essence, self, very; root ע-צ-מ (ʿ-ts-m), strength, selfhood | essence, the very thing itself, substance; (in compounds) the actual, the real | Used in philosophical and legal Hebrew to indicate the essential or intrinsic nature of something, or to emphasize identity. Often appears in the construction *be-ʿetsem* ("in essence, actually") or *ʿetsem ha-davar* ("the very matter itself"). Also functions as a reflexive intensifier. In legal Hebrew, *ʿetsem ha-ʿinyan* means "the substance of the matter." Its use as an essence-term is more contextually determined than *mahut*. | [CORE] |
| דומה | dome | similar, resembling; root ד-מ-ה (d-m-h), to resemble, liken | similar, resembling, like | Evaluates resemblance or likeness between two entities. Used across all registers, including everyday and legal contexts (e.g., evaluating whether a copy resembles an original, or whether cases are analogous). The related noun *dimyon* means "resemblance" or "similarity," and the verb *ledamot* means "to resemble" or "to compare." | [CORE] |
| דמיון | dimyon | resemblance, likeness; from root ד-מ-ה (d-m-h), to resemble, to be like | resemblance, similarity, likeness; (also) imagination | In evaluative contexts, used to assess the degree to which one thing resembles another, relevant to questions of authenticity, imitation, and representation. The same word also means "imagination," which can create ambiguity; context disambiguates. Appears in aesthetic and legal discussions of imitation and copying. | [CORE] |
| דיוק | diyyuq | precision, exactness; verbal noun from root ד-י-ק (d-y-q), fineness, exactness | precision, accuracy, exactness | Refers to the quality of being exact or precise in a statement, measurement, or representation. Common expressions include *be-diyyuq* ("precisely, exactly") and *ḥoser diyyuq* ("lack of precision, inaccuracy"). The verb *ledayeq* means "to be precise" or "to examine closely." | הגון | hagun | decent, proper, fitting; root ה-ג-נ (h-g-n), related to propriety; etymology debated | decent, proper, respectable, fitting, fair | Evaluates persons, conduct, or offers as conforming to standards of decency, fairness, and propriety.

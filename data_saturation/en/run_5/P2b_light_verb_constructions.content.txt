# CLCA Protocol v3.4 — P2b Compositional Constructions
**Language: English (en)**

---

**Note on transliteration:** English uses the Latin script natively; transliteration is not applicable for any item below.

**Scope note:** This step documents attested multi-word constructions involving P1 base forms. Derivational forms already documented in P1b and P2a are excluded. Constructions already documented in P1b (e.g., *truth be told*, *in truth*, *true to life*) are cross-referenced rather than re-expanded. The focus is on periphrastic, idiomatic, and syntactically-driven multi-word units. Absence of constructions is recorded explicitly where no common expression is identified.

---

## 1. Base Form: *true*

---

**Construction 1**
- Base Form: *true*
- Example: *come true*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: (of a hope, dream, or prediction) to be realized or fulfilled
- Usage notes: Used in everyday speech to describe the realization of a wished-for or predicted state. The construction is verb-phrase-final and typically follows a subject denoting a hope, dream, or prediction ("her dream came true"). Cross-referenced from P1b Root 2 peripheral extensions.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 2**
- Base Form: *true*
- Example: *ring true*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: to seem genuine, plausible, or consistent with what one knows
- Usage notes: Used in evaluative contexts to describe a statement or account that has the quality of authenticity or plausibility when assessed by a listener. The construction is intransitive and typically appears in negative or conditional contexts ("that doesn't quite ring true"). Cross-referenced from P1b Root 2 peripheral extensions.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 3**
- Base Form: *true*
- Example: *hold true*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: to remain valid or applicable; to continue to be the case
- Usage notes: Used in formal and everyday discourse to assert that a principle, rule, or statement remains valid under specified conditions. "The same principle holds true in all cases."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 4**
- Base Form: *true*
- Example: *prove true*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: to turn out to be correct or accurate
- Usage notes: Used in narrative and evaluative contexts to describe a prediction, suspicion, or claim that is subsequently confirmed by events. "His fears proved true."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 5**
- Base Form: *true*
- Example: *too good to be true*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: so favorable as to seem implausible or likely to be deceptive
- Usage notes: A fixed idiomatic expression used to express skepticism about an apparently advantageous offer or situation. Cross-referenced from P1b Root 2 peripheral extensions.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 6**
- Base Form: *true*
- Example: *true to one's word*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: keeping a promise; doing what one said one would do
- Usage notes: Used in moral and interpersonal evaluative contexts to describe a person who fulfills a stated commitment. "She was true to her word and returned the money."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 7**
- Base Form: *true*
- Example: *true to life*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: accurately representing real life; realistic
- Usage notes: Used in aesthetic and literary evaluation. Already documented in P1b Root 2, item 26; cross-referenced here.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 8**
- Base Form: *true*
- Example: *true to form*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: behaving in a way consistent with one's established pattern or character
- Usage notes: Used in everyday and journalistic discourse, often with a slightly ironic tone. Already documented in P1b Root 2, item 27; cross-referenced here.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 9**
- Base Form: *true*
- Example: *out of true*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: not properly aligned; deviating from the correct position or angle
- Usage notes: Used in technical and craft contexts (carpentry, engineering) to describe a surface, edge, or structure that is not perfectly straight or aligned. "The wheel had come out of true."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 10**
- Base Form: *true*
- Example: *true enough*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: sufficiently accurate to be accepted; used to concede a point while implying qualification
- Usage notes: Functions as a discourse marker acknowledging partial or qualified agreement. "True enough, but that is not the whole story."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

## 2. Base Form: *truth*

---

**Construction 1**
- Base Form: *truth*
- Example: *tell the truth*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: to state what is actually the case; to be honest
- Usage notes: A basic verb-object collocation used across all registers, including legal contexts (oath formulas) and everyday discourse. Also functions as a discourse marker in the phrase *to tell the truth* (see P1b Root 2, item 24).
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 2**
- Base Form: *truth*
- Example: *truth be told*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: to be honest; frankly speaking
- Usage notes: A fixed discourse marker used to introduce a candid admission. Already documented in P1b Root 2, item 21; cross-referenced here.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 3**
- Base Form: *truth*
- Example: *in truth*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: in fact; actually
- Usage notes: A fixed prepositional phrase used as a discourse marker. Already documented in P1b Root 2, item 22; cross-referenced here.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 4**
- Base Form: *truth*
- Example: *the truth of the matter*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: what is actually the case; the real facts
- Usage notes: A fixed collocation used to signal that one is about to state the actual facts. Already documented in P1b Root 2, item 25; cross-referenced here.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 5**
- Base Form: *truth*
- Example: *the truth will out*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: the truth will eventually become known, regardless of attempts to conceal it
- Usage notes: A proverbial expression used in moral and narrative contexts. The archaic use of *out* as an intransitive verb ("to come out") is preserved in this fixed form.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 6**
- Base Form: *truth*
- Example: *stretch the truth*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: to exaggerate or distort the facts without outright lying
- Usage notes: Used in everyday and journalistic discourse to describe a statement that is misleading but not entirely false. "He was known to stretch the truth when it suited him."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 7**
- Base Form: *truth*
- Example: *bend the truth*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: to distort or misrepresent the facts; to be less than fully honest
- Usage notes: Used in everyday discourse similarly to *stretch the truth*, with a slightly stronger implication of deliberate distortion. "She had been bending the truth for years."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 8**
- Base Form: *truth*
- Example: *moment of truth*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: a critical point at which the true nature of something is revealed or a decisive test must be faced
- Usage notes: Used in everyday and journalistic discourse to describe a decisive or revelatory moment. Borrowed from Spanish *momento de la verdad* (bullfighting terminology). "The moment of truth came when the results were announced."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 9**
- Base Form: *truth*
- Example: *truth commission*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: an official body established to investigate and document past human rights abuses or political violence
- Usage notes: Used in political science and journalism to refer to bodies such as South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission. Functions as a compound noun.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 10**
- Base Form: *truth*
- Example: *truth serum*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: a drug supposedly capable of inducing a person to tell the truth under interrogation
- Usage notes: Used in journalistic, legal, and popular discourse. The term is informal and the concept is scientifically contested; no drug reliably produces truthful disclosure.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 11**
- Base Form: *truth*
- Example: *home truth*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: an unpleasant but accurate statement about a person, especially one they need to hear
- Usage notes: Already documented in P1b Root 2, item 19; cross-referenced here. Often appears in the plural.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 12**
- Base Form: *truth*
- Example: *gospel truth*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: something regarded as absolutely and unquestionably true
- Usage notes: Already documented in P1b Root 2, item 20; cross-referenced here.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 13**
- Base Form: *truth*
- Example: *half-truth*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: a statement that is only partially true, especially one used to mislead
- Usage notes: Already documented in P1b Root 2, item 18; cross-referenced here. Functions as a compound noun.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 14**
- Base Form: *truth*
- Example: *economical with the truth*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: deliberately withholding information or being misleading without technically lying
- Usage notes: A fixed adjectival phrase used in British English, particularly in political and journalistic discourse, as a euphemistic or ironic description of deliberate evasion. Associated with a 1986 British court case. "The minister was accused of being economical with the truth."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 15**
- Base Form: *truth*
- Example: *speak truth to power*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: to tell those in authority what is true, especially when it is unwelcome or dangerous to do so
- Usage notes: A fixed phrase used in political and activist discourse. Associated with Quaker usage and popularized in the twentieth century. "Journalists have a duty to speak truth to power."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

## 3. Base Form: *accurate*

---

**Construction 1**
- Base Form: *accurate*
- Example: *strictly accurate*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: precisely correct in every detail; true in the most exact sense
- Usage notes: Used in formal and legal discourse to qualify a statement as correct in the most precise sense, often implying that a less strict formulation might be misleading. "It would not be strictly accurate to say that no decision had been made."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 2**
- Base Form: *accurate*
- Example: *broadly accurate*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: correct in its general outlines, though possibly imprecise in details
- Usage notes: Used in journalistic and evaluative contexts to concede that a description is approximately correct without endorsing every detail. "The account was broadly accurate but omitted several important facts."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 3**
- Base Form: *accurate*
- Example: *factually accurate*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: correct with respect to the facts
- Usage notes: Used in journalistic, legal, and academic contexts to specify that a statement corresponds to verifiable facts, as distinct from being accurate in other respects (e.g., tone, interpretation). "The report was factually accurate but misleading in its framing."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 4**
- Base Form: *accurate*
- Example: *historically accurate*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: correct with respect to historical facts
- Usage notes: Used in academic, journalistic, and popular discourse to evaluate representations of past events. "The film was praised for being historically accurate."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Note on absence:** No common idiomatic or periphrastic constructions beyond adverbial modification patterns are identified for *accurate*. The word participates in modification constructions (*strictly*, *broadly*, *factually*, *historically* + *accurate*) but not in fixed idiomatic expressions of the type found with *true* or *truth*.

## 4. Base Form: *genuine*

---

**Construction 1**
- Base Form: *genuine*
- Example: *the genuine article*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: the real or authentic thing, as opposed to an imitation or substitute
- Usage notes: A fixed noun phrase used in everyday discourse to assert that something is the authentic version of what it purports to be. "This is the genuine article, not a reproduction."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 2**
- Base Form: *genuine*
- Example: *genuine concern*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: real and sincere concern, as opposed to performed or feigned concern
- Usage notes: A common collocation used in political, journalistic, and everyday discourse to distinguish authentic emotional or moral engagement from its simulation. "There was genuine concern among residents about the proposal."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 3**
- Base Form: *genuine*
- Example: *genuine surprise*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: real and unfeigned surprise
- Usage notes: A common collocation used to assert that an emotional reaction is authentic. "Her genuine surprise at the announcement was evident."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Note on absence:** *Genuine* does not participate in a large set of fixed idiomatic constructions beyond the *genuine article* pattern and adjectival collocations with emotion and state nouns. The word functions primarily as a modifier rather than as the head of fixed expressions.

## 5. Base Form: *authentic*

---

**Construction 1**
- Base Form: *authentic*
- Example: *authentic self*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: one's true or genuine identity, as opposed to a performed or socially constructed persona
- Usage notes: Used in philosophical (especially existentialist), psychological, and popular self-help discourse. "She was encouraged to express her authentic self."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 2**
- Base Form: *authentic*
- Example: *authentic experience*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: an experience regarded as genuine, meaningful, and not artificially constructed or commodified
- Usage notes: Used in tourism, cultural criticism, and philosophical discourse. "Travelers increasingly seek authentic experiences rather than packaged tours."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 3**
- Base Form: *authentic*
- Example: *authentic voice*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: a manner of expression regarded as genuinely representative of a person's or community's true perspective
- Usage notes: Used in literary criticism, journalism, and cultural discourse. "Critics praised the novel for its authentic voice."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Note on absence:** *Authentic* participates in a range of noun-phrase collocations (with *self*, *experience*, *voice*, *cuisine*, *performance*, etc.) but does not appear to generate fixed idiomatic expressions of the type found with *true* or *truth*. The collocational pattern is productive but compositional rather than idiomatic.

## 6. Base Form: *valid*

---

**Construction 1**
- Base Form: *valid*
- Example: *valid point*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: an argument or observation that is well-founded and deserves consideration
- Usage notes: A common collocation used in discussion and debate to acknowledge that an interlocutor has made a reasonable observation. "That's a valid point, but it doesn't address the main issue."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 2**
- Base Form: *valid*
- Example: *valid argument*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: in logic, an argument whose conclusion follows necessarily from its premises; in everyday use, a well-founded argument
- Usage notes: Used in both technical logical discourse and everyday evaluative contexts. The technical and everyday senses diverge: in logic, validity is a formal property independent of the truth of the premises.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 3**
- Base Form: *valid*
- Example: *valid concern*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: a concern that is legitimate and deserves to be taken seriously
- Usage notes: Used in political, journalistic, and everyday discourse to acknowledge that a worry or objection has genuine grounds. "The committee acknowledged that residents had raised valid concerns."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Construction 4**
- Base Form: *valid*
- Example: *still valid*
- Transliteration: N/A
- English translation: continuing to be in force, applicable, or correct
- Usage notes: Used in legal, administrative, and everyday contexts to assert that a document, claim, or principle has not expired or been superseded. "The passport is still valid for another two years."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Note on absence:** *Valid* does not participate in fixed idiomatic expressions beyond its standard adjectival collocations. Its multi-word constructions are compositional.

**Construction 4**

- Example: *that's correct*
- Transliteration: N/A

- Transliteration: N/A

## Summary of Absences

The following P1 base forms do not participate in common fixed multi-word constructions beyond compositional noun-phrase modification patterns. Their multi-word uses are productive but not idiomatic:

- *accurate* — participates in adverbial modification patterns (*strictly accurate*, *broadly accurate*, *factually accurate*) but no fixed idioms
- *genuine* — participates in adjectival collocations but only *the genuine article* qualifies as a fixed expression
- *authentic* — participates in productive collocations (*authentic self*, *authentic experience*) but these are compositional
- *valid* — participates in adjectival collocations but no fixed idioms
- *spurious* — no fixed multi-word constructions; compositional modification only
- *veridical* — technical term; no fixed multi-word constructions
- *apocryphal* — participates in compositional noun phrases only
- *specious* — no fixed multi-word constructions; compositional modification only
- *fictitious* — participates in legal compound nouns but no fixed idioms
- *ostensible* — participates in compositional noun phrases only

The items with the richest multi-word construction inventories are *true*, *truth*, *false*, and *real*, all of which participate in fixed idiomatic expressions, discourse markers, and compound nouns across multiple registers.

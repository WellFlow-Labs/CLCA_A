# CLCA Protocol v3.4 — P2b Compositional Constructions
**Language: English (en)**

---

## Preliminary Note on Scope and Method

This step documents attested multi-word constructions involving the 30 P1 lexical items. The scope is restricted to constructions that are not derivational (handled in P2a) and not simple noun phrases or modifier-noun collocations unless the combination has developed a meaning not fully predictable from its parts or constitutes a recognized fixed expression.

Four construction types are distinguished throughout:

- **Periphrastic constructions:** multi-word expressions that perform a grammatical or semantic function (e.g., expressing negation, modality, or aspect) that a single word might otherwise perform
- **Idiomatic constructions:** fixed or semi-fixed expressions whose meaning is not fully compositional
- **Syntactically-driven constructions:** patterns where the P1 item appears in a grammatically distinctive frame (e.g., cleft constructions, predicative uses, complement structures)
- **Fixed collocations:** strongly conventionalized multi-word sequences that function as units

The boundary between a fixed collocation and a compound is not always sharp; where a form has been documented in P1b as a compound (e.g., *half-truth*, *post-truth*, *truth table*), it is not repeated here. The focus is on syntactically separable or phrasal constructions.

Absence is recorded explicitly. Register variation is noted where relevant.

## P1 Item 1: **true**

---

**Construction 1.1**
- **Base Form:** true
- **Example:** *It is true that she was there.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** It is a fact that she was there.
- **Usage notes:** Extraposition construction with *it* as dummy subject and a *that*-clause as the logical subject. This is the standard syntactic frame for predicating truth of a proposition in formal and everyday English. The construction is highly productive and appears across all registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 1.2**
- **Base Form:** true
- **Example:** *That is true.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** That statement corresponds to the facts.
- **Usage notes:** Simple predicative construction with *be* as copula. The most basic syntactic frame for asserting truth; functions as a response to a prior assertion. Ubiquitous across all registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 1.3**
- **Base Form:** true
- **Example:** *It rings true.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** It seems genuine and believable.
- **Usage notes:** Idiomatic verbal construction. Documented in P1b (item 1.28) as a phrasal verb; included here because the construction involves a syntactically distinctive predicative complement use of *true* with a non-copular verb. The idiom derives from testing coins by their sound. Common in everyday and journalistic registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 1.4**
- **Base Form:** true
- **Example:** *Her dream came true.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** Her dream was realized; what she hoped for actually happened.
- **Usage notes:** Idiomatic construction with *come* as a catenative verb and *true* as a predicative complement. Documented in P1b (item 1.29). The construction applies specifically to wishes, predictions, and hopes becoming actual. Common in everyday speech.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 1.5**
- **Base Form:** true
- **Example:** *This holds true in most cases.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** This remains valid or applicable in most cases.
- **Usage notes:** Semi-idiomatic construction with *hold* as a catenative verb and *true* as a predicative complement. Documented in P1b (item 1.30). Used in intellectual, scientific, and everyday discourse to assert that a principle or statement remains accurate under specified conditions.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 1.6**
- **Base Form:** true
- **Example:** *True to his word, he arrived on time.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** As he had promised, he arrived on time; he kept his word.
- **Usage notes:** Fixed prepositional phrase *true to* + noun phrase, functioning as a sentence-initial adjunct. Expresses correspondence between a person's stated intention and their subsequent action. Common in narrative and journalistic registers. The pattern *true to* + noun is productive: *true to form*, *true to type*, *true to life*, *true to oneself*.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 1.7**
- **Base Form:** true
- **Example:** *True to form, she arrived late.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** Characteristically, as one would expect from her established pattern, she arrived late.
- **Usage notes:** Fixed phrase *true to form* functioning as a sentence adverbial. Documented in P1b (item 1.22). Expresses that someone is behaving in a manner consistent with their established pattern. Common in journalistic and everyday registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 1.8**
- **Base Form:** true
- **Example:** *She stayed true to herself.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** She remained faithful to her own values and identity.
- **Usage notes:** Construction *stay/remain true to oneself* expressing fidelity to personal identity or values. Common in self-help, motivational, and everyday discourse. The reflexive *oneself* is the canonical form; specific pronouns (*true to himself*, *true to herself*) are also frequent.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 1.9**
- **Base Form:** true
- **Example:** *Too good to be true.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** So favorable as to be implausible or likely to be deceptive.
- **Usage notes:** Fixed idiomatic phrase, typically used predicatively ("it seemed too good to be true") or as a nominal modifier ("a too-good-to-be-true offer"). Expresses skepticism about something that appears excessively favorable. Common across all registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 1.10**
- **Base Form:** true
- **Example:** *The same is true of X.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** The same applies to X; X is also characterized by what was just described.
- **Usage notes:** Fixed discourse construction used to extend a predication to a new subject. Common in academic, journalistic, and everyday registers as a cohesive device. The pattern *the same is true of* / *the same holds true for* is highly frequent in expository prose.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 1.11**
- **Base Form:** true
- **Example:** *It is true to say that…*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** It is accurate to assert that…
- **Usage notes:** Hedging construction combining *it is true* with an infinitival complement. Used to introduce a qualified or partial concession before a contrasting point. Common in academic and journalistic registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 1.12**
- **Base Form:** true
- **Example:** *True enough, but…*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** That is accurate as far as it goes, but…
- **Usage notes:** Concessive discourse marker. Used to acknowledge the partial validity of a preceding claim before introducing a qualification or counterargument. Common in spoken and written argumentative discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 1.13**
- **Base Form:** true
- **Example:** *Is it true that…?*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** Does the following correspond to the facts?
- **Usage notes:** Standard interrogative frame for requesting confirmation of a proposition. Ubiquitous across all registers. The construction *is it true that* + *that*-clause is the canonical form for propositional truth questions.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

## P1 Item 2: **truth**

---

**Construction 2.1**
- **Base Form:** truth
- **Example:** *To tell the truth, I don't know.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** To be honest, I don't know.
- **Usage notes:** Fixed infinitival phrase functioning as a sentence adverbial or discourse marker. Used to introduce a candid admission, often one that the speaker might have been reluctant to make. Common across all registers. Variant: *to tell you the truth*.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 2.2**
- **Base Form:** truth
- **Example:** *The truth is that we failed.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** The actual fact of the matter is that we failed.
- **Usage notes:** Predicative construction with *the truth* as subject and a *that*-clause as complement. Used to introduce a frank or unwelcome statement of fact. Common across all registers. Variant: *the truth of the matter is that*.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 2.3**
- **Base Form:** truth
- **Example:** *The truth of the matter is that no one knows.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** The actual situation is that no one knows.
- **Usage notes:** Extended variant of Construction 2.2. The phrase *the truth of the matter* functions as a discourse-initial frame emphasizing candor. Common in spoken and journalistic registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 2.4**
- **Base Form:** truth
- **Example:** *There is some truth in what she says.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** What she says is partially accurate.
- **Usage notes:** Existential construction with *there is* and a partitive noun phrase. Used to acknowledge partial validity of a claim without full endorsement. Common across all registers. Variants: *there is truth in*, *there is a grain of truth in*.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 2.5**
- **Base Form:** truth
- **Example:** *There is a grain of truth in it.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** It contains a small element of accuracy.
- **Usage notes:** Fixed idiomatic construction with *a grain of truth* as a partitive noun phrase. Expresses that a claim, though largely inaccurate or exaggerated, contains a small element of accuracy. Common in everyday and journalistic registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 2.6**
- **Base Form:** truth
- **Example:** *In truth, the situation was more complex.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** Actually; in reality; to be honest.
- **Usage notes:** Fixed prepositional phrase *in truth* functioning as a sentence adverbial. Used to introduce a correction or qualification of a preceding statement. More formal than *actually* or *in fact*; common in literary and academic registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 2.7**
- **Base Form:** truth
- **Example:** *The truth will out.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** The truth will eventually become known.
- **Usage notes:** Fixed proverbial construction. The verb *out* is used intransitively in an archaic sense meaning "to come out" or "to emerge." Common in everyday speech as a proverbial expression of confidence that deception cannot be maintained indefinitely.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 2.8**
- **Base Form:** truth
- **Example:** *Speak truth to power.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** Confront those in authority with honest, unwelcome facts.
- **Usage notes:** Fixed imperative construction, originally associated with Quaker activism and popularized in political discourse. Used to describe the act of challenging authority with honest speech. Common in political and journalistic registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 2.9**
- **Base Form:** truth
- **Example:** *The whole truth and nothing but the truth.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** Complete and unqualified truthfulness.
- **Usage notes:** Fixed legal formula, part of the oath administered to witnesses in common law courts ("the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth"). Used outside legal contexts as an emphatic assertion of complete honesty. Common across registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 2.10**
- **Base Form:** truth
- **Example:** *Economical with the truth.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** Deliberately omitting relevant facts; misleading without technically lying.
- **Usage notes:** Fixed idiomatic phrase, widely attributed to Edmund Burke and popularized in British political discourse. Used to describe deliberate misleading through selective disclosure rather than outright falsehood. Common in journalistic and political registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 2.11**
- **Base Form:** truth
- **Example:** *Stretch the truth.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** To exaggerate or misrepresent facts without outright lying.
- **Usage notes:** Idiomatic verbal construction. Used to describe mild or moderate misrepresentation. Common in everyday and journalistic registers. Less severe than *lie* but more than *exaggerate*.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 2.12**
- **Base Form:** truth
- **Example:** *Bend the truth.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** To distort or misrepresent facts; to be less than fully honest.
- **Usage notes:** Idiomatic verbal construction, parallel to *stretch the truth*. Used to describe deliberate but partial misrepresentation. Common in everyday and journalistic registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 2.13**
- **Base Form:** truth
- **Example:** *The truth hurts.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** Accurate information is often unwelcome or painful to hear.
- **Usage notes:** Fixed proverbial construction. Used to acknowledge that honest information may be uncomfortable. Common in everyday speech.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 2.14**
- **Base Form:** truth
- **Example:** *Truth be told, I was relieved.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** To be honest; if I am being candid.
- **Usage notes:** Fixed subjunctive construction functioning as a sentence adverbial. Used to introduce a candid admission. The subjunctive *be* marks this as a fixed expression; it is not a productive subjunctive pattern in contemporary English. Common in spoken and informal written registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 2.15**
- **Base Form:** truth
- **Example:** *A truth universally acknowledged.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** A fact accepted by everyone.
- **Usage notes:** Fixed literary allusion to the opening of Jane Austen's *Pride and Prejudice*. Used in contemporary discourse, often ironically, to introduce a claim as if it were self-evident. Common in literary and journalistic registers; the ironic use is more frequent than the literal.
- **Epistemic status:** [MARGINAL]

---

**Construction 2.16**
- **Base Form:** truth
- **Example:** *Truth is stranger than fiction.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** Real events are often more surprising or improbable than invented ones.
- **Usage notes:** Fixed proverbial construction, attributed to Byron. Used to comment on surprising real events. Common in everyday and journalistic registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

## P1 Item 3: **accurate**

---

**Construction 3.1**
- **Base Form:** accurate
- **Example:** *To be accurate, the figure is 47, not 45.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** To state the correct figure, it is 47, not 45.
- **Usage notes:** Infinitival phrase *to be accurate* functioning as a sentence adverbial, used to introduce a correction or precise statement. Common in academic, journalistic, and professional registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 3.2**
- **Base Form:** accurate
- **Example:** *Strictly accurate.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** Precisely correct in every detail.
- **Usage notes:** Fixed adverb-adjective collocation. *Strictly accurate* is used to assert complete precision, often in contexts where a less precise statement has been made or might be expected. Common in academic and professional registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 3.3**
- **Base Form:** accurate
- **Example:** *Broadly accurate.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** Correct in its main outlines, though not in every detail.
- **Usage notes:** Fixed adverb-adjective collocation. *Broadly accurate* is used to acknowledge partial correctness while allowing for minor errors or simplifications. Common in academic, journalistic, and professional registers. Contrasts with *strictly accurate*.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 3.4**
- **Base Form:** accurate
- **Example:** *Reasonably accurate.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** Correct to a satisfactory degree; not perfectly precise but acceptably close.
- **Usage notes:** Hedged adverb-adjective collocation. Used to qualify a claim of accuracy without asserting complete precision. Common across all registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 3.5**
- **Base Form:** accurate
- **Example:** *Accurate to within five percent.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** Correct within a margin of five percent.
- **Usage notes:** Construction *accurate to within* + measure phrase, used in scientific, technical, and statistical contexts to specify a margin of error. The pattern *accurate to* + measure is productive in technical registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

## P1 Item 6: **valid**

---

**Construction 6.1**
- **Base Form:** valid
- **Example:** *That's a valid point.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** That is a legitimate and well-founded observation.
- **Usage notes:** Predicative construction used in conversational and academic discourse to acknowledge the legitimacy of an interlocutor's argument or observation. The phrase *a valid point* is a highly frequent collocation. Common across all registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 6.2**
- **Base Form:** valid
- **Example:** *The ticket is valid for thirty days.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** The ticket may be used for a period of thirty days.
- **Usage notes:** Predicative construction with *valid for* + time period, used in legal and administrative contexts to specify the duration of validity. The pattern *valid for* + duration/purpose is productive in administrative and legal registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 6.3**
- **Base Form:** valid
- **Example:** *The argument is valid but unsound.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** The argument's conclusion follows necessarily from its premises, but at least one premise is false.
- **Usage notes:** Technical construction in formal logic distinguishing *validity* (the logical form is correct) from *soundness* (the argument is valid and all premises are true). Common in philosophy and logic pedagogy.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 6.4**
- **Base Form:** valid
- **Example:** *Strictly speaking, the argument is not valid.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** When assessed by the precise logical standard, the argument does not meet the criterion of validity.
- **Usage notes:** Construction with *strictly speaking* as a sentence adverbial qualifying the predication of validity. Common in academic and philosophical registers when distinguishing technical from colloquial uses of *valid*.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

## P1 Item 7: **correct**

---

**Construction 7.1**
- **Base Form:** correct
- **Example:** *That is correct.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** That is right; that corresponds to the facts or the required standard.
- **Usage notes:** Simple predicative construction. Functions as a confirmation response in conversational and formal contexts. Common across all registers; slightly more formal than *that's right*.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 7.2**
- **Base Form:** correct
- **Example:** *If I am correct, the meeting is on Thursday.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** If my information is right, the meeting is on Thursday.
- **Usage notes:** Conditional construction with *if I am correct* as a hedging clause. Used to introduce a claim while acknowledging the possibility of error. Common in spoken and written registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 7.3**
- **Base Form:** correct
- **Example:** *Correct me if I'm wrong, but…*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** Please tell me if I have made an error, but…
- **Usage notes:** Fixed imperative construction functioning as a hedging device. Used to introduce a claim while inviting correction. Common in spoken and informal written registers. The construction is often used rhetorically, with the speaker confident in their claim.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 7.4**
- **Base Form:** correct
- **Example:** *Politically correct.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** Conforming to a set of progressive social norms regarding language and behavior; (often used critically) excessively concerned with avoiding offense.
- **Usage notes:** Fixed adverb-adjective collocation that has developed a distinct and contested meaning in contemporary political discourse. The phrase *politically correct* (and its abbreviation *PC*) is used both descriptively and critically. Common across all registers; the evaluative valence varies by speaker.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 7.5**
- **Base Form:** correct
- **Example:** *Stand corrected.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** To acknowledge that one was wrong and accept a correction.
- **Usage notes:** Fixed construction, typically in the first person: *I stand corrected*. Used to formally acknowledge an error after being corrected. Common in formal, academic, and journalistic registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

## P1 Item 9: **false**

---

**Construction 9.1**
- **Base Form:** false
- **Example:** *That is false.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** That statement does not correspond to the facts.
- **Usage notes:** Simple predicative construction. The direct negation of *that is true*. Common across all registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 9.2**
- **Base Form:** false
- **Example:** *Under false pretenses.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** By means of deception; by misrepresenting one's identity, intentions, or circumstances.
- **Usage notes:** Fixed prepositional phrase, typically used in the construction *obtain something under false pretenses*. Legal and everyday registers. The phrase is a standard legal description of a form of fraud.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 9.3**
- **Base Form:** false
- **Example:** *A false sense of security.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** An unwarranted feeling of safety; confidence that is not justified by the actual situation.
- **Usage notes:** Fixed noun phrase. Used to describe a state of unwarranted confidence that leaves a person vulnerable. Common in journalistic, political, and everyday registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 9.4**
- **Base Form:** false
- **Example:** *A false alarm.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** A warning that turns out to be unfounded; a situation that appeared dangerous but was not.
- **Usage notes:** Fixed noun phrase. Used in everyday, journalistic, and technical registers to describe a warning signal that does not correspond to an actual emergency. Common across all registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 9.5**
- **Base Form:** false
- **Example:** *A false start.*
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **English translation:** An unsuccessful attempt to begin something; a beginning that has to be abandoned and restarted.
- **Usage notes:** Fixed noun phrase, originally from athletics (a start before the signal). Used figuratively in everyday and journalistic registers to describe any failed initial attempt.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**Construction 9.6**

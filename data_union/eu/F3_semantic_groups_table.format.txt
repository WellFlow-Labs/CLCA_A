| Paired truth-assertion and falsity-assertion formulas / Expressions used to assert or deny that a statement is true | These expressions function as the primary predicative formulas for asserting that something is true or false in Basque. They appear in direct parallel syntactic environments (*egia da* / *gezurra da*), are explicitly contrasted with each other, and together constitute the baseline binary for propositional evaluation in everyday, legal, and philosophical discourse. They are used in argumentative discourse, testimony, and everyday factual exchange. The pair is so tightly linked that derivational families, collocations, and discourse functions mirror each other systematically. | *egia*; *egia da*; *egia esan*; *egia hutsa*; *egia osoa eta ezer ez besterik*; *egia esateko*; *egia da, baina...*; *egia da ala ez?*; *egia da ala gezurra?*; *gezurra*; *gezurra da*; *gezurra esan*; *gezurra zabaldu*; *gezurra eta egia nahastu*; *gezurretan ari da*; *gezurra txuri*; *gezurra sinestu*; *oker egon*; *zuzen egon*; *oker nago*; *ziur nago*; *ziur ez egon*; *oker ez banago* | *ziur nago* and *ziur ez egon* also appear in Group 2 (epistemic certainty expressions); *zuzen* and *oker* also appear in Groups 5 and 9 in their other uses | The parallelism is morphological, derivational, and discursive. *Ziur nago* and *ziur ez egon* shade into Group 2 and are listed in both. *Zuzen* and *oker* appear here in their propositional-correctness uses but have additional uses in other contexts. |
| Adjectival characterization of truth or falsity; epistemic certainty and uncertainty expressions | This group encompasses two related clusters. The first consists of adjectival forms derived from the truth/falsity roots that are used attributively or predicatively to characterize statements, objects, or persons as truthful, genuine, false, or fake. They are distinguished from the nominal forms in Group 1 by their explicitly adjectival function and their range of application. The second cluster consists of expressions used to calibrate how confident a speaker is in a proposition, ranging from full certainty to acknowledged uncertainty. A notable feature is that *ziurrenik*, derived from the root meaning "certain," functions in practice as a probability hedge ("probably," "most likely") rather than as an assertion of full certainty. | *egiazkoa*; *egiazko*; *egiazkotasuna*; *egiazki*; *egiazko informazioa zabaldu*; *egiazko adiskidea*; *egiazko arrazoia*; *gezurrezkoa*; *gezurrezko*; *gezurrezko lekukotasuna*; *gezurrezko informazioa zabaldu*; *gezurrezko agindua*; *gezurtasuna*; *gezurrezko agiriak erabili*; *gezurrezko lekukotasuna eman*; *gezurrezko itxura eman*; *ziur egon*; *ziur nago*; *ziur asko*; *ziurrenik*; *ziur-ziur*; *ziur samar egon*; *ziur al zaude?*; *ziur ez dakit*; *ziurtasun osoz*; *ziurtasun eza*; *ziurtasun falta*; *antza du*; *antza denez* | *egiazkoa* also appears in Groups 3, 7, and 8; *antza du* and *antza denez* also appear in Group 9; *ziur nago* also appears in Group 1 | The distinction between Group 1 and this group is functional: Group 1 items are used as nominals in predicative formulas; this group's adjectival items are used as adjectives modifying nouns or as predicative adjectives. *Antza du* and *antza denez* appear in both this group and Group 9. *Egiazkoa* appears in Groups 3, 7, and 8 with somewhat different emphases depending on the noun it modifies. |

| Verification and proof expressions; expressions used in contexts of formal or procedural verification | These expressions are used in contexts where the establishment of truth or fact through evidence, testing, or official procedure is at issue. | Expressions used to characterize something as false, fake, or spurious | These expressions are used to evaluate objects, documents, statements, or representations as not being what they claim to be — as counterfeit, fabricated, or spuriously presented. | Expressions used to evaluate the credibility of sources and testimony; expressions used to evaluate whether something can be trusted or believed | These expressions appear in journalistic, legal, and analytical contexts where the question is whether a source, witness, or account deserves to be believed or relied upon. | Appearance-versus-reality expressions | These expressions are used in contexts where the outward appearance of something is distinguished from what is actually the case. They appear in moral, analytical, literary, and everyday contexts where the speaker is signaling that surface impressions may be misleading or that something presents itself as other than it is. They are distinguished from Group 8 (hedging) by their focus on the contrast itself rather than on the speaker's epistemic position.

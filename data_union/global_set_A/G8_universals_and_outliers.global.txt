# G8 — Candidate Universals, Outliers, and Open Questions

---

## 1. Candidate Universals / Strong Tendencies

---

**CU-1. Predicative attribution is broadly available for truth-domain properties across all attested languages.**

**Statement.** In all eight languages, a subject can receive a truth-domain predicate (true, false, genuine, accurate, valid, honest, credible) through a copular or stative construction. No documented restriction on predicate type or semantic dimension was found.

**Evidence summary.** CF1 (Predicative Attribution) is rated ALLOWED across all twelve semantic dimensions in all eight languages (ka, yo, fi, eu, ta, id, tr, ko). The specific copular form varies — enclitic *-a* in Georgian, zero copula in Tamil/Indonesian/Turkish/Korean, overt copula in Finnish/Basque/Yoruba — but the constructional function is universally available. No language restricts this function to a subset of truth-domain dimensions.

**Caveats / exceptions.** Korean *-seong* quality nominals require the existential *있다/없다* frame rather than standard copular predication (*-hada* is blocked for this class). This is a morphological restriction on a specific suffix class, not a restriction on the predicative attribution function itself. The function remains available; only the surface realization differs.

**Confidence rating.** High.

---

**CU-2. Adnominal modification is broadly available for truth-domain evaluative terms across all attested languages.**

**Statement.** In all eight languages, truth-domain evaluative terms can modify nouns in attributive position (accurate information, forged document, reliable source, honest person). No language restricts this function to a subset of truth-domain dimensions.

**Evidence summary.** CF3 (Adnominal Modification) is rated ALLOWED across all twelve semantic dimensions in all eight languages. The modifier position varies — prenominal in seven languages, postnominal in Yoruba — but the constructional function is universally available. Indonesian restricts direct juxtaposition (without the *yang* relativizer) to fixed collocations, but the *yang* relativizer construction is fully productive and unrestricted.

**Caveats / exceptions.** The Yoruba postnominal pattern is a systematic structural difference in modifier direction, not a restriction on the function. The Indonesian *yang* requirement is a surface morphosyntactic constraint, not a semantic restriction.

**Confidence rating.** High.

---

**CU-3. Abstract nominalization of truth-domain properties is broadly productive across all attested languages.**

**Statement.** In all eight languages, truth-domain properties (truthfulness, accuracy, credibility, validity, honesty, authenticity) can be named as abstract entities through a productive morphological process. The resulting nominals serve as subjects, objects, and genitive modifiers.

**Evidence summary.** CF6 (Abstract Nominalization) is rated ALLOWED across all twelve semantic dimensions in all eight languages. Specific suffixes vary: *-eba/-oba* (Georgian), *ì-* prefix (Yoruba), *-uus/-yys* (Finnish), *-tasun* (Basque), *-mai* (Tamil), *ke-...-an* circumfix (Indonesian), *-luk/-lik* (Turkish), *-seong* (Korean). The functional outcome is consistent: a nominal naming the property as an abstract entity that participates in evaluative constructions.

**Caveats / exceptions.** The degree of elaboration varies: Korean *-seong* nominals are more systematically documented than Tamil *-mai* compounds for some dimensions. Some abstract nominals are restricted to formal registers. These are register restrictions, not restrictions on the availability of the nominalization function.

**Confidence rating.** High.

---

**CU-4. Negative attribution is broadly available for truth-domain properties across all attested languages.**

**Statement.** In all eight languages, truth-domain predicates can be negated. The negation may be syntactic (negation particle + copula) or morphological (privative prefix/circumfix), but the function is universally available.

**Evidence summary.** CF2 (Negative Attribution) is rated ALLOWED across all twelve semantic dimensions in all eight languages. Syntactic negation is universal; morphological privative negation is additionally documented in at least six languages (Georgian *u-...-o*, Finnish *epä-*, Basque *ez-/-gabe*, Tamil *-aṟṟa*, Indonesian *ke-tidak-...-an*, Turkish *-sız*, Korean *부-*).

**Caveats / exceptions.** The specific negation strategy varies and is not always interchangeable within a language. Indonesian distinguishes *tidak* (adjectival predicates) from *bukan* (nominal predicates); Korean distinguishes *아니다* (nominal) from *-지 않다* (adjectival). Turkish *değil* is the sole strategy with no morphological alternant for adjectival predicates. These are language-internal restrictions on negation strategy, not restrictions on the availability of the negation function.

**Confidence rating.** High.

---

**CU-5. The propositional truth / personal honesty distinction is recurrently lexicalized across unrelated language families.**

**Statement.** In all eight languages, the vocabulary for evaluating whether a specific statement is true (propositional correspondence) is distinct from the vocabulary for evaluating whether a person's habitual conduct is honest (personal integrity). The two functions are never covered by identical terms, though they may share a root.

**Evidence summary.** Georgian uses *simarṭle/č̣ešmariteba* for propositional truth and *gulc̣rpeli* for sincerity. Yoruba uses *òtítọ́* for propositional truth and *ìwà rere* for moral character. Finnish uses *tosi/totuus* for propositional truth and *rehellinen* for honesty. Basque uses *egia* for propositional truth and *benetakoa* for genuine sincerity. Tamil uses *uṇmai* for propositional truth and *nērmai* for honesty/integrity. Indonesian uses *benar* for correctness and *jujur* for honesty. Turkish uses *doğru* for correctness and *dürüst* for uprightness. Korean uses *사실* for factual accuracy and *정직* for honesty.

**Caveats / exceptions.** In Yoruba, the *tọ́* root covers both propositional correctness and procedural/moral correctness without differentiation, creating a partial merger of D1 and D11. However, the D1/D2 distinction (propositional truth vs. personal honesty) is maintained even in Yoruba: *òtítọ́* (propositional truth) is distinct from *ìwà rere* (good character). The merger is between D1 and D11, not between D1 and D2.

**Confidence rating.** High.

---

**CU-6. The source-trustworthiness / content-credibility distinction is recurrently lexicalized across unrelated language families.**

**Statement.** In all eight languages, the vocabulary for evaluating whether a source can be depended upon (source trustworthiness) is distinct from the vocabulary for evaluating whether a specific claim merits belief (content credibility). The distinction is explicitly documented in at least four languages and is consistent with the vocabulary organization in all eight.

**Evidence summary.** Georgian distinguishes *sarc̣muno* (inherent trustworthiness of source) from *damajerebeliˈ* (persuasive effect on hearer). Yoruba distinguishes *fidagarri* (trustworthy source) from *sinesgarri* (believable content). Finnish distinguishes *luotettava* (reliable/dependable over time) from *uskottava* (believable for a specific claim). Basque distinguishes *fidagarria* (trustworthy source) from *sinesgarria* (believable content). Tamil distinguishes *nampakamāna* (reliable) from *nampattakka* (worth trusting for a specific claim). Indonesian distinguishes *terpercaya* (trusted/reliable) from *kredibel* (credible). Turkish distinguishes *güvenilir* (reliable) from *inanılır* (believable). Korean distinguishes *신뢰성* (reliability of a source) from *신빙성* (credibility of a specific claim).

**Caveats / exceptions.** The degree of lexical differentiation varies. In some languages the distinction is explicitly noted in the F3 boundary notes (Georgian, Yoruba, Finnish, Basque); in others it is recoverable from the vocabulary organization but less explicitly documented. The distinction may be gradient rather than categorical in some languages.

**Confidence rating.** High.

---

**CU-7. The neutral-copy / fraudulent-fake distinction is recurrently lexicalized in a majority of attested languages.**

**Statement.** In at least six of the eight languages, the vocabulary for genuineness-versus-counterfeit distinguishes a neutral intermediate (imitation or copy without fraudulent intent) from a fraudulent fake (counterfeit with intent to deceive). The neutral intermediate is not simply a weaker version of the fraudulent fake; it has different evaluative charge and different legal implications.

**Evidence summary.** Finnish distinguishes *jäljitelmä* (imitation, without necessarily implying fraud) from *väärennös* (forgery, implying fraudulent intent). Basque distinguishes *kopiakoa* (copy) from *faltsua* (fake). Tamil distinguishes *nakal* (copy, neutral) from *pōli* (fake, negative). Indonesian distinguishes *tiruan* (imitation, neutral) from *palsu* (fake, fraudulent). Turkish distinguishes *taklit* (imitation) from *sahte* (counterfeit). Korean distinguishes *모조품* (imitation goods) from *위조품* (forged item). Georgian and Yoruba show less elaboration of this three-way contrast.

**Caveats / exceptions.** Georgian has a binary *namdvili/q̇albi* (genuine/fake) without a documented neutral intermediate. Yoruba lacks a dedicated forgery vocabulary. The three-way distinction is thus documented in 6/8 languages, not all eight. The two languages without it may express the distinction through context or through general deception vocabulary applied to objects, but this is not documented.

**Confidence rating.** Medium-high (6/8 languages with explicit documentation).

---

**CU-8. The legal/procedural validity vs. moral/substantive legitimacy distinction is recurrently maintained across all attested languages.**

**Statement.** In all eight languages, the vocabulary for evaluating whether an action or document meets formal legal conditions (procedural validity) is distinct from the vocabulary for evaluating whether it is rightful in a broader normative sense (legitimacy). The distinction is explicitly documented in at least six languages.

**Evidence summary.** Georgian distinguishes *kanonieri* (lawful, tied to positive law) from *legitimuri* (legitimate, with possible normative dimension). Finnish distinguishes *pätevä* (valid/competent) from *oikeudenmukainen* (just/fair). Basque distinguishes *baliozkoa* (valid) from *zilegi* (lawful/permissible) from *zuzen* (correct/just). Tamil distinguishes *caṭṭappaṭi* (according to law) from *niyāyam* (justice/rightness). Indonesian distinguishes *sah* (legally valid) from *adil* (fair/just). Turkish distinguishes *yasal* (legal, conforming to written law) from *meşru* (legitimate, extending beyond legality). Korean distinguishes *합법* (lawful) from *정당* (legitimate/justified). Yoruba has *ẹ̀tọ́* (right/entitlement) and *òdodo* (righteousness/justice) but the legality/legitimacy distinction is less explicitly drawn.

**Caveats / exceptions.** Yoruba shows the least elaboration of this dimension. The distinction between operative validity (in force) and invalidity (void) is documented in all eight languages, but the legality/legitimacy sub-distinction is less consistently documented in Yoruba.

**Confidence rating.** High (all 8 languages maintain some distinction; 7/8 explicitly documented).

---

**CU-9. The deliberate deception / innocent error distinction is recurrently lexicalized across all attested languages.**

**Statement.** In all eight languages, the vocabulary for falsehood distinguishes between statements that are false because of deliberate intent to deceive and those that are false because of honest mistake or misunderstanding. The two categories are covered by different lexical items or by different morphological forms.

**Evidence summary.** Georgian distinguishes *sicrue/ẗq̇uili* (deliberate lie) from *mcdari* (erroneous, without implying intent). Yoruba distinguishes *irọ́* (deliberate lie) from *àṣìṣe* (error/mistake). Finnish distinguishes *valhe* (lie, deliberate) from *virhe* (error, neutral). Basque distinguishes *gezurra* (lie, deliberate) from *okerra* (wrong/mistaken). Tamil distinguishes *poy* (lie, deliberate) from *tavaṟu* (mistake/error). Indonesian distinguishes *bohong* (deliberate lie) from *keliru* (innocent error/misunderstanding). Turkish distinguishes *yalan* (deliberate lie) from *yanlış* (wrong/incorrect, neutral to intent). Korean distinguishes *거짓말* (deliberate lie) from *실수* (mistake, innocent).

**Caveats / exceptions.** The degree of lexical differentiation varies. Indonesian shows the most explicit documentation of this distinction in the F3 grouping tables. In some languages (Yoruba, Tamil), the distinction is present but less elaborated as a distinct lexical category.

**Confidence rating.** High.

**CU-10. The intransitive truth-revelation construction is broadly attested across all attested languages.**

**Statement.** In all eight languages, truth can be the grammatical subject of an intransitive verb expressing emergence or disclosure (the truth came out, the truth came to light, the truth was revealed). This construction treats truth as an entity that moves from a hidden state to a visible state.

**Evidence summary.** Georgian *simartle gamovida* (the truth came out), Yoruba *ó farahàn pé òtítọ́ ni* (it became clear that it is the truth), Finnish *totuus tulee ilmi* (the truth comes to light), Basque *egia argitara atera* (truth brought to light), Tamil *uṇmai veḷippaṭṭatu* (the truth came to light), Indonesian *kebenaran akan terungkap* (the truth will come out), Turkish *gerçek ortaya çıktı* (the truth came out), Korean *진실이 드러나다* (the truth emerges). The construction is documented in all eight languages across unrelated language families.

**Caveats / exceptions.** The specific verb varies across languages. In Korean, three distinct verbs are documented with slightly different nuances (*드러나다*, *밝혀지다*, *판명되다*), while other languages typically use a single verb. The presupposition of a latent pre-existing state (truth was already true before disclosure) is consistent across languages but is more explicitly documented in some than others.

**Confidence rating.** High.

---

**CU-11. A proverbial or fixed construction asserting the ultimate triumph or indestructibility of truth is broadly attested across all attested languages.**

**Statement.** In all eight languages, at least one fixed or semi-fixed construction asserts the ultimate triumph, indestructibility, or inevitability of truth. The specific formulation varies but the proverbial register is present in all eight languages.

**Evidence summary.** Georgian *simartle gamarjvebs* (truth prevails), Yoruba *òtítọ́ kò ní kú* (truth will not die), Finnish *totuus tulee ilmi* (used in this proverbial sense), Basque *egia beti ateratzen da azalera* (the truth always comes to the surface), Tamil *uṇmai tāṉ vellum* (truth alone will prevail), Indonesian *kebenaran akan terungkap pada akhirnya* (the truth will come out in the end), Turkish *gerçek er ya da geç ortaya çıkar* (the truth comes out sooner or later), Korean *진실은 하나다* (there is only one truth) and related expressions.

**Caveats / exceptions.** The specific framing varies considerably: triumph (Georgian, Tamil, Korean), indestructibility (Yoruba), inevitability (Finnish, Basque, Indonesian, Turkish), singularity (Korean). The Turkish variant (*doğruluk her zaman ödüllendirilmez*, truthfulness is not always rewarded) is more pessimistic than the others, though the proverbial frame is still present. These constructions are pragmatic-cultural phenomena and may not reflect a structural constraint.

**Confidence rating.** High (as a cultural-pragmatic pattern); medium (as a structural constraint).

---

**CU-12. Lexicalized single-word agentive nominals for habitual truth-tellers are broadly absent or marginal across all attested languages.**

**Statement.** In all eight languages, no lexicalized single-word agentive nominal for a habitual truth-teller, honest person (as a role noun), or authenticator is documented. The concept is expressed periphrastically in all eight languages. This contrasts with the universal availability of lexicalized agentive nominals for habitual liars and forgers.

**Evidence summary.** Georgian has *mt'q̇uari* (liar) and *gamaq̇albebeli* (forger) but no single-word truth-teller agentive. Yoruba has *aṣírọ́* (liar) but no single-word truth-teller. Finnish has *valehtelija* (liar) and *väärentäjä* (forger) but no single-word truth-teller. Basque has *gezurti* (liar) but *egiazale* (truth-lover) is documented as marginal only. Tamil has *poyyaṉ* (liar) but *uṇmaiyāḷar* (persons of truth) is documented as marginal only. Indonesian has *pembohong* (liar) and *pemalsu* (forger) but no single-word truth-teller. Turkish has *yalancı* (liar) and *sahteci* (forger) but no single-word truth-teller. Korean has *거짓말쟁이* (liar) and *위조범* (forger) but no single-word truth-teller. The asymmetry is explicitly noted in the F4 construction tables for all eight languages.

**Caveats / exceptions.** Basque *egiazale* (truth-lover) and Tamil *uṇmaiyāḷar* (persons of truth) are documented but rated as marginal. These may represent incipient lexicalization rather than a complete absence. The asymmetry may reflect frequency effects (liars are more salient as social categories) rather than a structural prohibition. However, the consistency across unrelated language families is striking.

**Confidence rating.** High (as an empirical pattern); medium (as a structural constraint — the mechanism is not established).

**CU-13. Direct causation of dispositional character traits is broadly blocked or strongly resisted across all attested languages.**

**Statement.** In all eight languages, constructions expressing that an agent directly causes a person to become honest, sincere, or to possess genuine inner character are absent or strongly marked. The concept of making someone honest is expressed indirectly (through encouragement, instruction, or creating conditions) rather than through a direct causative construction.

**Evidence summary.** This pattern is documented across all eight languages in the F4 construction tables. The blocking is most explicitly noted for D2 (Personal Honesty and Integrity) and D7 (Authenticity of Inner States). No language documents a natural collocation for "make someone honest" as a direct causative. The block is consistent across South Caucasian, Niger-Congo, Finno-Ugric, language isolate, Dravidian, Austronesian, Turkic, and Koreanic language families.

**Caveats / exceptions.** Some indirect facilitation constructions are attested in a few languages (Basque *zintzotu eragin*, Korean *진정성을 높이다*). These appear to cause conditions for honesty rather than honesty itself. The distinction between direct causation of the dispositional state and indirect facilitation of conditions for that state is not always clearly drawn in the data. The blocking may be a strong preference rather than an absolute prohibition.

**Confidence rating.** High (as an empirical pattern across all 8 languages); medium-high (as a structural constraint — the mechanism is consistent with the data but the direct/indirect causation boundary is not always clearly drawn).

**CU-14. Positive causation of authenticity is broadly blocked; negative causation (forgery) is broadly available across all attested languages.**

**Statement.** In all eight languages, constructions expressing that an agent manufactures or creates genuine authenticity are absent or strongly marked. Constructions expressing that an agent destroys or falsifies authenticity (forgery, counterfeiting) are broadly available and lexicalized.

**Evidence summary.** Georgian has *gaq̇albeba* (forgery) but no productive construction for manufacturing genuine authenticity. Finnish has *väärentää* (to forge) but no productive construction for manufacturing genuine authenticity. Basque has *faltsifikatu* (to forge) but no productive construction for manufacturing genuine authenticity. Tamil has *pōli āvaṇam* (forged document) but no productive construction for manufacturing genuine authenticity. Indonesian has *memalsukan* (to falsify) but no productive construction for manufacturing genuine authenticity. Turkish has *sahte belge* (forged document) but no productive construction for manufacturing genuine authenticity. Korean has *위조하다* (to forge) but no productive construction for manufacturing genuine authenticity. The asymmetry is consistent across all eight languages.

**Caveats / exceptions.** Certification and authentication constructions (Georgian *dadastureba*, Finnish *todentaa*, Basque *egiaztatu*, Korean *인증하다*) are attested, but these confirm pre-existing authenticity rather than manufacture it. The distinction between confirming authenticity and manufacturing it is maintained across languages.

**Confidence rating.** High.

**CU-15. A spatial metaphor of straightness underlies truth and correctness vocabulary across unrelated language families.**

**Statement.** In all eight languages, the primary root or base for truth and correctness has a spatial meaning of "straight" or "direct" that underlies its epistemic and moral uses. The antonym in several languages is also spatial (crooked, bent).

**Evidence summary.** Georgian *marṭl-* (straight/right), Yoruba *tọ́* (straight/correct), Finnish *oikea* (right, also directional), Basque *zuzen* (straight/correct), Tamil *nēr* (straight/direct), Indonesian *benar* (correct), Turkish *doğru* (straight/correct/true), Korean *olbareuda* (upright/correct). The antonym is spatial in Turkish *eğri* (crooked/dishonest), Basque *oker* (crooked/wrong), and Georgian *eğri* (crooked).

**Caveats / exceptions.** The spatial etymology is more transparent in some languages than others. For Indonesian *benar*, the spatial connection is noted as possible but not certain. For Korean *올바르다*, the spatial component (*바르다*, to be straight/upright) is present but the compound is more opaque. The pattern may reflect independent parallel development rather than a single origin. This is a semantic pattern, not a structural constraint.

**Confidence rating.** Medium-high (consistent across all 8 languages but the spatial etymology is not equally transparent in all cases).

**CU-16. Positive emergence of truth is more natural than negative emergence across attested languages.**

**Statement.** In all eight languages, constructions expressing that truth comes to light or is revealed (positive emergence) are more natural and more consistently documented than constructions expressing that truth becomes false or is destroyed (negative emergence). The asymmetry is consistent across language families.

**Evidence summary.** The intransitive truth-revelation construction (truth as grammatical subject of an emergence verb) is documented in all eight languages for positive emergence. Constructions for negative emergence (truth becoming false, truth being destroyed) are not documented as natural collocations in any of the eight languages. The asymmetry is consistent with the positive emergence bias documented in the G7a matrix.

**Caveats / exceptions.** The absence of negative emergence constructions may reflect data collection focus rather than a genuine structural constraint. The data does not systematically test negative emergence constructions across all languages. The asymmetry is strongly suggested but not definitively established.

**Confidence rating.** Medium-high.

---

**CU-17. A discourse-framing construction for frank disclosure is broadly attested across all attested languages.**

**Statement.** In all eight languages, at least one construction functions as a sentence-level marker signaling that what follows is the speaker's honest assessment, a correction of a prior assumption, or a frank disclosure. The degree of grammaticalization varies from fully grammaticalized (Indonesian *sebenarnya*, Turkish *aslında*) to fully compositional (Yoruba hortative construction).

**Evidence summary.** Georgian *simartle rom vtq̇vat* (to tell the truth, frankly speaking), Yoruba *jẹ́ kí a sọ òtítọ́* (let us speak the truth), Finnish *totta puhuen* (to tell the truth), Basque *egia esan behar* (truth be told), Tamil *uṇmaiyil* (in truth/actually), Indonesian *sebenarnya* (actually/in truth), Turkish *aslında* (actually/in fact), Korean *사실을 말하자면* (to tell the truth). The function is present in all eight languages.

**Caveats / exceptions.** The degree of grammaticalization varies considerably. The Yoruba realization (hortative construction) is the most compositionally transparent and least grammaticalized. The Indonesian and Turkish realizations are fully grammaticalized. This variation suggests that the discourse-framing function is a pragmatic universal but the specific constructional form is language-specific.

**Confidence rating.** High (as a pragmatic-functional pattern); medium (as a structural constraint — the function is broadly attested but the form is not).

## 2. Major Outliers

---

### 2.1 Language-Level Outliers

**Yoruba (yo) — Broadest divergence from the cross-language pattern**

Yoruba diverges from the cross-language pattern in several respects:

1. **Postnominal modifier position.** Yoruba is the only language in the dataset where truth-domain evaluative modifiers follow the head noun rather than preceding it. This is a systematic structural difference in the direction of modification affecting all adnominal modification constructions. The divergence is broad and reflects a typological property of Yoruba rather than a truth-domain-specific constraint.

2. **Absence of documented dedicated forgery vocabulary.** Yoruba lacks documented dedicated vocabulary for documentary forgery and counterfeiting as a distinct legal category. The concept is presumably expressible through general deception vocabulary applied to documents, but this is not documented. The divergence is narrow (specific to D4 in legal contexts) and may reflect a gap in data collection rather than a genuine absence.

3. **Absence of documented dedicated legal/procedural validity vocabulary.** Yoruba lacks documented dedicated vocabulary for the validity or invalidity of legal instruments parallel to the other seven languages. The divergence is narrow (specific to D5) and may reflect a data collection gap.

4. **Absence of a grammaticalized discourse-framing adverbial.** Yoruba uses a hortative construction (*jẹ́ kí a sọ òtítọ́*, let us speak the truth) for the frank-disclosure function rather than a grammaticalized adverbial. This is the most compositionally transparent realization of CF8 in the dataset. The divergence is narrow (specific to the surface form of CF8) and does not affect the availability of the function.

5. **Merger of propositional correctness and procedural correctness.** The *tọ́* root covers both D1 (propositional correctness) and D11 (procedural/moral correctness) without lexical differentiation. This merger is shared with Tamil but is more complete in Yoruba.

**Assessment.** The postnominal modifier position is the broadest divergence and reflects a typological property of Yoruba. The absence of forgery and validity vocabulary may reflect data collection gaps rather than genuine structural differences. The D1/D11 merger is a genuine structural difference but is shared with Tamil. Overall, the Yoruba divergences are real but most are narrow or potentially attributable to data collection scope.

---

**Korean (ko) — Most internally differentiated vocabulary; unique constructional constraints**

Korean diverges from the cross-language pattern in the direction of greater elaboration and more specific constructional constraints:

1. **The *있다/없다* existential frame as the primary predication strategy for *-seong* nominals.** Korean is the only language in the dataset where a morphological restriction blocks standard copular predication (*-hada*) for a specific class of abstract quality nominals (*-seong*), forcing the existential *있다/없다* frame instead. This is a language-specific constructional constraint with no parallel in any other language. The divergence is narrow (specific to *-seong* nominals) but well-documented.

2. **The *-하다/-되다* stative/agentive distinction.** Korean is the only language in the dataset that documents a systematic semantic distinction between performed quality (*-하다*) and inherent/resultant quality (*-되다*) for truth-domain predicates. This morphological distinction has no parallel in any other language. The divergence is narrow but well-documented.

3. **Three-way inchoative verb distinction for truth-revelation.** Korean documents three distinct inchoative verbs for truth-revelation (*드러나다*, *밝혀지다*, *판명되다*) with different nuances of agency, formality, and the nature of the revelation process. Other languages typically use a single verb for this function. The divergence is narrow (specific to the internal differentiation of CF10) and well-documented.

4. **Most internally differentiated vocabulary.** Korean maintains distinct Sino-Korean roots for factual accuracy (*사실*), truthfulness/sincerity (*진실*), precision/exactness (*정확*), honesty in conduct (*정직*), trustworthiness (*신뢰*), and authenticity of inner state (*진정성*). No other language in the dataset shows this degree of internal differentiation.

**Assessment.** The Korean divergences are in the direction of greater elaboration and more specific constructional constraints rather than in the direction of absence or merger. The *있다/없다* restriction and the *-하다/-되다* distinction are genuine structural differences with no parallel in other languages. The greater vocabulary differentiation may reflect the Sino-Korean vocabulary layer rather than a structural property of Korean per se.

---

**Basque (eu) — Unique three-way verifiability distinction and documented semantic shift**

Basque diverges from the cross-language pattern in two specific respects:

1. **Three-way verifiability distinction.** Basque is the only language in the dataset that documents a three-way morphological distinction within the verifiability sub-domain: *egiaztagarria* (verifiable), *egiaztagaitza* (difficult to verify), *egiaztaezina* (impossible to verify). Other languages show only a binary verifiable/unverifiable opposition. The divergence is narrow (specific to D9 within the verifiability sub-dimension) but well-documented.

2. **Semantic shift: certainty → probability.** Basque *ziurrenik* has shifted from "certainly" to "probably/most likely" — a semantic shift from full certainty to hedged probability within the same derivational family. No parallel shift is documented in any other language in the dataset. The divergence is narrow (specific to a single form) and is a lexicalized change rather than a structural constraint.

**Assessment.** The Basque divergences are narrow and well-documented. The three-way verifiability distinction is a genuine structural elaboration not found in other languages. The *ziurrenik* semantic shift is a lexicalized change that does not affect the broader structural patterns.

---

**Turkish (tr) — Unique parallel morphological opposition for verification/falsification**

Turkish is the only language in the dataset where the same derivational suffix (*-la-*) applies productively to both a truth base (*doğru*) and a falsity base (*yanlış*) to produce a morphologically parallel pair of opposing verbs (*doğrulamak/yanlışlamak*) with further parallel stacking generating *doğrulanabilirlik/yanlışlanabilirlik* (verifiability/falsifiability). This is a language-specific elaboration of D9 not found in any other language.

**Assessment.** The Turkish divergence is narrow (specific to the morphological encoding of the verification/falsification distinction) and well-documented. It does not affect the broader structural patterns.

---

**Georgian (ka) — Unique root extension into non-epistemic institutional domains and register bifurcation**

Georgian is unique in two specific respects:

1. **Breadth of institutional extension of the *marṭl-* root.** The *marṭl-* root extends into orthography (*marṭlc̣era*), Orthodox Christianity (*marṭlmadidebeli*), the justice system (*marṭlmsajuleba*), and proportionality (*marṭlzomiereba*). No other language in the dataset shows this breadth of institutional extension from a single truth-domain root.

2. **Register bifurcation of *sarwmunoeba*.** The same form means "credibility" in secular discourse and "faith/religion" in ecclesiastical discourse. This is a single-form semantic split unique in the dataset.

**Assessment.** Both divergences are narrow and well-documented. They reflect the specific historical and cultural elaboration of the truth domain in Georgian rather than broad structural differences.

---

**Tamil (ta) — Partial merger of propositional and procedural correctness**

Tamil shares with Yoruba the merger of D1 (propositional correctness) and D11 (procedural/moral correctness) under the *cari* root, which covers both factual correctness and procedural correctness without differentiation. Tamil also shows less elaboration of D6 (Precision and Exactness) and D8 (Appearance vs. Underlying Reality) as distinct lexical dimensions compared to Georgian, Finnish, Basque, and Korean.

**Assessment.** The Tamil divergences are narrower than the Yoruba divergences. The D1/D11 merger is a genuine structural difference but is shared with Yoruba. The less elaborated D6 and D8 dimensions may reflect the data collection scope rather than genuine structural differences.

---

### 2.2 Construction-Level Outliers

**CF7 (Agentive Nominalization) — Universal positive-role block**

The universal absence of lexicalized single-word agentive nominals for truth-tellers, honest persons (as role nouns), and authenticators is a construction-level outlier in the sense that it is a systematic gap rather than a restriction on a specific language or dimension. The gap is consistent across all eight languages and all language families represented. It is the most consistently documented blocked pathway in the dataset.

**Assessment.** The divergence is broad (affects all dimensions where positive agentive nominals would be expected) and consistent across all eight languages. The mechanism is not established — it may reflect frequency effects, structural constraints, or both. The asymmetry with the negative pole (liar, forger: universally lexicalized) is striking.

---

**CF8 (Discourse Framing) — Degree of grammaticalization varies widely**

The discourse-framing construction for frank disclosure varies from fully grammaticalized (Indonesian *sebenarnya*, Turkish *aslında*) to fully compositional (Yoruba hortative construction). This is a construction-level outlier in the sense that the surface form varies more widely than any other constructional function in the dataset.

**Assessment.** The divergence is in the degree of grammaticalization rather than in the availability of the function. The function is broadly attested; the surface form varies. This is a typological variation in grammaticalization degree rather than a structural outlier.

---

**CF9 (Existential / Scalar Predication) — Korean-specific morphological restriction**

The Korean *있다/없다* existential frame as the primary predication strategy for *-seong* nominals (where *-hada* is blocked) is a construction-level outlier. No other language in the dataset documents an equivalent morphological restriction on predication strategy for abstract quality nominals.

**Assessment.** The divergence is narrow (specific to *-seong* nominals in Korean) and well-documented. It does not affect the broader structural patterns.

---

**CF11 (Parallel Morphological Opposition) — Restricted to Turkish**

The parallel morphological opposition for verification/falsification (*doğrulamak/yanlışlamak*, *doğrulanabilirlik/yanlışlanabilirlik*) is documented as a productive pattern only in Turkish. Partial parallels exist in Finnish and Basque but are not as systematically productive.

**Assessment.** The divergence is narrow (specific to the morphological encoding of the verification/falsification distinction) and well-documented. It is a language-specific elaboration rather than a cross-linguistic pattern.

---

### 2.3 Dimension-Specific Outliers

**D6 (Precision and Exactness) — Less elaborated in Yoruba and Tamil**

The precision/exactness dimension is not elaborated as a distinct lexical category in Yoruba and Tamil; the concept is expressed through the general correctness vocabulary of D1. In contrast, Georgian, Finnish, Basque, Indonesian, and Korean show dedicated precision vocabulary (*zusti*, *tarkka*, *zehatza*, *tepat/akurat*, *정확*).

**Assessment.** The divergence is narrow (specific to D6) and may reflect the data collection scope rather than a genuine structural difference. The concept of precision is presumably expressible in Yoruba and Tamil through compositional constructions.

---

**D7 (Authenticity of Inner States) — Degree of elaboration varies widely**

The inner-state authenticity dimension is most elaborated in Korean (*진정성* as a prominent public evaluative category) and least elaborated in Tamil and Turkish as a distinct lexical dimension. The degree of elaboration varies more widely for D7 than for any other dimension in the dataset.

**Assessment.** The divergence is in degree of elaboration rather than in the availability of the dimension. The concept is expressible in all eight languages; the degree of lexical elaboration varies. Korean's *진정성* cluster is a genuine outlier in the direction of greater elaboration.

---

**D8 (Appearance vs. Underlying Reality) — Dedicated adjective absent in five of eight languages**

The appearance-vs.-reality dimension is expressed through dedicated adjectives in Georgian (*moč̣venebiti*), Finnish (*näennäinen*), and Basque (*itxurazko*), but through discourse markers and constructions in Yoruba, Tamil, Indonesian, Turkish, and Korean. The dedicated adjective for "apparent/seeming/ostensible" is absent or marginal in five of the eight languages.

**Assessment.** The divergence is narrow (specific to the availability of a dedicated adjective for the "apparent/seeming" pole of D8). The contrast between appearance and reality is expressible in all eight languages; the lexical encoding varies.

---

**D1/D11 merger in Yoruba and Tamil**

The merger of propositional correctness (D1) and procedural/moral correctness (D11) under a single root (*tọ́* in Yoruba, *cari* in Tamil) is a dimension-specific outlier. In the other six languages, distinct vocabulary is used for these two functions.

**Assessment.** The divergence is narrow (specific to the D1/D11 boundary) and is shared between two unrelated language families (Niger-Congo and Dravidian). The merger may reflect a genuine structural property of these languages or may reflect the data collection scope.

## 3. Open Questions

---

**OQ-1. What is the mechanism behind the universal positive agentive nominalization block?**

The universal absence of lexicalized single-word agentive nominals for truth-tellers and honest persons (as role nouns) is the most consistently documented empirical finding in the dataset. The mechanism is not established. Possible explanations include: (a) frequency effects — liars are more salient as social categories than truth-tellers; (b) a structural constraint related to the dispositional causation block — if dispositional character traits cannot be externally caused, they may also resist being named as stable role identities; (c) a pragmatic asymmetry — the positive pole is the default and does not require marking. Targeted elicitation testing whether coined positive agentive forms (e.g., *\*totuudenpuhuja* in Finnish, *\*진실쟁이* in Korean) are rejected as unnatural, awkward, or simply unusual would help distinguish these explanations.

---

**OQ-2. Is the D1/D11 merger (propositional correctness / procedural correctness) a genuine structural property of Yoruba and Tamil, or a data collection artifact?**

The merger of D1 and D11 under a single root is documented in two unrelated language families (Niger-Congo and Dravidian). If genuine, it suggests that the VER/NORM boundary is not universally maintained and may be a secondary elaboration in languages that distinguish them. More targeted data collection in Yoruba and Tamil, specifically testing whether the two dimensions can be distinguished in context, would help resolve this question.

---

**OQ-3. Is the neutral-copy / fraudulent-fake distinction (CU-7) genuinely absent in Georgian and Yoruba, or is it expressed through constructions not captured in the current data?**

The three-way distinction (original/neutral copy/fraudulent fake) is documented in 6/8 languages. Georgian has a binary *namdvili/q̇albi* without a documented neutral intermediate. Yoruba lacks a dedicated forgery vocabulary. It is unclear whether these languages genuinely lack the neutral intermediate category or whether it is expressed through context or through general deception vocabulary applied to objects. Targeted elicitation in Georgian and Yoruba would help resolve this question.

---

**OQ-4. What is the cross-linguistic status of the appearance-vs.-reality dimension (D8)?**

D8 is expressed through dedicated adjectives in three languages (Georgian *moč̣venebiti*, Finnish *näennäinen*, Basque *itxurazko*) and through discourse markers and constructions in the other five. It is unclear whether the absence of a dedicated adjective in five languages reflects a genuine structural difference or a data collection gap. More targeted data collection, specifically testing whether "apparent/seeming/ostensible" can be expressed as a single attributive adjective in Yoruba, Tamil, Indonesian, Turkish, and Korean, would help resolve this question.

---

**OQ-5. Is the positive emergence bias (CU-16) a structural constraint or a frequency effect?**

The universal preference for positive emergence constructions (truth comes to light) over negative emergence constructions (truth becomes false) is consistent across all eight languages. However, the data does not systematically test negative emergence constructions. It is unclear whether negative emergence is structurally blocked or simply infrequent. Targeted elicitation of negative emergence constructions (e.g., "the truth became false," "the genuine article became a fake") across all eight languages would help resolve this question.

---

**OQ-6. What is the cross-linguistic status of the inner-state authenticity dimension (D7)?**

D7 is most elaborated in Korean (*진정성*) and least elaborated in Tamil and Turkish as a distinct lexical dimension. It is unclear whether the variation in elaboration reflects a genuine structural difference or a difference in the cultural salience of inner-state authenticity as a public evaluative category. More targeted data collection in Tamil and Turkish, specifically testing whether inner-state authenticity can be distinguished from general honesty, would help resolve this question.

---

**OQ-7. Does the indirect facilitation of dispositional character traits (attested in Basque and Korean) constitute a genuine exception to the dispositional causation block (CU-13), or is it a distinct construction type?**

Some causative constructions for inner-state authenticity are attested in Basque (*zintzotu eragin*) and Korean (*진정성을 높이다*). These appear to cause conditions for authenticity rather than authenticity itself. The distinction between direct causation of the dispositional state and indirect facilitation of conditions for that state is not always clearly drawn in the data. More targeted elicitation of direct vs. indirect causation constructions across all eight languages would help resolve this question.

---

**OQ-8. Is the spatial metaphor of straightness (CU-15) a genuine cross-linguistic pattern or a coincidence of independent parallel development?**

The spatial metaphor of straightness underlying truth and correctness vocabulary is attested in all eight languages. However, the spatial etymology is more transparent in some languages than others, and the pattern may reflect independent parallel development rather than a single origin. Comparative historical data and broader language coverage would help determine whether this is a genuine cross-linguistic pattern or a coincidence.

---

**OQ-9. What is the cross-linguistic status of the philosophical/technical truth domain (truth values, truth conditions, truth tables)?**

The philosophical/technical truth domain is documented in Finnish (*totuusarvo*, *totuusehdot*, *totuustaulu*), Turkish (*doğruluk değeri*, *doğruluk koşulu*, *doğruluk tablosu*), and Korean (*진리값*), but is absent or marginal in the other five languages. It is unclear whether this reflects a genuine structural difference or a difference in the degree of philosophical/logical elaboration in the data collection. More targeted data collection in Georgian, Yoruba, Basque, Tamil, and Indonesian would help resolve this question.

---

**OQ-10. Is the proverbial assertion that truth ultimately prevails (CU-11) a structural constraint or a cultural-pragmatic pattern?**

The cross-linguistic proverbial pattern asserting the ultimate triumph of truth is documented in all eight languages. However, it is unclear whether this reflects a structural constraint (truth is conceptualized as temporally persistent and inevitable) or a cultural-pragmatic pattern (moral instruction in diverse cultures emphasizes the value of truth). The Turkish variant (*doğruluk her zaman ödüllendirilmez*, truthfulness is not always rewarded) is more pessimistic than the others, suggesting the pattern may be cultural rather than structural. More language coverage, including languages from cultures with different moral traditions, would help resolve this question.

---

**OQ-11. What is the cross-linguistic status of the Basque three-way verifiability distinction?**

Basque is the only language in the dataset that documents a three-way morphological distinction within the verifiability sub-domain (verifiable/difficult to verify/impossible to verify). It is unclear whether this is a language-specific elaboration or whether other languages express the same three-way distinction through different means (e.g., through adverbial modification of a binary verifiable/unverifiable opposition). More targeted data collection in the other seven languages, specifically testing whether the three-way distinction can be expressed, would help resolve this question.

---

**OQ-12. Does the Korean *있다/없다* existential frame restriction reflect a broader cross-linguistic pattern of predication strategy differentiation for abstract quality nominals?**

The Korean morphological restriction that blocks *-hada* predicative suffixation on *-seong* quality nominals, forcing the existential *있다/없다* frame, is documented only in Korean. It is unclear whether this is a language-specific morphological constraint or whether other languages show analogous restrictions on predication strategies for abstract quality nominals that were not captured in the current data collection. More targeted data collection in the other seven languages, specifically testing predication strategies for abstract quality nominals, would help resolve this question.

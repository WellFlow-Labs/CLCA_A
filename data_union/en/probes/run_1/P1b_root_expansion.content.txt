# CLCA Protocol v3.2 — P1b ROOT EXPANSION
# Language: English (en)

---

## Step 1 — Identify Central Roots

---

**Root 1: TRUE / TRĒOWE**

- **Root form:** *true* (Old English *trēowe*)
- **P1 items participating:** *true* (1), *truth* (2), *trustworthy* (27) — via *trust* from cognate Old Norse *traust*; also *veracity* (15) and *verisimilitude* (24) share the semantic field but derive from Latin *verus* rather than this root. Direct participation: **3 items** (*true*, *truth*, *trustworthy*); the root also underlies the derivational family of *truly*, *untrue*, *trueness* mentioned within entries.
- **Core meaning contribution:** Faithfulness, conformity with fact, reliability of persons and propositions. The root supplies the primary English word for propositional truth and generates the most productive derivational family in the domain.

---

**Root 2: VERUS (Latin)**

- **Root form:** *ver-* (Latin *verus*, true)
- **P1 items participating:** *veracious* (14), *veracity* (15), *verisimilitude* (24) — 3 items directly; the root also underlies *verify*, *verdict*, *verity*, and related forms mentioned in usage notes.
- **Core meaning contribution:** Objective truth, factual correspondence, truthfulness as a property of persons and statements. Supplies the formal and learned stratum of the English truth-evaluation vocabulary.

---

**Root 3: FALSUS (Latin)**

- **Root form:** *fals-* (Latin *falsus*, from *fallere*, to deceive)
- **P1 items participating:** *false* (10), *falsehood* (11) — 2 items directly; the root also underlies *falsify*, *falsification*, *falsity*, *falsely*, *falseness* mentioned within entries.
- **Core meaning contribution:** Untruth, deception, inauthenticity. Supplies the primary learned and formal vocabulary for the negative pole of the truth-evaluation domain, covering both propositional falsity and moral duplicity.

---

> **Note on selection rationale:** Three roots were selected because no single root dominates overwhelmingly. *TRUE/TRĒOWE* is the most productive in everyday English; *VERUS* supplies the formal and technical stratum; *FALSUS* anchors the negative pole. The root *GENUINE/GENUINUS* and *VALID/VALIDUS* were considered but each participates in only one or two P1 items and generate smaller families. The root *CRED-* (Latin *credere*) was also considered given *credible* and *credibility* in P1, but its expansion belongs more centrally to the belief/trust domain than the truth-evaluation domain proper.

## Step 2 — Expand Each Root

---

### ROOT 1: TRUE / TRĒOWE

---

**1.1**
- **Form:** *true*
- **Morphological breakdown:** Old English *trēowe* (faithful, trustworthy)
- **Natural English translation:** true
- **Context of use:** The base adjective, used predicatively and attributively across all registers to assert factual correctness, genuineness, or faithfulness. Already in P1; listed here as the anchor form.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.2**
- **Form:** *truth*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *true* + Old English *-th* (abstract noun suffix)
- **Natural English translation:** truth
- **Context of use:** The nominalization of *true*; refers to the property of being true or to a true statement. Already in P1; listed here as anchor form.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.3**
- **Form:** *truly*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *true* + *-ly* (adverbial suffix)
- **Natural English translation:** truly, genuinely, really
- **Context of use:** Used as an adverb to intensify or affirm the genuineness of a predicate (*truly remarkable*, *truly sorry*) and in formal letter closings (*Yours truly*). Appears across all registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.4**
- **Form:** *untrue*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *un-* (negation) + *true*
- **Natural English translation:** untrue, false, unfaithful
- **Context of use:** Applied to statements and representations to indicate that they do not correspond to fact. Also used of persons to indicate unfaithfulness or disloyalty, particularly in older or literary usage.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.5**
- **Form:** *untruth*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *un-* (negation) + *truth*
- **Natural English translation:** untruth, falsehood, lie
- **Context of use:** Refers to a false statement, often with a softer connotation than *lie*, implying that the falsity may be partial or unintentional. Used in formal, journalistic, and political discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.6**
- **Form:** *trueness*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *true* + *-ness* (abstract noun suffix)
- **Natural English translation:** trueness, accuracy, fidelity
- **Context of use:** Refers to the quality of being true or accurate, particularly in technical contexts such as engineering (the *trueness* of a surface or measurement). Less common than *truth* in philosophical contexts.
- **Epistemic status:** [MARGINAL]

---

**1.7**
- **Form:** *true-blue*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *true* + *blue* (historically associated with constancy and loyalty)
- **Natural English translation:** genuinely loyal, staunchly faithful, thoroughly committed
- **Context of use:** Used to describe persons or things that are thoroughly genuine, loyal, or committed to a cause or identity. Common in informal and political discourse (*a true-blue conservative*).
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.8**
- **Form:** *true-life*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *true* + *life*
- **Natural English translation:** based on real events, factual
- **Context of use:** Used attributively to describe narratives, films, or accounts that are based on actual events rather than invented (*a true-life story*). Common in popular media and publishing.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.9**
- **Form:** *true north*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *true* + *north* (geographic direction)
- **Natural English translation:** geographic north (as opposed to magnetic north); also: one's guiding principle or authentic direction
- **Context of use:** In navigation, refers to the direction of the geographic North Pole as distinct from magnetic north. Metaphorically used in leadership and self-help discourse to mean one's authentic values or guiding purpose.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.10**
- **Form:** *true to life*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *true* + *to* + *life*
- **Natural English translation:** realistic, lifelike, accurately representing reality
- **Context of use:** Applied to artistic works, descriptions, and portrayals to indicate that they accurately represent real experience or appearance. Used in literary criticism, art criticism, and everyday evaluation of representations.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.11**
- **Form:** *true to form*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *true* + *to* + *form* (established pattern of behavior)
- **Natural English translation:** characteristically, as expected, consistent with past behavior
- **Context of use:** Used to indicate that someone or something is behaving in a manner consistent with their established character or pattern. Common in journalism and informal commentary.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.12**
- **Form:** *true to type*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *true* + *to* + *type* (established category or pattern)
- **Natural English translation:** conforming to the expected characteristics of a type or category
- **Context of use:** Used in biological, psychological, and informal contexts to indicate that an individual or instance conforms to the defining characteristics of its category.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.13**
- **Form:** *come true*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *come* + *true* (predicative adjective)
- **Natural English translation:** to be realized, to happen as hoped or predicted
- **Context of use:** Applied to dreams, predictions, and wishes to indicate that they have been realized in fact. Extremely common in everyday speech and narrative contexts.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.14**
- **Form:** *ring true*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *ring* (to sound) + *true* (predicative adjective)
- **Natural English translation:** to sound genuine, to seem credible or authentic
- **Context of use:** Applied to statements, explanations, and accounts to indicate that they have the quality of being believable or genuine. The metaphor derives from testing coins by their sound. Common in everyday and journalistic usage.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.15**
- **Form:** *hold true*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *hold* + *true* (predicative adjective)
- **Natural English translation:** to remain valid, to continue to apply
- **Context of use:** Used of principles, rules, and generalizations to indicate that they remain applicable in a given context or over time. Common in academic, legal, and analytical writing.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.16**
- **Form:** *prove true*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *prove* + *true* (predicative adjective)
- **Natural English translation:** to turn out to be correct, to be confirmed by events
- **Context of use:** Applied to predictions, suspicions, and claims that are subsequently confirmed by evidence or events. Used across all registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.17**
- **Form:** *true enough*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *true* + *enough* (degree modifier)
- **Natural English translation:** admittedly correct, fair enough, that is accurate as far as it goes
- **Context of use:** Used as a concessive expression to acknowledge the partial or qualified truth of a statement before introducing a qualification or counterpoint. Common in spoken and written argumentation.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.18**
- **Form:** *too true*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *too* (intensifier) + *true*
- **Natural English translation:** unfortunately very accurate, regrettably correct
- **Context of use:** Used as an emphatic affirmation, often with a tone of resignation or regret, to confirm that something unpleasant or unwelcome is indeed the case. Common in informal speech.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.19**
- **Form:** *truth-telling*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *truth* + *tell* + *-ing* (gerund/participial suffix)
- **Natural English translation:** the practice of telling the truth, honest disclosure
- **Context of use:** Used in moral philosophy, journalism, and political discourse to describe the practice or act of disclosing facts honestly, often in contexts where there is pressure to conceal or distort.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.20**
- **Form:** *truth-seeker*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *truth* + *seek* + *-er* (agent suffix)
- **Natural English translation:** one who seeks truth, an investigator of facts
- **Context of use:** Applied to philosophers, journalists, scientists, and others who are characterized by a commitment to discovering what is true. Used in intellectual and journalistic discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.21**
- **Form:** *truth value*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *truth* + *value* (logical or semantic value)
- **Natural English translation:** the truth or falsity of a proposition
- **Context of use:** A technical term in formal logic and semantics referring to whether a proposition is true or false. Used in logic, linguistics, and philosophy of language.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.22**
- **Form:** *truth condition*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *truth* + *condition* (requirement for obtaining)
- **Natural English translation:** the condition under which a statement is true
- **Context of use:** A technical term in formal semantics and philosophy of language referring to the circumstances that must obtain for a sentence to be true. Central to truth-conditional semantics.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.23**
- **Form:** *truth table*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *truth* + *table* (systematic arrangement)
- **Natural English translation:** a table showing the truth values of compound propositions
- **Context of use:** A technical term in formal logic and computer science referring to a tabular representation of all possible truth values of a logical expression and its components.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.24**
- **Form:** *truth claim*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *truth* + *claim* (assertion)
- **Natural English translation:** an assertion that something is true
- **Context of use:** Used in philosophy, theology, and academic discourse to refer to any assertion that purports to state a fact about reality, particularly when the validity of such assertions is under examination.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.25**
- **Form:** *ground truth*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *ground* (foundational, actual) + *truth*
- **Natural English translation:** verified factual information; the actual state of affairs on the ground
- **Context of use:** Used in remote sensing, machine learning, and military contexts to refer to information obtained by direct observation or measurement, used as a standard against which models or reports are validated. Also used more broadly to mean the actual facts of a situation.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.26**
- **Form:** *home truth*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *home* (direct, personal) + *truth*
- **Natural English translation:** an unpleasant but accurate fact told directly to someone about themselves
- **Context of use:** Used to describe a frank and often uncomfortable statement of fact directed at a person about their own character, behavior, or situation. Common in interpersonal and journalistic contexts.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.27**
- **Form:** *half-truth*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *half* + *truth*
- **Natural English translation:** a statement that is partially true but misleading
- **Context of use:** Applied to statements that contain some factual basis but omit or distort information in a way that creates a false impression. Used in political, journalistic, and moral discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.28**
- **Form:** *truth be told*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *truth* + *be* + *told* (subjunctive/passive construction)
- **Natural English translation:** to be honest, frankly speaking, if one is to be truthful
- **Context of use:** Used as a discourse marker to introduce a frank or candid statement, often one that the speaker might otherwise hesitate to make. Common in spoken and informal written English.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.29**
- **Form:** *the truth of the matter*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *the truth* + *of the matter* (prepositional phrase specifying the subject)
- **Natural English translation:** the actual facts of the situation
- **Context of use:** A fixed phrase used to introduce or emphasize what the speaker regards as the actual facts, often in contrast to appearances, claims, or misconceptions. Common across all registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.30**
- **Form:** *moment of truth*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *moment* + *of* + *truth*
- **Natural English translation:** a decisive moment when the true nature of something is revealed or tested
- **Context of use:** Used to describe a critical juncture at which the real quality, character, or validity of something is put to the test. Borrowed from Spanish *momento de la verdad* (bullfighting). Common in journalism, business, and everyday speech.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.31**
- **Form:** *trust*
- **Morphological breakdown:** Old Norse *traust* (confidence, help); cognate with *true*
- **Natural English translation:** trust, confidence, reliance
- **Context of use:** Refers to confident reliance on the integrity, ability, or character of a person or thing. Extremely productive: *trustworthy*, *trustworthiness*, *distrust*, *mistrust*, *entrust*, *trust fund*, *trust relationship*. Listed here as a cognate root form.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.32**
- **Form:** *trustworthy*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *trust* + *worthy* (deserving)
- **Natural English translation:** deserving of trust, reliable, honest
- **Context of use:** Already in P1; listed here to anchor the *trust* branch of the root family.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.33**
- **Form:** *trustworthiness*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *trust* + *worthy* + *-ness*
- **Natural English translation:** the quality of being trustworthy
- **Context of use:** Used in assessments of character, institutional reliability, and source credibility. Common in journalism, psychology, and ethics.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.34**
- **Form:** *distrust*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *dis-* (negation/reversal) + *trust*
- **Natural English translation:** distrust, lack of confidence, suspicion
- **Context of use:** Used as noun and verb to indicate a lack of confidence in the reliability or honesty of a person, institution, or source. Common across all registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.35**
- **Form:** *mistrust*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *mis-* (wrongly, badly) + *trust*
- **Natural English translation:** mistrust, suspicion, doubt
- **Context of use:** Used as noun and verb to indicate suspicion or doubt about the reliability or honesty of someone or something. Slightly more active in connotation than *distrust*. Common across all registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.36**
- **Form:** *entrust*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *en-* (causative prefix) + *trust*
- **Natural English translation:** to place in someone's care, to give responsibility to
- **Context of use:** Used to indicate the act of placing something valuable or important in the care or responsibility of another person. Common in legal, administrative, and everyday contexts.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.37**
- **Form:** *true-born*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *true* + *born* (past participle of *bear*)
- **Natural English translation:** of genuine birth or origin, authentically of a particular kind
- **Context of use:** Used to indicate that someone is genuinely of a particular nationality, lineage, or character by birth. Primarily literary and historical in current usage (*a true-born Englishman*).
- **Epistemic status:** [MARGINAL]

---

**1.38**
- **Form:** *true-hearted*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *true* + *heart* + *-ed* (having the quality of)
- **Natural English translation:** loyal, sincere, faithful in heart
- **Context of use:** Applied to persons to indicate genuine loyalty and sincerity of feeling. Primarily literary and archaic in current usage.
- **Epistemic status:** [MARGINAL]

---

**1.39**
- **Form:** *post-truth*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *post-* (after) + *truth*
- **Natural English translation:** relating to a situation in which objective facts are less influential than emotional appeals in shaping public opinion
- **Context of use:** A contemporary term used in political and media discourse to describe an environment in which truth is systematically devalued in public debate. Widely used since approximately 2016.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.40**
- **Form:** *truth-functional*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *truth* + *function* + *-al*
- **Natural English translation:** having a truth value determined solely by the truth values of its components
- **Context of use:** A technical term in formal logic referring to connectives or compound propositions whose truth value is entirely determined by the truth values of their constituent propositions.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

> **Note:** The *true/truth/trust* family is highly productive. Additional attested forms include *truthful*, *truthfully*, *truthfulness*, *untruthful*, *untruthfulness*, *true-false* (as in *true-false test*), *truth drug*, *truth serum*, *gospel truth*, *naked truth*, *plain truth*, *self-evident truth*, *universal truth*, *eternal truth*, *inconvenient truth*, and *speaking truth to power*. The forms listed above represent the 20–30 most common and analytically significant.

### ROOT 2: VERUS (Latin *ver-*)

---

**2.1**
- **Form:** *verify*
- **Morphological breakdown:** Latin *verus* (true) + *-fy* (from Latin *facere*, to make)
- **Natural English translation:** to confirm the truth of, to check for accuracy
- **Context of use:** Used across legal, scientific, administrative, and everyday contexts to indicate the act of checking or confirming that something is true, accurate, or genuine. Extremely common in digital and bureaucratic contexts (*verify your identity*, *verify a claim*).
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**2.2**
- **Form:** *verification*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *verify* + *-ation* (process noun suffix)
- **Natural English translation:** the process of verifying, confirmation of truth or accuracy
- **Context of use:** Used in scientific, legal, administrative, and technical contexts to refer to the process of establishing that something is true, accurate, or meets a required standard. Also used in logic and philosophy of science.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**2.3**
- **Form:** *verifiable*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *verify* + *-able* (capable of being)
- **Natural English translation:** capable of being verified, able to be checked or confirmed
- **Context of use:** Applied to claims, hypotheses, and data to indicate that they can in principle be checked against evidence. Central to empiricist and scientific discourse. Productive: *verifiability*, *unverifiable*.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**2.4**
- **Form:** *verifiability*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *verifiable* + *-ity* (abstract noun suffix)
- **Natural English translation:** the property of being verifiable
- **Context of use:** A key term in logical positivism and philosophy of science, referring to the property of a statement that makes it possible in principle to confirm or disconfirm it by empirical evidence.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**2.5**
- **Form:** *unverifiable*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *un-* (negation) + *verifiable*
- **Natural English translation:** not capable of being verified or confirmed
- **Context of use:** Applied to claims, allegations, and hypotheses that cannot be checked against evidence, either in principle or in practice. Used in epistemology, journalism, and legal contexts.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**2.6**
- **Form:** *verity*
- **Morphological breakdown:** Latin *veritas* (truth), from *verus* (true) + *-ity*
- **Natural English translation:** truth, a true statement or principle
- **Context of use:** A formal and literary term for truth or a fundamental truth. Used in philosophical and religious discourse (*eternal verities*). Less common in everyday speech than *truth*.
- **Epistemic status:** [MARGINAL]

---

**2.7**
- **Form:** *veracity*
- **Morphological breakdown:** Medieval Latin *veracitas*, from *verax* (truthful), from *verus* (true)
- **Natural English translation:** truthfulness, accuracy, honesty
- **Context of use:** Already in P1; listed here to anchor the *verax* branch of the *verus* family. Used in legal and journalistic contexts to assess the reliability of testimony.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**2.8**
- **Form:** *veracious*
- **Morphological breakdown:** Latin *verax* (truthful) + *-ious* (adjectival suffix)
- **Natural English translation:** habitually truthful, accurate
- **Context of use:** Already in P1; listed here as part of the *verus* family. Applied to persons and statements to indicate habitual truthfulness.
- **Epistemic status:** [MARGINAL]

---

**2.9**
- **Form:** *verisimilitude*
- **Morphological breakdown:** Latin *verus* (true) + *similis* (like) + *-tude* (abstract noun suffix)
- **Natural English translation:** the appearance of truth, plausibility, lifelikeness
- **Context of use:** Already in P1; listed here as part of the *verus* family. Used in literary criticism and philosophy of science.
- **Epistemic status:** [MARGINAL]

---

**2.10**
- **Form:** *verdict*
- **Morphological breakdown:** Medieval Latin *veredictum*, from *verus* (true) + *dictum* (thing said, from *dicere*, to say)
- **Natural English translation:** a formal decision or judgment, especially in a legal trial
- **Context of use:** The formal decision of a jury or judge in a legal proceeding. Also used more broadly to mean any authoritative judgment or conclusion. Extremely common in legal and everyday discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**2.11**
- **Form:** *aver*
- **Morphological breakdown:** Old French *averer*, from Medieval Latin *averare*, from *verus* (true)
- **Natural English translation:** to assert confidently, to declare as true
- **Context of use:** Used in legal and formal contexts to indicate a confident assertion or declaration of fact. More formal than *assert* or *claim*. Productive: *averment*.
- **Epistemic status:** [MARGINAL]

---

**2.12**
- **Form:** *averment*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *aver* + *-ment* (noun suffix)
- **Natural English translation:** a formal assertion or declaration, especially in legal pleading
- **Context of use:** A legal term referring to a positive assertion of fact in a pleading. Used in formal legal writing and procedure.
- **Epistemic status:** [MARGINAL]

---

**2.13**
- **Form:** *veridical*
- **Morphological breakdown:** Latin *veridicus* (truth-telling), from *verus* (true) + *dicere* (to say) + *-al*
- **Natural English translation:** truthful, corresponding to facts; (of a perception or dream) accurately representing reality
- **Context of use:** Used in philosophy and psychology to describe perceptions, dreams, or experiences that accurately represent an actual state of affairs, as opposed to hallucinations or illusions. Also used more broadly to mean truthful or accurate.
- **Epistemic status:** [MARGINAL]

---

**2.14**
- **Form:** *veridicality*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *veridical* + *-ity*
- **Natural English translation:** the property of being veridical, accurate correspondence with reality
- **Context of use:** Used in philosophy of perception and cognitive science to refer to the property of a perceptual experience or representation that accurately reflects the actual state of the world.
- **Epistemic status:** [MARGINAL]

---

**2.15**
- **Form:** *in veritas* / *veritas*
- **Morphological breakdown:** Latin *veritas* (truth)
- **Natural English translation:** truth (used as a motto or in formal/philosophical contexts)
- **Context of use:** Used in English primarily as a Latin motto (*In vino veritas*, *Veritas* as a university motto) and in formal philosophical or rhetorical contexts. Not a productive English form but well-attested in English usage.
- **Epistemic status:** [MARGINAL]

---

**2.16**
- **Form:** *in vino veritas*
- **Morphological breakdown:** Latin: *in* (in) + *vino* (wine, ablative) + *veritas* (truth)
- **Natural English translation:** in wine there is truth; people speak honestly when drunk
- **Context of use:** A Latin proverb used in English to express the idea that alcohol causes people to reveal their true thoughts or feelings. Used in everyday, literary, and journalistic contexts.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**2.17**
- **Form:** *fact-verify* / *fact-verification*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *fact* + *verify* / *fact* + *verification*
- **Natural English translation:** to check the factual accuracy of a claim; the process of doing so
- **Context of use:** Used in journalistic and media contexts to describe the process of checking whether specific factual claims are accurate. Related to but distinct from *fact-check* / *fact-checking*.
- **Epistemic status:** [MARGINAL]

---

**2.18**
- **Form:** *verified*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *verify* + *-ed* (past participle / adjectival)
- **Natural English translation:** confirmed as true or genuine, authenticated
- **Context of use:** Used as an adjective to indicate that something has been confirmed as accurate or genuine. Extremely common in digital contexts (*verified account*, *verified information*).
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**2.19**
- **Form:** *unverified*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *un-* + *verified*
- **Natural English translation:** not yet confirmed, unconfirmed, not authenticated
- **Context of use:** Applied to claims, reports, and accounts that have not yet been checked against evidence. Common in journalism and intelligence contexts.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**2.20**
- **Form:** *cross-verify*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *cross-* (across, mutual) + *verify*
- **Natural English translation:** to verify by checking against multiple independent sources
- **Context of use:** Used in research, journalism, and intelligence contexts to describe the process of confirming information by checking it against more than one independent source or method.
- **Epistemic status:** [MARGINAL]

---

> **Note:** The *verus* root is moderately productive in English. Additional attested forms include *verifier*, *self-verifying*, *peer-verified*, and the philosophical term *verisimilar*. The forms listed above represent the most common and analytically significant. The root is less productive in everyday English than in formal, legal, and academic registers.

### ROOT 3: FALSUS (Latin *fals-* / *fall-*)

---

**3.1**
- **Form:** *false*
- **Morphological breakdown:** Old English *fals*, from Latin *falsus*, past participle of *fallere* (to deceive)
- **Natural English translation:** false, untrue, counterfeit, deceitful
- **Context of use:** Already in P1; listed here as the anchor form of the *falsus* family.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.2**
- **Form:** *falsely*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *false* + *-ly* (adverbial suffix)
- **Natural English translation:** in a false manner, incorrectly, deceptively
- **Context of use:** Used as an adverb to indicate that something is done or stated in a manner that is untrue or deceptive (*falsely accused*, *falsely claimed*). Common across all registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.3**
- **Form:** *falseness*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *false* + *-ness* (abstract noun suffix)
- **Natural English translation:** the quality of being false, insincerity, deceptiveness
- **Context of use:** Refers to the quality of being false, either in the sense of factual inaccuracy or moral duplicity. Less common than *falsity* or *falsehood* in formal contexts.
- **Epistemic status:** [MARGINAL]

---

**3.4**
- **Form:** *falsity*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *false* + *-ity* (abstract noun suffix, via Latin *falsitas*)
- **Natural English translation:** the quality of being false; a false statement
- **Context of use:** Used in philosophical and formal contexts to refer to the property of being false, or to a specific false statement. Contrasts with *truth* and *verity*. Less common in everyday speech than *falsehood*.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.5**
- **Form:** *falsehood*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *false* + *-hood* (condition, state)
- **Natural English translation:** a false statement, a lie, the condition of being false
- **Context of use:** Already in P1; listed here as part of the *falsus* family.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.6**
- **Form:** *falsify*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *false* (from Latin *falsus*) + *-ify* (from Latin *facere*, to make)
- **Natural English translation:** to alter fraudulently, to make false, to misrepresent
- **Context of use:** Applied to documents, records, data, and evidence to indicate that they have been altered or fabricated to misrepresent the truth. Used in legal, scientific, and journalistic contexts. Productive: *falsification*, *falsifier*.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.7**
- **Form:** *falsification*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *falsify* + *-ation* (process noun suffix)
- **Natural English translation:** the act of falsifying; in philosophy of science, the process of showing a hypothesis to be false
- **Context of use:** Used in legal and administrative contexts to refer to the fraudulent alteration of records or evidence. Also a key term in Karl Popper's philosophy of science, where it refers to the logical possibility of showing a hypothesis to be false by empirical evidence.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.8**
- **Form:** *falsifiability*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *falsifiable* + *-ity*
- **Natural English translation:** the property of being capable of being shown to be false
- **Context of use:** A central concept in Popperian philosophy of science, referring to the property of a scientific hypothesis that makes it possible in principle to refute it by empirical evidence. Widely used in philosophy of science and science education.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.9**
- **Form:** *falsifiable*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *falsify* + *-able*
- **Natural English translation:** capable of being shown to be false, refutable
- **Context of use:** Applied to hypotheses and theories to indicate that they make predictions that could in principle be contradicted by evidence. A criterion of scientific status in Popperian philosophy.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.10**
- **Form:** *unfalsifiable*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *un-* + *falsifiable*
- **Natural English translation:** not capable of being shown to be false, irrefutable
- **Context of use:** Applied to claims and theories that cannot in principle be contradicted by any possible evidence. Used in philosophy of science and critical discourse to indicate that a claim falls outside the scope of scientific testing.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.11**
- **Form:** *false alarm*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *false* + *alarm* (warning signal)
- **Natural English translation:** a warning that turns out to be unfounded
- **Context of use:** Applied to warnings, alerts, and fears that prove to be without basis in fact. Used across all registers, from emergency services to everyday speech.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.12**
- **Form:** *false start*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *false* + *start* (beginning)
- **Natural English translation:** an unsuccessful or premature beginning; in athletics, a start made before the signal
- **Context of use:** Used in athletics to refer to a start made before the official signal, and more broadly to any unsuccessful or premature beginning of an endeavor. Common in everyday and journalistic usage.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.13**
- **Form:** *false pretenses*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *false* + *pretenses* (claims, representations)
- **Natural English translation:** fraudulent misrepresentations used to obtain something
- **Context of use:** A legal term referring to the use of deliberate misrepresentations to obtain money, property, or other benefits. Used in criminal law and in everyday speech to describe deceptive behavior (*obtained under false pretenses*).
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.14**
- **Form:** *false impression*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *false* + *impression* (belief formed in the mind)
- **Natural English translation:** an incorrect or misleading belief created in someone's mind
- **Context of use:** Used in legal, moral, and everyday contexts to describe a mistaken belief that has been created, often deliberately, by misleading statements or actions.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.15**
- **Form:** *false witness*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *false* + *witness* (testimony, one who testifies)
- **Natural English translation:** false testimony, perjury; one who gives false testimony
- **Context of use:** Used in legal and religious contexts to refer to dishonest testimony or to a person who gives such testimony. The prohibition against bearing *false witness* is a foundational moral and legal principle in many traditions.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.16**
- **Form:** *bear false witness*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *bear* (to give, to carry) + *false* + *witness*
- **Natural English translation:** to give false testimony, to lie under oath or in a formal context
- **Context of use:** A fixed phrase derived from the biblical commandment, used in legal and moral discourse to describe the act of giving dishonest testimony. Also used more broadly to mean to make false accusations.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.17**
- **Form:** *false flag*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *false* + *flag* (identifying symbol)
- **Natural English translation:** a covert operation designed to appear as if carried out by a different party; an act of deliberate misattribution
- **Context of use:** Originally a military and intelligence term for operations conducted under a false identity. Now widely used in political and journalistic discourse to describe alleged acts of deliberate misattribution or staged events.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.18**
- **Form:** *false economy*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *false* + *economy* (saving, thrift)
- **Natural English translation:** an apparent saving that results in greater expense or loss
- **Context of use:** Used in everyday and business discourse to describe a decision that appears to save money or resources but actually results in greater cost or harm in the long run.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.19**
- **Form:** *false dichotomy*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *false* + *dichotomy* (division into two)
- **Natural English translation:** a logical fallacy presenting only two options when more exist
- **Context of use:** A term in logic and rhetoric referring to the fallacy of presenting a situation as having only two mutually exclusive options when in fact other possibilities exist. Used in academic, journalistic, and everyday argumentation.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.20**
- **Form:** *false equivalence*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *false* + *equivalence* (equality, correspondence)
- **Natural English translation:** the fallacy of treating two things as equivalent when they are not
- **Context of use:** Used in logic, journalism, and political discourse to describe the rhetorical error of treating two significantly different things as if they were equally valid or comparable.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.21**
- **Form:** *false positive*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *false* + *positive* (indicating presence of a condition)
- **Natural English translation:** a test result incorrectly indicating the presence of a condition
- **Context of use:** A technical term in statistics, medicine, and testing to describe a result that incorrectly indicates a positive finding when the condition is actually absent. Contrasted with *false negative*.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.22**
- **Form:** *false negative*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *false* + *negative* (indicating absence of a condition)
- **Natural English translation:** a test result incorrectly indicating the absence of a condition
- **Context of use:** A technical term in statistics, medicine, and testing to describe a result that incorrectly indicates a negative finding when the condition is actually present. Contrasted with *false positive*.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.23**
- **Form:** *fallacy*
- **Morphological breakdown:** Latin *fallacia* (deception, trick), from *fallere* (to deceive); the same root as *falsus*
- **Natural English translation:** a mistaken belief; an error in reasoning
- **Context of use:** Used in logic and rhetoric to refer to a flaw in an argument that renders it invalid, and in everyday speech to refer to a widely held but mistaken belief. Productive: *fallacious*, *fallaciously*, *fallaciousness*.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.24**
- **Form:** *fallacious*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *fallacy* + *-ious* (adjectival suffix)
- **Natural English translation:** based on a fallacy, logically unsound, misleading
- **Context of use:** Applied to arguments, reasoning, and beliefs to indicate that they are based on faulty logic or false premises. Used in academic, journalistic, and formal discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.25**
- **Form:** *fallible*
- **Morphological breakdown:** Medieval Latin *fallibilis*, from *fallere* (to deceive, to err)
- **Natural English translation:** capable of making mistakes, liable to error
- **Context of use:** Applied to persons, systems, and methods to indicate that they are capable of being wrong or making errors. Contrasted with *infallible*. Used in philosophical, religious, and everyday contexts. Productive: *fallibility*, *infallible*, *infallibility*.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.26**
- **Form:** *fallibility*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *fallible* + *-ity*
- **Natural English translation:** the capacity to make mistakes, liability to error
- **Context of use:** Used in epistemology, theology, and everyday discourse to refer to the property of being capable of error. A key concept in fallibilist epistemology.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.27**
- **Form:** *infallible*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *in-* (negation) + *fallible*
- **Natural English translation:** incapable of error, unfailingly reliable
- **Context of use:** Applied to persons, methods, and institutions to indicate that they are incapable of making mistakes. Used in theological contexts (papal infallibility) and in everyday speech, often with ironic intent. Productive: *infallibility*, *infallibly*.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.28**
- **Form:** *infallibility*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *infallible* + *-ity*
- **Natural English translation:** the quality of being incapable of error
- **Context of use:** Used in theological contexts (particularly Roman Catholic doctrine of papal infallibility) and in philosophical and everyday discourse to refer to the property of being incapable of error.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.29**
- **Form:** *false consciousness*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *false* + *consciousness* (awareness, understanding)
- **Natural English translation:** a distorted understanding of one's social position or interests
- **Context of use:** A term from Marxist theory referring to a distorted or ideologically conditioned understanding of one's own social position and interests. Used in social theory, political philosophy, and critical discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.30**
- **Form:** *false memory*
- **Morphological breakdown:** *false* + *memory* (recollection)
- **Natural English translation:** a recollection of an event that did not occur or occurred differently
- **Context of use:** A term in cognitive psychology and legal discourse referring to a memory of an event that is factually inaccurate, either spontaneously generated or implanted through suggestion.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

> **Note:** The *falsus/fallere* root is highly productive in English. Additional attested forms include *false friend* (in linguistics), *false modesty*, *false step*, *false move*, *false note*, *false prophet*, *false idol*, *false hope*, *falsetto* (in music), *default* (from Old French *defaillir*, related to *fallere*), and *fail* (also from *fallere* via Old French). The forms listed above represent the 20–30 most common and analytically significant. The root's productivity extends across legal, scientific, logical, psychological, and everyday registers.

## Step 3 — Peripheral Extensions

The following expressions involve the three roots identified above but appear in contexts where the contribution of the root shifts away from its core truth-evaluation function. These are flagged without interpretation or categorization.

---

**From ROOT 1 (TRUE / TRĒOWE):**

- *true north* — used in navigation to refer to geographic direction, and in leadership discourse to refer to guiding values or purpose, without direct reference to propositional truth or falsity.
- *true-blue* — used to indicate loyalty and political commitment, where the primary sense is fidelity rather than factual accuracy.
- *truly* — used as an intensifier (*truly remarkable*, *truly extraordinary*) where the contribution is degree or emphasis rather than truth-evaluation.
- *trust fund* — a legal and financial instrument; the *trust* component refers to a fiduciary relationship rather than epistemic reliability.
- *entrust* — refers to the act of placing something in someone's care; the truth-evaluation dimension is absent.
- *come true* — refers to the realization of wishes or predictions; the sense is actualization rather than propositional truth.
- *true* in engineering and carpentry — used to describe a surface, edge, or alignment that is perfectly straight or level (*true the wheel*, *out of true*), where the sense is geometric accuracy rather than propositional truth.
- *moment of truth* — used to describe a decisive or climactic moment; the sense is revelation of quality or character rather than propositional truth-evaluation.
- *post-truth* — used to characterize a political and media environment; the sense involves the social and political status of truth rather than its propositional properties.

---

**From ROOT 2 (VERUS):**

- *verdict* — used in legal contexts to refer to a formal decision; the *ver-* component contributes a sense of authoritative declaration rather than direct truth-evaluation.
- *in vino veritas* — used to describe the social and psychological effects of alcohol on candor; the truth-domain sense is present but embedded in a social observation.
- *verisimilitude* — used in literary and aesthetic criticism to evaluate the convincingness of fictional representations; the sense is appearance of truth rather than truth itself.
- *veridical* — used in philosophy of perception and psychology to describe perceptual experiences; the sense involves the relationship between experience and reality rather than propositional truth.

---

**From ROOT 3 (FALSUS / FALLERE):**

- *falsetto* — a vocal register in music; the *fals-* component refers to an artificial or non-natural voice quality rather than propositional falsity.
- *false economy* — refers to a mistaken practical judgment about costs and benefits; the sense is practical error rather than propositional falsity.
- *false start* — used in athletics and everyday speech to refer to a premature or unsuccessful beginning; the sense is procedural incorrectness rather than propositional falsity.
- *fallible* / *infallible* — used in theological and everyday contexts to refer to the capacity for error in general, not specifically to propositional truth-evaluation.
- *fallacy* — in everyday usage, refers to a widely held mistaken belief; the logical sense (flaw in argument structure) is distinct from the propositional falsity sense.
- *false consciousness* — used in social theory to refer to ideological distortion of self-understanding; the sense involves systematic misrecognition rather than simple propositional falsity.
- *false memory* — used in psychology to refer to inaccurate recollection; the sense involves the reliability of cognitive processes rather than the truth-value of propositions.
- *false flag* — used in political and military discourse to refer to misattribution of agency; the sense involves identity and attribution rather than propositional truth.
- *false positive / false negative* — used in statistics and medicine to refer to errors in classification or detection; the sense is technical and procedural rather than directly propositional.

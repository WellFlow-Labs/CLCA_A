| **Inchoative derivation ("become true/good/correct")** | A morpheme derives a verb meaning "to become" a truth-domain state | zh, he, sa, el, en, tl, sw, qu | zh: 变真 (biàn zhēn, become true), 变得可靠 (become reliable); he: hitgalah (be revealed), hitbarar (become clear); sa: satyaṃ bhavati (become true, √bhū); el: γίνεται σωστό (becomes correct, γίνομαι); en: become true, turn out to be true, prove true; tl: magkatotoo (mag-ka- inchoative: come true), allinyay (Quechua parallel); sw: kuwa kweli (become true, kuwa + predicate); qu: chiqaqyay (become true, -ya- inchoative), allinyay (become good, -ya- inchoative) | **Widely attested** — all 8 languages. ## 2. Narrative Analysis

### Patterns Recurring Across Unrelated Language Families

**Abstract nominalization is universal.** Every language in the corpus derives abstract nouns from truth-domain bases, and these abstract nouns are used in formal, philosophical, and analytical discourse. The morphological strategies differ substantially — Chinese uses suffixes (-性, -度), Hebrew uses -ut, Sanskrit uses -tā/-tva, Greek uses -ότητα/-εια, English uses -ity/-ness/-cy/-th, Tagalog uses the ka-...-an circumfix, Swahili uses the u- prefix, and Quechua uses the infinitive as a nominal — but the functional pattern is consistent across all eight languages and across unrelated language families (Sino-Tibetan, Semitic, Indo-European, Austronesian, Bantu, Quechuan). This is the most robustly cross-linguistic pattern in the corpus.

**Manner adverbials from truth-domain terms are universal.** All eight languages convert truth-domain terms into manner adverbials, and these adverbials are among the most frequent expressions in each language. The strategies again differ: Chinese uses the particle 地, Hebrew and Swahili use prepositional phrases (be-, kwa-), Sanskrit and Quechua use case suffixes (instrumental, ablative, accusative), Greek and English use adverbial suffixes (-ώς, -ly), and Tagalog uses the accusative case marker -ta. Despite the typological diversity, the functional outcome is the same: truth-domain terms are productively converted to manner adverbials across all language families.

**Causative derivation for verification is universal; for falsification it is not.** All eight languages show productive causative verbs for establishing truth, verifying, or authenticating. The morphological strategies differ (Chinese resultative compounds, Hebrew -ώνω, Swahili -ish-, Tagalog pa-...-an, Quechua -ncha-), but the pattern is consistent. By contrast, causative verbs for making something false, falsifying, or invalidating are clearly attested in only 3–4 languages (Greek ακυρώνω, English falsify/invalidate, Swahili batilisha). Quechua explicitly notes the absence of a dedicated "make false" causative as a structural gap. This asymmetry — productive causatives for establishing truth but not for establishing falsity — is one of the most consistent cross-linguistic patterns in the corpus and is attested across unrelated language families (Indo-European, Bantu, Quechuan).

**Negative agentives (liars, deceivers) are more elaborated than positive agentives (truth-tellers).** Across at least six of eight languages, the vocabulary for persons characterized by lying or deception is more lexically elaborated than the vocabulary for persons characterized by truthfulness. In Greek, ψεύτης has diminutive, augmentative, and feminine forms; no parallel elaboration exists for a truth-teller agentive. In English, "liar" is a simple lexical item while "truth-teller" requires compounding. In Hebrew, shakran (liar) is common while no parallel positive agent is noted. In Tagalog, sinungaling (liar) is a simple lexical item while no parallel simple term for "truth-teller" is noted. In Swahili, mdanganyifu (deceiver) is attested while mtu wa kweli (person of truth) is periphrastic. In Quechua, llullaq (liar) is a simple agentive while chiqanchaq (verifier) is more specialized and less common. This asymmetry is attested across Semitic, Indo-European, Austronesian, Bantu, and Quechuan families.

**Discourse markers grammaticalized from truth vocabulary appear in seven of eight languages.** Chinese (说实话, 实际上), Hebrew (האמת היא ש...), Greek (στην αλήθεια), English (to be honest, truth be told), Tagalog (sa totoo lang), Swahili (kwa kweli, haki ni kwamba), and Quechua (chiqaqmanta rimay) all show truth-domain vocabulary grammaticalized into discourse markers that introduce frank statements, corrections, or emphatic affirmations. Sanskrit shows less of this pattern. The grammaticalization of truth vocabulary into discourse markers appears across Sino-Tibetan, Semitic, Indo-European, Austronesian, Bantu, and Quechuan families.

### Striking Asymmetries

**The positive/negative inchoative asymmetry.** Inchoative constructions for "becoming true" or "being verified" are more natural and more lexicalized than parallel constructions for "becoming false." Quechua explicitly notes the absence of *llullayay (become false) while chiqaqyay (become true) is attested. Hebrew F6 notes that "the thing became true" constructions are marginal while revelation verbs (hitgalah, hitbarar) are preferred. Greek F6 notes that γίνομαι (become) is unnatural with ψέμα (lie) while natural with σωστός (correct). This asymmetry is documented in at least four languages across unrelated families and may reflect a broader conceptual pattern where truth is construed as something that is revealed or established rather than something that comes into being, while falsity is construed as a state that is exposed or demonstrated rather than something that develops.

**The positive/negative causative asymmetry.** As noted above, causatives for establishing truth are productive across all eight languages while causatives for establishing falsity are absent or marginal in at least five. This is not a typological accident: it appears across Sino-Tibetan, Semitic, Indo-European, Austronesian, Bantu, and Quechuan families. The asymmetry suggests that the conceptual frame of "causing something to be true" (verification, authentication, validation) is more grammatically elaborated than "causing something to be false" (falsification) across diverse language families.

**The positive/negative agentive asymmetry.** As noted above, negative agentives (liars, deceivers) are consistently more elaborated than positive agentives (truth-tellers) across at least six languages. This asymmetry is attested across unrelated language families and is one of the most consistent patterns in the corpus.

**The Chinese 假 morpheme uniquely merges three functions.** The morpheme 假 (jiǎ) covers deliberate falsehood (假话 = lies), material inauthenticity (假冒 = counterfeit), and hypothetical framing (假如 = if, 假设 = suppose). No other language in the corpus shows this three-way merger in a single morpheme. In all other languages, hypothetical framing uses vocabulary that is not lexically connected to falsity terms.

**Swahili haki uniquely merges truth and rights/justice.** The term haki covers both "truth" (haki ni kwamba... = "the truth is that...") and "rights/justice" (haki za binadamu = "human rights"). This merger is unique in the corpus. In all other languages, truth and rights/justice are covered by distinct lexical items.

### One-Way or Blocked Derivational Pathways

**Blocked: "become false" inchoative.** In at least four languages (Quechua, Hebrew, Greek, and implied in others), the inchoative pathway from "false" to "become false" is blocked or marginal while the parallel pathway from "true" to "become true" is productive. This is a one-way pathway: the inchoative is available for positive truth-domain states but not (or less readily) for negative ones.

**Blocked: "make false" causative.** In at least five languages (Quechua, Tagalog, Swahili, Hebrew, and partially Chinese), the causative pathway from "false" to "make false" is blocked or expressed only periphrastically, while the parallel pathway from "true" to "make true/verify" is productive. This is a one-way pathway: causative derivation is available for establishing truth but not (or less readily) for establishing falsity.

**Blocked: positive agentive elaboration.** In at least six languages, the agentive derivation pathway is more productive for negative agents (liars, deceivers) than for positive agents (truth-tellers, honest persons). The positive agentive pathway is not entirely blocked — forms like chiqanchaq (Quechua verifier), yachachiq (Quechua teacher), and mwenye kweli (Swahili truthful person) exist — but it is consistently less elaborated, less colloquial, and more likely to require periphrastic expression.

**One-way: Greek ψευδο- prefix.** The ψευδο- prefix in Greek generates compounds across scientific, medical, legal, religious, and everyday domains, but there is no parallel productive prefix for "true/genuine" that generates a comparable range of compounds. The ορθο- prefix covers some of this space (correct/straight) but does not directly parallel ψευδο- as a truth-domain prefix. This is a one-way elaboration: the false/pseudo- domain is more productively compounded than the true/genuine domain.

**One-way: Sanskrit sat- compounding.** The sat- root generates an extensive vocabulary for positive moral qualities (satpuruṣa, sajjana, sadguṇa, satsaṅga, satkarma, satpatha, sadvṛtta, sadvacana) but the parallel asat- compounds are fewer and less elaborated. This is a one-way elaboration: the positive moral quality domain is more productively compounded than the negative.

### Productive vs. Restricted vs. Lexicalized/Fossilized Patterns

**Productive patterns:**
- Abstract nominalization via suffix/prefix/circumfix (all 8 languages, freely applicable to new bases)
- Manner adverbial formation (all 8 languages, freely applicable)
- Causative derivation for verification (all 8 languages, productive with verification roots)
- Negative prefix on truth-domain adjectives (all 8 languages, productive; most systematic in Sanskrit with a-)
- Quechua -ya- inchoative (productive across the entire truth domain: allinyay, chiqaqyay, sut'iyay, hatunyay, sumaqyay, millayyay, qhapaqyay, wakchayay, k'uchiyay, hunt'ayay, ch'usaqyay)
- Quechua -chi- causative (productive across the truth domain: allinyachiy, chiqanchay, pantachiy, sut'iyachiy, hatunchay, huch'uyachiy, sumaqyachiy, millaychay, hunt'achiy, ch'usaqchay)
- Greek ψευδο- prefix compounding (productive across diverse domains)
- Tagalog mag-...-an reciprocal (productive for honesty and argumentation verbs)

**Restricted patterns:**
- Chinese AABB reduplication (productive but lexically restricted: not all truth-domain adjectives permit it; common with 真实, 老实, 实在, 认真 but not freely generative)
- Greek color-modified lie constructions (άσπρο/μαύρο/κόκκινο ψέμα: restricted to ψέμα; not extended to other truth-domain nouns)
- Quechua -sapa abundance suffix (productive but tends to appear in character descriptions and moral evaluations rather than everyday usage; marked as marginal in F2a)
- Hebrew -an agentive suffix (productive for negative agents: shakran, safkan; less productive for positive agents)
- Tagalog napaka- intensifier (productive but restricted to intensification contexts)
- Sanskrit -vādin agentive compounding (productive for speech-related agents: satyavādin, mṛṣāvādin, asatyavādin; restricted to this semantic domain)

**Lexicalized/fossilized patterns:**
- Chinese four-character expressions (chéngyǔ): fixed in form, not productively generative; each is a lexical item (千真万确, 名副其实, 实事求是, 弄虚作假)
- Hebrew fixed expressions (emet ve-yatsiv, amat midah, la-amito shel davar): lexicalized compounds or phrases, not productively generative
- Sanskrit Vedic ṛta compounds (ṛtajña, ṛtāvan, ṛtavradhna): archaic, not productive in classical Sanskrit; marked as marginal in F1
- English "false" compounds (false alarm, false start, false dawn, false economy, false flag): each has a specialized meaning not derivable from "false" alone; not productively generative as a class
- Greek αληθοφανής (apparently true), αληθοφάνεια (verisimilitude): attested but marked as marginal; not productively generative
- Swahili proverbs (ukweli ni uchungu, ukweli hauna miguu): fixed expressions, not productively generative; marked as marginal in F4

**Unclear or weakly attested patterns:**
- Quechua -nna continuative on truth-domain terms (chiqaqninnaq: still its truth): marked as marginal in F1; distribution unclear
- Quechua -cha diminutive on truth-domain verbs (chiqaqchay: make somewhat true, allinchay: make somewhat good): marked as marginal in F2a; limited attestation
- Sanskrit mithyā as adjective (*maithya): marked as uncertain in F2a; not clearly attested
- Tagalog pakatotoo (as true as possible): marked as marginal in F1; limited attestation
- Tagalog ikatotoo (to cause to be true): marked as marginal in F1; limited attestation
- Quechua pantarqo (to err suddenly, -rqo sudden action suffix): marked as marginal in F1; limited attestation in truth domain specifically

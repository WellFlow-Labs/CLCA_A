## Narrative Analysis

### Recurring partition patterns across languages

**The propositional truth / object genuineness split** is the most consistently documented partition across all eight languages. Every language maintains distinct vocabulary for evaluating whether a *statement* is true and whether an *object or person* is genuine, even when a single root participates in both domains. In Chinese, 真实 is the primary term for the factual accuracy of reports and accounts, while 真伪 frames the question of whether an object is genuine or counterfeit. In Hebrew, *ʾemet* is used for propositional truth while *mezuyaf* is the standard term for a forged document. In English, *true* and *accurate* evaluate statements while *genuine* and *authentic* evaluate objects and persons. In Tagalog, *totoo* is the primary predicate for factual truth while *tunay* is the primary predicate for genuineness of objects. In Swahili, *kweli* covers propositional truth while *halisi* covers genuineness of goods and documents. In Quechua, *cheqa* covers both domains but the collocations differ: *cheqam* as a standalone assertion evaluates a proposition, while *cheqa qelqa* evaluates a document as authentic. The split is present in all languages, though the degree of lexical separation varies: Sanskrit and Hebrew show the sharpest lexical differentiation, while Quechua and Tagalog show more overlap through a single root covering both domains.

**The correctness-relative-to-a-standard grouping** is present in all eight languages but is lexically separated from propositional truth to varying degrees. Chinese maintains a clear distinction between 真实 (correspondence to reality) and 正确 (conformity to a standard), with 准确 adding a precision dimension. Hebrew distinguishes *ʾemet* (truth) from *nakhon* (correct, right) and *meduyaq* (precise). English distinguishes *true* from *correct* and *accurate*, with *exact* adding a no-deviation dimension. Tagalog distinguishes *totoo* from *tama* (correct) and *wasto* (proper, conforming to standard), with *tumpak* adding a precision dimension. Swahili distinguishes *kweli* from *sahihi* (correct, accurate). Greek distinguishes *αληθής* from *ακριβής* (precise). Sanskrit distinguishes *satya* from *sādhu* (grammatically correct) and *yukta* (appropriate). Quechua distinguishes *cheqa* from *chiqan* (straight, correct) and *allin* (good, right). In all languages, the correctness grouping is present, but the degree of lexical elaboration varies: Chinese and Hebrew show the most explicit sub-distinctions within this grouping (e.g., zh 正确 vs. 准确; he *nakhon* vs. *meduyaq*).

**The reliability-of-persons-and-sources grouping** is present in all eight languages and is consistently distinct from both propositional truth and object genuineness. Chinese uses 可靠 and 诚实; Hebrew uses ʾamin, mehiman, neʾeman; Sanskrit uses pramāṇabhūta and related forms; Greek uses αξιόπιστος; English uses reliable, credible, trustworthy; Tagalog uses mapagkakatiwalaan and kredible; Swahili uses uaminifu, amini; Quechua uses iñinapaq. In all languages, this grouping is applied to persons, institutions, and information sources, and it is evaluated over time or across situations rather than for a single instance. The grouping consistently overlaps with the sincerity/honesty grouping (see below), but the two are maintained as distinct in the data for all eight languages.

**The sincerity and honesty of persons grouping** is present in all eight languages and is consistently distinguished from the reliability grouping by its focus on the alignment between inner states and outward expression. Chinese uses 诚实, 真诚, 真心; Hebrew uses kanut, yashar; Sanskrit uses satya in ethical contexts and satyavādin; Greek uses αληθινός in personal contexts; English uses sincere, honest, truthful; Tagalog uses tapat; Swahili uses uaminifu in its moral dimension; Quechua uses cheqa sonqo (true-hearted). The overlap between sincerity and reliability is most pronounced in Tagalog (where *tapat* covers both) and Hebrew (where *neʾeman* covers both faithfulness and reliability), and least pronounced in English (where *sincere* and *reliable* are clearly distinct).

**The deliberate falsification and fraud grouping** is present in all eight languages and is consistently distinguished from simple error or inaccuracy by the implication of intent. Chinese uses 伪造, 欺骗, 弄虚作假; Hebrew uses ziyyuf, mezuyaf; Sanskrit uses asatya in legal contexts and asatyavādin; Greek uses πλαστός, πλαστογραφία; English uses fraudulent, fabricated, counterfeit; Tagalog uses huwad, peke, linlang; Swahili uses ghushi, udanganyifu, kudanganya; Quechua uses llulla, llullakuy. In all languages, this grouping is associated with legal and moral condemnation and is distinguished from the error grouping (where intent is absent or not implied).

**The discourse-framing constructions for frank or corrective statements** are attested in all eight languages, suggesting a cross-linguistically robust pragmatic function. The forms vary: Chinese uses sentence-initial adverbials (实际上, 事实上); Hebrew uses a clausal frame (ha-ʾemet hi she-); Sanskrit uses *-taḥ* adverbials (vastutaḥ); Greek uses a clausal frame (η αλήθεια είναι ότι); English uses parenthetical phrases (to tell the truth, truth be told); Tagalog uses discourse-initial particles and frames (sa totoo lang, ang totoo); Swahili uses adverbial phrases (kwa kweli, ukweli ni kwamba); Quechua uses adverbial constructions (cheqaqta rimay). The function is the same across all languages: to signal that what follows is the speaker's genuine assessment of the actual state of affairs, often in contrast to a prior claim or expectation.

### Where languages split distinctions more finely

**Sanskrit splits the truth domain most finely** of any language in the corpus. It maintains distinct vocabulary for: propositional truth (*satya*), cosmic order (*ṛta*), ontological being (*sat*), valid cognition (*pramā*), the source of valid cognition (*pramāṇa*), the property of being a valid source (*prāmāṇya*), error (*bhrama*), illusion (*bhrānti*), grammatical correctness (*sādhu*), contextual appropriateness (*yukta*, *ucita*, *aucitya*), and levels of reality (*paramārtha*, *vyāvahārika*). No other language in the corpus maintains all of these as distinct lexical groupings. The *ābhāsa* element further marks pseudo-instances within multiple sub-domains (*pramāṇābhāsa*, *hetvābhāsa*), creating a systematic cross-cutting distinction not paralleled elsewhere.

**Hebrew splits the reliability dimension more finely than most languages**, maintaining distinct terms for: general reliability (*ʾamin*), formal legal credibility (*mehiman*), personal faithfulness (*neʾeman*), and the abstract property of trustworthiness (*ʾaminut*, *mehimanut*, *neʾemanut*). The *kasher/pasul* pair further distinguishes ritual fitness from general validity, a distinction not lexicalized in most other languages.

**Chinese splits the correctness dimension more finely than most languages**, maintaining distinct terms for: general correctness (*正确*), precision of fit to a target (*准确*), and the property of being exact (*精确*, implied). The 实 family further distinguishes factual reality (*事实*), actual conditions (*实际*), and practical grounding (*务实*) in ways that are not paralleled in most other languages.

**Quechua splits the epistemic-source dimension more finely than any other language** through its grammaticalized evidentiality system, which distinguishes direct knowledge (*-m/-mi*), reported information (*-s/-si*), and conjecture (*-chá*) as obligatory grammatical categories. This distinction is expressed lexically or constructionally in other languages (e.g., en *reportedly*, *allegedly*; sw *inasemekana*) but is not grammaticalized as an obligatory category.

### Where languages merge distinctions found elsewhere

**Tagalog merges the honesty and loyalty dimensions** in the single root *tapat* and its derivative *katapatan*, which covers both honest speech and faithful commitment to persons. In most other languages, these are distinct lexical groupings: English distinguishes *honest* (speech) from *faithful* (commitment); Hebrew distinguishes *yashar* (upright in speech) from *neʾeman* (faithful to a person); Chinese distinguishes 诚实 (honest) from 忠诚 (loyal). The merger in Tagalog is supported by the data, which shows *tapat* used in both contexts without apparent distinction.

**Swahili merges the religious permissibility and legal legitimacy dimensions** in the *halali/haramu* pair, which covers both Islamic dietary law and broader secular legitimacy. In most other languages, these are distinct: English distinguishes *lawful* (legal) from *permissible* (moral/religious); Chinese distinguishes 合法 (legal) from 道德 (moral). The merger in Swahili reflects the Arabic loanword stratum and the historical role of Islamic law in Swahili-speaking communities.

**Hebrew merges the ritual fitness and legal admissibility dimensions** in the *kasher/pasul* pair, which applies to both ritual objects (food, religious items) and legal persons (witnesses, parties). In most other languages, these are distinct: English distinguishes *valid* (legal) from *proper* (ritual); Chinese distinguishes 合法 (legal) from 合规 (compliant with rules). The merger in Hebrew reflects the historical integration of religious and civil law in the halakhic tradition.

**Quechua merges the truth and goodness dimensions** in the root *allin*, which covers both moral goodness and practical correctness. In most other languages, these are distinct: English distinguishes *good* (moral) from *correct* (epistemic); Chinese distinguishes 好 (good) from 正确 (correct). The merger in Quechua is supported by the data, which shows *allin* used in both moral evaluation of persons and correctness evaluation of statements and actions.

### Cases where a language appears to lack a clear lexical contrast found elsewhere

**The ontological dimension (what genuinely exists vs. what is unreal)** is most elaborately developed in Sanskrit, where *sat/asat*, *mithyā*, and the *paramārtha/vyāvahārika* distinction constitute a distinct semantic group. While all other languages have terms for "real" vs. "unreal" (zh 真实/虚假; he *ʾamiti/medumaʾ*; el *αληθινός/φαινομενικός*; en *real/illusory*; tl *tunay/huwad*; sw *halisi/bandia*; qu *cheqa/llulla*), none of them develops a multi-level ontological framework with named intermediate categories. The *mithyā* category (neither real nor unreal) and the *pratibhāsa* level (mere appearance) are specific to Sanskrit in the corpus.

**The verisimilitude dimension** (appearing true without necessarily being true) is explicitly lexicalized in Chinese (逼真, 真实感), Greek (αληθοφανής, αληθοφάνεια), English (verisimilitude, plausible), and to some extent Hebrew (ʾemunat shaʾv, blind faith). It is not documented as a distinct lexical grouping in Tagalog, Swahili, or Quechua in the available data, though the Tagalog *parang totoo* and *mukhang totoo* constructions cover some of the same semantic territory constructionally.

**The legal validity dimension** (whether a document, contract, or decision has legal force) is most explicitly developed in Hebrew (*taqef*, *batel*, *kasher*, *pasul*), Chinese (合法, 违法, 合法性), Greek (έγκυρος, άκυρος), and English (*valid*, *invalid*, *legitimate*). It is present but less elaborated in Swahili (*halali*, *uhalali*) and Tagalog (*lehitimo*). It is not documented as a distinct lexical grouping in Quechua or Sanskrit in the available data, though Sanskrit has *dharma* and related terms that cover some of the same territory in legal-religious contexts.

**The grammaticalized evidentiality dimension** is present only in Quechua. The distinction between direct knowledge, reported information, and conjecture is expressed through obligatory grammatical markers in Quechua but is absent as a grammatical category in all other languages in the corpus. Other languages express these distinctions lexically (en *reportedly*, *allegedly*, *I think*; sw *inasemekana*; tl *daw*) or through specific constructions, but not as an obligatory grammatical system.

**The cosmic order dimension** (*ṛta*, *dharma* in their cosmological senses) is specific to Sanskrit in the corpus. While other languages have terms for moral rightness (zh 道德; he *tsedeq*; en *right*; tl *tama*; sw *haki*; qu *allin*), none of them develops a cosmological framework in which truth and cosmic order are aspects of the same concept.

### Distinguishing well-attested cross-language patterns from single-language observations

**Well-attested across all eight languages:**
- The primary binary opposition between truth and falsehood, expressed through a lexical pair
- The split between propositional truth of statements and genuineness of objects
- Abstract nominalization of truth-domain adjectives using language-specific suffixes
- The agentive asymmetry (negative-pole agents more consistently lexicalized than positive-pole agents)
- Discourse-framing constructions for frank or corrective statements
- Causative constructions for verification and confirmation

**Well-attested across most languages (six or more):**
- The correctness-relative-to-a-standard grouping, distinct from propositional truth
- The reliability-of-persons-and-sources grouping
- The sincerity and honesty grouping
- The deliberate falsification and fraud grouping
- Proverbial expressions asserting that truth is painful or will eventually be revealed

**Attested in several languages (three to five):**
- The verisimilitude dimension (appearing true without being true): zh, el, en, he
- The legal validity dimension as a distinct lexical grouping: he, zh, el, en, sw
- The precision/exactness sub-distinction within correctness: zh, he, en, tl
- The *accurate* (tolerates margin) vs. *exact* (no deviation) distinction: zh, he, en

**Single-language or two-language observations:**
- The ontological dimension with named intermediate categories (*mithyā*, *pratibhāsa*): sa only
- The cosmic order dimension (*ṛta*, *dharma* in cosmological sense): sa only
- Grammaticalized evidentiality as an obligatory grammatical category: qu only
- The *loob* (inner self) constructions for moral evaluation: tl only
- The *aucitya* (contextual fittingness) as a named aesthetic-ethical category: sa only
- The *post-truth* compound as a documented lexical item: en, el
- The *piga uongo* vs. *sema ukweli* light-verb asymmetry: sw only
- The *-ncha-* vs. *-chi-* causative asymmetry between positive and negative roots: qu only
- The *ʾi-* prefix on nominalizations as a productive formal negation strategy: he only
- The *kasher/pasul* pair merging ritual fitness with legal admissibility: he only

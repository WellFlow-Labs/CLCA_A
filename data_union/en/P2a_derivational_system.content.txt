# CLCA Protocol v3.4 — P2a Derivational System
# Language: English (en)
# Layer: P2a_derivational_system

---

**Note on scope:** This step documents attested derivational morphology for each P1 base lexical item. Forms already fully documented in P1 or P1b are cross-referenced rather than re-described at length. Transliteration is not applicable for English (Latin script native). The "Literal Breakdown" field is provided where morphological analysis is informative. Derivations are listed as attested; paradigm gaps are preserved and noted explicitly.

---

## Item 1: *true*

---

**1.1**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Derived Form:** *truly*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *true* + *-ly* (adverbial suffix)
- **Natural English translation:** in a true manner; genuinely; really
- **Usage:** Functions as adverb of manner (*truly believe*), intensifier (*truly remarkable*), and epistolary closing (*yours truly*). Documented in P1b Root 2, item 1.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.2**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Derived Form:** *truth*
- **Literal Breakdown:** OE *trēowth*; *tru-* + *-th* (abstract noun suffix; also given as OE *trēowe* + *-þ*)
- **Natural English translation:** the property of being true; a true statement or fact; the quality or state of being true
- **Usage:** The primary nominalization of *true* and the central noun of the truth-evaluation domain. Independently listed as a P1 base form. Highly productive in further compounds (see base form *truth* below). Already documented in P1 (item 2) and P1b (1.2).
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.3**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Derived Form:** *trueness*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *true* + *-ness* (abstract noun suffix)
- **Natural English translation:** the quality of being true or accurate; exactness of alignment; fidelity
- **Usage:** Used in technical and engineering contexts (*trueness of a surface*, *trueness of a bearing surface must be checked*) and occasionally in abstract contexts. Less common than *truth* for propositional accuracy. Documented in P1b (1.6).
- **Epistemic status:** [MARGINAL]

---

**1.4**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Derived Form:** *truism*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *true* + *-ism*
- **Natural English translation:** a statement so obviously true as to be not worth stating; a platitude
- **Usage:** Documented in P1b Root 2, item 25. Carries a mildly dismissive connotation. Used in intellectual and general discourse to dismiss a claim as trivially obvious. "It is a truism that prevention is better than cure."
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.5**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Derived Form:** *untrue*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *un-* (negation) + *true*
- **Natural English translation:** not true; false; unfaithful
- **Usage:** Used in general speech as a somewhat softer alternative to *false*. Applied to statements that do not correspond to fact, and to persons who are unfaithful or disloyal, particularly in literary or older usage. Documented in P1b (1.4). "That statement is simply untrue."
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.6**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Derived Form:** *truer*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *true* + *-er* (comparative suffix)
- **Natural English translation:** more true, more accurate, more genuine
- **Usage:** The regular comparative form of *true*, used in comparative constructions (*no truer statement has ever been made*). Fully productive but unremarkable morphologically.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**1.7**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Derived Form:** *truest*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *true* + *-est* (superlative suffix)
- **Natural English translation:** most true, most accurate, most genuine
- **Usage:** The regular superlative form of *true*, used in superlative constructions (*the truest account available*). Fully productive but unremarkable morphologically.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

> **Note on *true*:** The comparative and superlative forms (*truer*, *truest*) are attested and grammatically regular but carry no special derivational interest beyond standard adjectival inflection. The most analytically significant derivations are *truth*, *truly*, *untrue*, *trueness*, and *truism*. The form *true* also functions as a verb in technical contexts (*to true a wheel*, *to true up a surface*), generating *truing* and *trued*, but these are peripheral to the truth-evaluation domain.

> **Gap noted:** No attested *\*truify*, *\*truable*, or *\*truation* attested.

## Item 2: *truth*

---

**2.1**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Derived Form:** *truthful*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *truth* + *-ful* (having the quality of)
- **Natural English translation:** disposed to tell the truth; reflecting the truth; habitually telling the truth
- **Usage:** Applied to persons (habitual disposition) and statements to indicate a disposition toward or instance of telling the truth. Contrasts with *untruthful* and *dishonest*. Common across all registers. Independently listed as P1 base form 25.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**2.2**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Derived Form:** *truthfully*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *truth* + *-ful* + *-ly*
- **Natural English translation:** in a truthful manner; honestly
- **Usage:** Adverbial form of *truthful*, used to indicate that a statement is made honestly or that an action is performed with honesty. Used in legal, journalistic, and everyday contexts. "I can truthfully say that..." / "Please answer the questions truthfully."
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**2.3**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Derived Form:** *truthfulness*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *truth* + *-ful* + *-ness*
- **Natural English translation:** the quality or habit of being truthful; honesty
- **Usage:** Refers to the habitual disposition to tell the truth, applied to persons and accounts. Used in moral, psychological, legal, and journalistic assessments of character and reliability. "The truthfulness of the account was not in dispute."
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**2.4**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Derived Form:** *untruth*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *un-* + *truth*
- **Natural English translation:** a false statement; a lie (often euphemistic); the condition of being false
- **Usage:** Documented in P1b Root 2, item 2. Refers to a statement that is not true, often with a softer connotation than *lie*, suggesting that the falsity may be partial or that the speaker is being diplomatic. Used as a softer alternative to *lie* in formal and political contexts. Documented in P1b (1.5).
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**2.5**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Derived Form:** *untruthful*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *un-* + *truth* + *-ful*
- **Natural English translation:** not truthful; given to telling falsehoods; dishonest; false
- **Usage:** Documented in P1b Root 2, item 3. Applied to persons and statements to indicate a failure or refusal to tell the truth. Softer in connotation than *lying* but stronger than *inaccurate*. "The report was found to be untruthful in several key respects."
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**2.6**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Derived Form:** *untruthfully*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *un-* + *truth* + *-ful* + *-ly*
- **Natural English translation:** in an untruthful manner; dishonestly
- **Usage:** Adverbial form of *untruthful*, used to characterize statements or actions as dishonest. Less common than *falsely* or *dishonestly* but attested in formal and journalistic usage. "He testified untruthfully about his whereabouts."
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**2.7**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Derived Form:** *untruthfulness*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *un-* + *truth* + *-ful* + *-ness*
- **Natural English translation:** the quality of being untruthful; habitual dishonesty in speech
- **Usage:** Documented in P1b Root 2, item 4. Refers to the disposition or practice of not telling the truth. Used in moral, psychological, and formal evaluative registers. Less common than *untruthful*. "The untruthfulness of the testimony was established by cross-examination."
- **Epistemic status:** [MARGINAL]

---

**2.8**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Derived Form:** *truthiness*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *truth* + *-i-* (linking vowel) + *-ness*
- **Natural English translation:** the quality of seeming or feeling true without necessarily being true; truth asserted by intuition or conviction rather than evidence
- **Usage:** Coined and popularized by Stephen Colbert (2005); subsequently adopted in journalistic and academic discourse on post-truth phenomena. Distinct from *truthfulness* in that it describes a subjective sense of truth rather than actual correspondence to fact.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE] (within contemporary media and political discourse)

---

> **Note on *truth*:** The derivational family of *truth* is moderately productive. The *truthful / untruthful* branch is the most active. The form *truthy* (meaning having the superficial appearance of truth, or in informal usage, seeming true) is attested in contemporary informal and media discourse but remains marginal in formal registers; it is not listed here as a standard derivation. The compound forms (*truth-telling*, *truth-seeker*, *truth value*, *truth table*, *truth condition*, *truth claim*, *ground truth*, *home truth*, *half-truth*, *post-truth*) are documented in P1b and are not repeated here.

> **Gap noted:** No attested *\*truther* as a neutral agent noun; *truther* exists in informal usage but with specific conspiratorial connotations (e.g., *9/11 truther*) rather than as a general derivation meaning "one who tells the truth" (for which *truth-teller* is used instead). This asymmetry is preserved.

## Item 3: *accurate*

---

**3.1**
- **Base Form:** *accurate*
- **Derived Form:** *accurately*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *accurate* + *-ly*
- **Natural English translation:** in an accurate manner; precisely; correctly; without error
- **Usage:** Adverbial form, used to indicate that something is done or stated with close correspondence to fact or reality. Used in general and technical registers. "The instrument measures temperature accurately to within 0.1 degrees." (*accurately described*, *accurately measured*)
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.2**
- **Base Form:** *accurate*
- **Derived Form:** *accuracy*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *accurate* + *-cy* (abstract noun suffix, via *-cy* from Latin *-tia*)
- **Natural English translation:** the quality of being accurate; precision; correctness; closeness of a measurement or description to the true value
- **Usage:** Refers to the degree of correspondence between a measurement, description, or representation and the actual state of affairs. Used in scientific, technical, journalistic, and everyday contexts. "The accuracy of the report was questioned."
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.3**
- **Base Form:** *accurate*
- **Derived Form:** *inaccurate*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *in-* (negation) + *accurate*
- **Natural English translation:** not accurate; incorrect; imprecise; containing errors
- **Usage:** Applied to measurements, descriptions, and representations to indicate that they do not closely correspond to fact or reality. Used in general and technical registers. "The map was found to be inaccurate in several respects."
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.4**
- **Base Form:** *accurate*
- **Derived Form:** *inaccurately*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *in-* + *accurate* + *-ly*
- **Natural English translation:** in an inaccurate manner; incorrectly; imprecisely; with errors
- **Usage:** Adverbial form of *inaccurate*, used to characterize statements or representations as failing to correspond to fact. Used in general registers. (*inaccurately reported*, *inaccurately described*, *the figures were inaccurately reported*)
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.5**
- **Base Form:** *accurate*
- **Derived Form:** *inaccuracy*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *in-* + *accurate* + *-cy*
- **Natural English translation:** the quality of being inaccurate; a specific error or imprecision
- **Usage:** Used both as an abstract noun referring to the property of being inaccurate and as a count noun referring to a specific error or imprecision in a text, measurement, or account. Used in general and technical registers, often in the plural. "The report contained several factual inaccuracies."
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

> **Note on *accurate*:** The derivational family is compact and regular. No agent noun (*\*accurator*) or verbal form (*\*accurize* is attested only in highly specialized firearms contexts and is not considered a standard derivation here) is in common use. The family is essentially limited to the adjective, its adverb, its abstract noun, and their negated counterparts.

> **Gap noted:** No attested *\*accurateness* in common use; *accuracy* serves the nominalization function. No *\*overaccurate* or *\*underaccurate* attested as standard forms.

## Item 4: *genuine*

---

**4.1**
- **Base Form:** *genuine*
- **Derived Form:** *genuinely*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *genuine* + *-ly*
- **Natural English translation:** in a genuine manner; sincerely; really
- **Usage:** Adverbial form, used to affirm that something is real, sincere, or authentic. Used as an adverb of manner and as an intensifier. Common across all registers, including as an intensifier. (*genuinely surprised*, *genuinely concerned*)
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**4.2**
- **Base Form:** *genuine*
- **Derived Form:** *genuineness*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *genuine* + *-ness*
- **Natural English translation:** the quality of being genuine; authenticity; sincerity
- **Usage:** Refers to the property of being real, authentic, or sincerely felt. Used in evaluative contexts. Less common than *authenticity* or *sincerity* in formal contexts but fully attested. "The genuineness of the document was disputed." / "The genuineness of her emotion."
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**4.3**
- **Base Form:** *genuine*
- **Derived Form:** *ingenuine*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *in-* + *genuine*
- **Natural English translation:** not genuine; insincere
- **Usage:** Attested but rare; *ingenuine* appears in some philosophical and literary texts but is not the standard negation. *Not genuine* or *disingenuous* are preferred in most contexts. Included as attested but low-frequency.
- **Epistemic status:** [UNCERTAIN]

---

**4.4**
- **Base Form:** *genuine*
- **Derived Form:** *disingenuine*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *dis-* (negation/reversal) + *in-* + *genuine*
- **Natural English translation:** not genuine; insincere
- **Usage:** Occasionally attested as a back-formation or blend from *disingenuous*, but not a standard or widely accepted derivation. Included here with caution; *disingenuous* (see below) is the standard form.
- **Epistemic status:** [UNCERTAIN]

---

**4.5**
- **Base Form:** *genuine* (via related form *ingenuous*)
- **Derived Form:** *disingenuous*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *dis-* (negation) + *in-* (into, within) + Latin *genuinus* (native, natural)
- **Natural English translation:** not candid or sincere; giving a false impression of frankness
- **Usage:** Applied to persons and statements to indicate a lack of candor or an appearance of openness that conceals a hidden agenda. Semantically adjacent to *genuine* but derived through *ingenuous* rather than directly from *genuine*. Common in formal, journalistic, and political discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**4.6**
- **Base Form:** *genuine* (via *disingenuous*)
- **Derived Form:** *disingenuously*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *disingenuous* + *-ly*
- **Natural English translation:** in a disingenuous manner; with false candor
- **Usage:** Adverbial form of *disingenuous*, used to characterize statements or behavior as lacking genuine candor. (*disingenuously claimed*, *disingenuously presented*)
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**4.7**
- **Base Form:** *genuine* (via *disingenuous*)
- **Derived Form:** *disingenuousness*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *disingenuous* + *-ness*
- **Natural English translation:** the quality of being disingenuous; lack of candor
- **Usage:** Refers to the property of appearing candid while concealing a hidden motive or agenda. Used in moral and political assessments.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

> **Note on *genuine*:** The standard negation in this family is *disingenuous* (see above), which is not a direct derivation from *genuine* but from the related adjective *ingenuous*. There is no standard *\*ingenuine* or *\*ungenuine* in common use. The derivational family of *genuine* itself is therefore limited primarily to *genuinely* and *genuineness*. No common nominalization *genuinity* is attested in standard use. *Genuineness* is the standard abstract noun.

> **Gap noted:** The standard negation of *genuine* in most contexts is the phrase *not genuine* or the semantically adjacent *disingenuous* (which derives from *ingenuous*, not directly from *genuine*). *\*Ungenuine* is occasionally encountered but is not standard. This asymmetry is preserved.

## Item 5: *authentic*

---

**5.1**
- **Base Form:** *authentic*
- **Derived Form:** *authentically*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *authentic* + *-ally* (adverbial suffix for *-ic* adjectives)
- **Natural English translation:** in an authentic manner; genuinely; originally
- **Usage:** Adverbial form, used to indicate that something is done or presented in a manner that is genuine or true to its original character. Used in aesthetic, philosophical, and everyday contexts. (*authentically reproduced*, *authentically expressed*) "She spoke authentically about her experience."
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**5.2**
- **Base Form:** *authentic*
- **Derived Form:** *authenticity*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *authentic* + *-ity*
- **Natural English translation:** the quality of being authentic; genuineness; originality; the property of being true to one's own values or origins
- **Usage:** Refers to the property of being genuine, original, or true to one's own character. Used in legal, aesthetic, philosophical, and everyday contexts. One of the most common nominalizations in this semantic domain. "The authenticity of the document was confirmed by experts." / "The authenticity of the painting."
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**5.3**
- **Base Form:** *authentic*
- **Derived Form:** *authenticate*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *authentic* + *-ate* (verbal suffix)
- **Natural English translation:** to establish as genuine; to verify the authenticity of; to confirm the authenticity of something
- **Usage:** Used in legal, archival, art-historical, digital, and everyday contexts to describe the act of confirming that something is genuine or that a person is who they claim to be. (*authenticate a document*, *authenticate a user*) "The signature was authenticated by a handwriting expert."
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**5.4**
- **Base Form:** *authentic*
- **Derived Form:** *authentication*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *authenticate* + *-ion*
- **Natural English translation:** the process of establishing genuineness or identity; in computing, the process of verifying identity
- **Usage:** Refers to the process or act of verifying that something is genuine or that a person or system is what it claims to be. Used in archival, legal, and computing contexts. Extremely common in digital security and legal contexts. (*two-factor authentication*, *document authentication*, *authentication of a signature*)
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**5.5**
- **Base Form:** *authentic*
- **Derived Form:** *authenticator*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *authenticate* + *-or* (agent suffix)
- **Natural English translation:** a person or device that authenticates; in computing, a tool or application used for authentication
- **Usage:** Refers to a person who verifies authenticity, or more commonly in contemporary usage, a software application or hardware device used to verify identity. Used primarily in computing and security contexts; also used in archival and legal contexts for a person who certifies authenticity. (*authenticator app*) "The authenticator app generates a one-time code."
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**5.6**
- **Base Form:** *authentic*
- **Derived Form:** *inauthenticity*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *in-* + *authentic* + *-ity*
- **Natural English translation:** the quality of being inauthentic; lack of genuineness; lack of correspondence to one's true values
- **Usage:** Refers to the property of being not genuine, false, or not true to one's own character. Used in philosophical (especially existentialist), psychological, and cultural criticism contexts. "Sartre's analysis of bad faith is an analysis of inauthenticity." / "The inauthenticity of the performance."
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**5.7**
- **Base Form:** *authentic*
- **Derived Form:** *inauthentic*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *in-* + *authentic*
- **Natural English translation:** not authentic; not genuine; false; lacking genuineness
- **Usage:** Applied to objects, persons, and experiences to indicate that they are not genuine or not true to their claimed origin or character. Used in aesthetic, philosophical, and everyday contexts. "The performance felt inauthentic and rehearsed." / "An inauthentic reproduction." / "An inauthentic self."
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**5.8**
- **Base Form:** *authentic*
- **Derived Form:** *inauthentically*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *in-* + *authentic* + *-ally*
- **Natural English translation:** in an inauthentic manner
- **Usage:** Adverbial form of *inauthentic*, used to characterize behavior or presentation as lacking genuine character. Used in philosophical and critical contexts; less frequent than *inauthenticity* but attested.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**5.9**
- **Base Form:** *authentic*
- **Derived Form:** *unauthenticated*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *un-* + *authenticated*
- **Natural English translation:** not authenticated; not verified as genuine
- **Usage:** Used in legal, archival, and computing contexts. (*an unauthenticated document*, *an unauthenticated user*)
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

## Item 6: *valid*

---

**6.1**
- **Base Form:** *valid*
- **Derived Form:** *validly*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *valid* + *-ly*
- **Natural English translation:** in a valid manner; with logical or legal force
- **Usage:** Adverbial form, used to indicate that something is done or argued in a manner that is logically sound or legally effective. Used in formal, logical, and legal registers. Less common than *validity* or *valid*. (*validly argued*, *validly executed*) "The conclusion follows validly from the premises."
- **Epistemic status:** [MARGINAL]

---

**6.2**
- **Base Form:** *valid*
- **Derived Form:** *validity*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *valid* + *-ity*
- **Natural English translation:** the quality of being valid; logical soundness; legal force; in logic, the property of an argument whose conclusion follows necessarily from its premises
- **Usage:** Refers to the property of being logically sound (in argumentation) or legally binding (in legal instruments). Also used in psychometrics to refer to the degree to which a test measures what it purports to measure. Used in logical, legal, scientific, and general registers. "The validity of the argument was disputed."
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**6.3**
- **Base Form:** *valid*
- **Derived Form:** *validate*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *valid* + *-ate* (verbal suffix)
- **Natural English translation:** to confirm as valid; to give official approval to; to verify; to recognize or affirm the legitimacy of something
- **Usage:** Used in legal, administrative, scientific, technical, and psychological contexts to indicate the act of confirming that something meets required standards or is logically or legally sound. Also used informally to mean to affirm someone's feelings or experiences. (*to validate someone's concerns*, *validate a claim*, *validate a parking ticket*, *validate someone's feelings*) "The results were validated by an independent team."
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**6.4**
- **Base Form:** *valid*
- **Derived Form:** *validation*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *validate* + *-ion*
- **Natural English translation:** the process of validating; official confirmation; verification; confirmation of validity
- **Usage:** Refers to the process of confirming that something is valid, accurate, or meets required standards. Used in scientific, administrative, psychological, and everyday contexts. Also used in the informal psychological sense of receiving affirmation of one's feelings. (*validation of a test instrument*) "Seeking external validation is a common human tendency."
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**6.5**
- **Base Form:** *valid*
- **Derived Form:** *validator*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *validate* + *-or*
- **Natural English translation:** a person or tool that validates; in computing, a program that checks conformity to a standard
- **Usage:** Refers to a person who validates, or more commonly in technical contexts, a software tool or system that checks whether data or code meets specified standards. Used primarily in computing and technical contexts. (*HTML validator*, *XML validator*, *an HTML validator*, *a ticket validator*) "Run the HTML through a validator before publishing."
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**6.6**
- **Base Form:** *valid*
- **Derived Form:** *invalid* (adjective)
- **Literal Breakdown:** *in-* (negation) + *valid*
- **Natural English translation:** not valid; logically unsound; legally ineffective; lacking legal force or logical soundness
- **Usage:** Applied to arguments, legal instruments, and claims to indicate that they lack logical force or legal effect. Used in legal, logical, and general registers. Note: homophonous with *invalid* (noun/adjective meaning a person with a disability or illness), which derives from the same Latin root but through a different semantic path; the two forms have different stress patterns. "The contract was declared invalid."
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**6.7**
- **Base Form:** *valid*
- **Derived Form:** *invalidity*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *in-* + *valid* + *-ity*
- **Natural English translation:** the quality of being invalid; lack of legal or logical force
- **Usage:** Refers to the property of being logically unsound or legally ineffective. Used in legal, logical, and formal contexts. (*the invalidity of the contract*, *the invalidity of the inference*)
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**6.8**
- **Base Form:** *valid*
- **Derived Form:** *invalidate*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *in-* + *valid* + *-ate*
- **Natural English translation:** to make invalid; to deprive of legal or logical force
- **Usage:** Used in legal, scientific, and everyday contexts to indicate the act of rendering something without force or effect. (*invalidate a contract*, *invalidate an argument*, *invalidate a test result*, *invalidate a hypothesis*, *invalidate an election*) "New evidence invalidated the earlier findings."
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**6.9**
- **Base Form:** *valid*
- **Derived Form:** *invalidation*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *invalidate* + *-ion*
- **Natural English translation:** the act of invalidating; the process of rendering something without force
- **Usage:** Refers to the process of making something legally or logically void. Also used in psychological discourse to refer to the dismissal or denial of another person's feelings or experiences. Used in legal and psychological contexts. (*invalidation of the election results*, *emotional invalidation*) "The invalidation of the election results was contested."
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

## Item 7: *correct*

---

**7.1**
- **Base Form:** *correct*
- **Derived Form:** *correctly*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *correct* + *-ly*
- **Natural English translation:** in a correct manner; accurately; properly; without error; in conformity with a standard
- **Usage:** Adverbial form, used to indicate that something is done or stated in conformity with fact, rule, or standard. Common across all registers. (*correctly identified*, *correctly performed*, *correctly stated*) "Please answer the questions correctly."
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**7.2**
- **Base Form:** *correct*
- **Derived Form:** *correctness*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *correct* + *-ness*
- **Natural English translation:** the quality of being correct; accuracy; conformity with standards
- **Usage:** Refers to the property of conforming to fact, rule, or standard. Also appears in the compound *political correctness*, where the sense shifts to conformity with social norms. Used in descriptive and normative contexts. Common across all registers. "The correctness of the calculation was verified."
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**7.3**
- **Base Form:** *correct*
- **Derived Form:** *incorrect*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *in-* + *correct*
- **Natural English translation:** not correct; wrong; inaccurate; not conforming to fact or standard
- **Usage:** Applied to answers, statements, and procedures to indicate that they do not conform to fact or standard. Used in general and formal registers. (*an incorrect assumption*, *incorrect grammar*) "The answer given was incorrect."
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**7.4**
- **Base Form:** *correct*
- **Derived Form:** *incorrectly*
- **Literal Breakdown:** *in-* + *correct* + *-

# CLCA Protocol v3.2 — P3 NATURAL SEMANTIC GROUPING
# Language: English (en)

---

## Preliminary Note

Groupings below are derived from patterns of usage, collocation, contextual contrast, and register distribution observed across P1 and P2 outputs. No predefined semantic framework has been imposed. Group boundaries are permeable; overlapping membership is noted explicitly. Items that do not fit any observed grouping are listed at the end.

## Group 1: Expressions used to affirm that a statement or account corresponds to the facts

**Label:** Factual correspondence affirmations

**Definition:** These expressions are used to assert or acknowledge that a proposition, description, or account matches what actually happened or what is actually the case. They appear in contexts where the speaker is either making a positive claim about factual accuracy or conceding that a claim is accurate. They contrast with expressions in Group 2 (which concern the genuineness of objects or persons rather than the truth of statements) and Group 5 (which concern the appearance of truth rather than its assertion).

**Representative expressions from P1 and P2:**

- *true* (P1.1) — predicative and attributive use: *that's true*, *it is true that*
- *truth* (P1.2) — *tell the truth*, *the truth of the matter*, *in truth*
- *accurate* (P1.3) — *factually accurate*, *broadly accurate*, *accurate to within*
- *correct* (P1.7) — *that is correct*, *correct in every detail*, *broadly correct*
- *right* (P1.8) — *you're right*, *am I right?*, *dead right*
- *truthful* (P2a.2.1) — applied to persons and statements
- *truthfully* (P2a.2.2) — *I can truthfully say that*
- *truly* (P2a.1.3) — as affirmation of genuineness of a predicate
- *factually accurate* (P2b.C3.4)
- *technically accurate* (P2b.C3.6) — with the implication that narrow accuracy may coexist with broader misleadingness
- *hold true* (P1b.1.15) — of principles and generalizations
- *prove true* (P1b.1.16) — of predictions confirmed by events
- *ring true* (P1b.1.14) — of accounts that have the quality of being believable
- *the whole truth* (P2b.C2.2)
- *nothing but the truth* (P2b.C2.3)
- *gospel truth* (P2b.C2.17)
- *the plain truth* (P2b.C2.18)
- *the naked truth* (P2b.C2.19)
- *ground truth* (P1b.1.25) — verified factual information used as a standard
- *verity* (P1b.2.6) — formal/literary register
- *veracity* (P1.15) — applied to accounts and testimony
- *sound* (P1.25) — of arguments whose premises are true and whose form is valid

**Observed contrasts within this group:** *accurate* and *correct* are used in contexts where a standard or fact exists against which correspondence can be measured; *true* and *right* are broader and appear in both propositional and moral contexts. *Sound* is restricted to arguments and judgments. *Gospel truth*, *the naked truth*, and *the plain truth* are emphatic variants used when the speaker wants to stress that no embellishment or concealment is present.

## Group 2: Expressions used to assert that an object, person, document, or emotion is what it purports to be

**Label:** Genuineness and non-imitation assertions

**Definition:** These expressions are used when the question at issue is not whether a statement is factually correct but whether a thing, person, or feeling is the real instance of its kind rather than a copy, imitation, performance, or fraud. They appear prominently in commercial, legal, archival, and interpersonal contexts. They contrast with Group 1 (propositional truth) and with Group 4 (which concerns the legal or procedural standing of acts and instruments).

**Representative expressions from P1 and P2:**

- *genuine* (P1.4) — *the genuine article*, *genuine concern*, *genuine mistake*, *genuine belief*
- *authentic* (P1.5) — *authentic self*, *authentic experience*, *authentic voice*, *certified authentic*
- *authenticity* (P2a.5.2)
- *authenticate* (P2a.5.3) — *authenticate a document*, *authenticate a user*
- *authentication* (P2a.5.4) — *two-factor authentication*
- *bona fide* (P1.22) — *bona fide offer*, *bona fide member*, *bona fide purchaser*
- *genuine article* (P2b.C4.1) — fixed phrase
- *sincere* (P1.21) — applied to emotions and expressions: *sincere apology*, *sincere belief*
- *sincerity* (P2a.21.2)
- *true to oneself* (P2b.C1.2)
- *true to one's word* (P2b.C1.1)
- *true-blue* (P1b.1.7) — loyalty and genuine commitment
- *true-born* (P1b.1.37) — literary/historical
- *true-hearted* (P1b.1.38) — literary/archaic
- *trustworthy* (P1.27)
- *trustworthiness* (P2a.1.33)
- *the real McCoy* (P2b.C22.5) — informal equivalent

**Observed contrasts within this group:** *Genuine* and *authentic* overlap substantially but *authentic* carries stronger connotations of originality and is more common in aesthetic, philosophical, and digital identity contexts. *Sincere* is restricted to emotions, expressions, and intentions; it is not used of objects. *Bona fide* is more formal and legal in register. *True to one's word* and *true to oneself* involve a relational sense of faithfulness rather than the object-identity sense of *genuine* and *authentic*.

## Group 3: Expressions used to describe deliberate production of something false or misleading

**Label:** Deliberate falsification and fraud

**Definition:** These expressions are used when the emphasis is on intentional deception — the deliberate creation, alteration, or presentation of something false as if it were true or genuine. They appear in legal, journalistic, and moral contexts and typically imply agency and intent, distinguishing them from expressions that describe mere error or inaccuracy. They contrast with Group 6 (which covers the state of being false without necessarily implying intent).

**Representative expressions from P1 and P2:**

- *falsification* (P1b.3.7 / P2a.10.5) — in the legal/administrative sense
- *falsehood* (P1.11) — with connotation of deliberate deception

**Observed contrasts within this group:** *Counterfeit* and *fraudulent* are the most strongly legal in register and imply financial or material gain as a motive. *Fake* is broader and more informal, covering both objects and performances. *Fabricated* emphasizes construction from nothing. *Falsify* emphasizes alteration of something that existed. *Disingenuous* is distinctive in that the deception operates through selective presentation rather than outright invention. *Falsehood* and *false witness* carry moral and sometimes religious weight beyond the purely legal.

## Group 4: Expressions used in assessments of legal standing, procedural validity, or official recognition

**Definition:** These expressions are used when the question is whether an act, instrument, claim, or authority has the force of law, meets required procedural standards, or is recognized as rightful by a relevant authority. **Observed contrasts within this group:** *Valid* is used in both logical and legal senses; in logic it concerns argument form, in law it concerns binding force. *Legitimate* is broader and extends into political philosophy (legitimate authority, legitimacy of a government). *Rightful* emphasizes entitlement by right or justice rather than procedural compliance. *Bona fide* in legal contexts specifically concerns good faith and absence of fraudulent intent. *Delegitimize* is distinctive as a verb describing the active undermining of recognized standing.

## Group 5: Expressions used to describe the appearance or likelihood of truth without asserting it

**Label:** Apparent truth and assessed probability

**Definition:** These expressions are used when a speaker is evaluating how convincing, probable, or believable something appears, without committing to a claim that it is actually true. They are common in analytical, journalistic, and investigative contexts where evidence is incomplete. They contrast with Group 1 (which asserts actual correspondence) and Group 3 (which concerns deliberate deception).

**Representative expressions from P1 and P2:**

- *implausible* (P2a.18.3)
- *implausibility* (P2a.18.5)

- *credibly* (P2a.17.1)

- *verisimilitude* (P1.24) — appearance of truth in literary and philosophical contexts
- *apocryphal* (P1.23) — widely circulated but of doubtful authenticity
- *probably apocryphal* (P2b.C23.2)
- *almost certainly apocryphal* (P2b.C23.3)
- *ring true* (P1b.1.14) — sounds genuine
- *ring false* (P2b.C10.15) — sounds unconvincing

**Observed contrasts within this group:** *Plausible* and *credible* both concern believability but *credible* is more commonly applied to persons and sources (witnesses, threats) while *plausible* is more commonly applied to explanations and scenarios. *Verisimilitude* is restricted to aesthetic and philosophical contexts. *Apocryphal* is distinctive in that it describes something widely believed despite doubtful authenticity — the appearance of truth has achieved cultural currency. The expressions *a grain of truth*, *kernel of truth*, *half-truth*, *stretch the truth*, *bend the truth*, and *economical with the truth* all describe partial or distorted truth, forming a sub-cluster within this group.

## Group 6: Expressions used to describe the state of being false, incorrect, or not what it appears

**Label:** Falseness and incorrectness

**Definition:** These expressions are used to characterize propositions, objects, or situations as not corresponding to fact, not being genuine, or not being what they appear to be. Unlike Group 3, these expressions do not necessarily imply deliberate intent; they can describe error, illusion, or structural incorrectness as well as deception. They contrast with Group 1 (factual correspondence) and Group 2 (genuineness).

- *untrue* (P2a.1.4)

**Label:** Source and witness reliability assessments

They appear in journalistic, legal, and investigative contexts and are applied to persons and sources rather than to propositions or objects directly. ## Group 9: Expressions used to describe correction of errors or restoration of accurate accounts

**Label:** Correction and rectification

**Definition:** These expressions are used when the focus is on the act of identifying and fixing an error, restoring an accurate account, or acknowledging that one was wrong. They appear in editorial, professional, legal, and everyday contexts. They contrast with Group 1 (which asserts truth) and Group 3 (which concerns deliberate falsification).

**Representative expressions from P1 and P2:**

- *correct* (P1.7) — as a verb: to correct an error
- *correction* (P2a.7.6)
- *corrective* (P2a.7.7)
- *self-correcting* (P2a.7.9)
- *stand corrected* (P2b.C7.4)
- *correct me if I'm wrong* (P2b.C7.3)
- *get it right* (P2b.C8.3)

- *set the record straight* (P2b.C8.5)

**Observed contrasts within this group:** *Stand corrected* and *correct me if I'm wrong* are conversational and interpersonal. *Set the record straight* is used specifically when a false or misleading account has been circulating. *Self-correcting* is applied to systems and processes rather than persons. *Invalidate* concerns the removal of force rather than the correction of factual error.

## Group 10: Expressions used in political and public discourse about the status of truth in collective life

**Label:** Public and political truth discourse

**Definition:** These expressions are used in political, journalistic, and activist contexts to discuss the role of truth, honesty, and deception in public life, institutions, and media. They are not primarily used to evaluate individual propositions but to characterize broader conditions, practices, or dynamics involving truth and power. - *the inconvenient truth* (P2b.C2.21)

**Observed contrasts within this group:** *Post-truth* and *fake news* characterize media and political environments. *Speaking truth to power* and *truth-telling* describe practices of honest disclosure against institutional resistance. *Plausible deniability* and *false flag* describe strategic uses of deception by actors with power. *Home truth* and *the inconvenient truth* describe accurate information that is unwelcome. *False consciousness* is distinctive in describing a systematic condition of misrecognition rather than a specific act of deception.

## Ambiguous and Overlapping Items

The following items show clear membership in more than one group, or their placement depends on context of use:

---

***sincere* / *sincerity* / *insincere* / *insincerity***
- Belongs to **Group 2** (genuineness of emotions and expressions) and potentially **Group 3** (deliberate performance of false feeling). The distinction depends on whether the insincerity is deliberate or merely absent. Usage does not consistently resolve this.

---

***credible* / *credibility***
- Belongs to both **Group 5** (appearance of truth, assessed probability) and **Group 7** (source and witness reliability). In legal contexts (*credible witness*) the focus is on the source; in analytical contexts (*credible explanation*) the focus is on the claim itself.

---

***valid* / *validity***
- Belongs to both **Group 4** (legal and procedural validity) and **Group 8** (logical and scientific validity). The two senses are distinct in usage but share the same form.

---

***falsification***
- Belongs to both **Group 3** (deliberate falsification of documents) and **Group 8** (Popperian scientific method). The two senses are clearly distinguished by context but share the same form.

---

***verify* / *verification* / *verified***
- Belongs to both **Group 7** (assessing sources and claims) and **Group 8** (scientific testability). *Verified account* (digital identity) belongs to **Group 2** (genuineness). The word operates across all three groups depending on context.

---

***true to life* / *verisimilitude* / *authentic reproduction***
- These expressions concern the accurate representation of reality in artistic or narrative contexts. They overlap between **Group 1** (correspondence to fact) and **Group 5** (appearance of truth). They are not straightforwardly in either group because the question is not whether a proposition is true but whether a representation is convincing or faithful.

---

***apocryphal***
- Belongs primarily to **Group 5** (appearance of truth without assertion of it) but also touches **Group 6** (falseness) since apocryphal stories are typically regarded as probably not true. The word is distinctive in that it describes something that has achieved cultural currency despite doubtful authenticity, which neither group fully captures.

---

***legitimate* / *legitimacy***
- Belongs primarily to **Group 4** (legal and procedural validity) but in political philosophy contexts (*legitimate authority*, *legitimacy of a government*) it operates in **Group 10** (public and political truth discourse). *Legitimate grievance* and *legitimate question* are closer to **Group 1** (well-founded, accurate) than to either Group 4 or Group 10.

---

***fallible* / *infallible* / *fallibility* / *infallibility***
- Belongs to **Group 8** (logical and scientific discourse) in philosophical contexts but is also used in everyday and theological contexts to describe the general capacity for error in persons and institutions. In the latter use it does not fit neatly into any group defined above.

---

***truth drug* / *truth serum***
- These expressions belong to **Group 10** (public and political truth discourse) in their espionage and political uses, but they also belong to a more specific domain (pharmacology, interrogation) that is not well represented in any group above.

---

---

## Items That Do Not Fit Clearly into Any Group

The following items from P1 and P2 do not show a clear pattern of usage that aligns them with any of the groups identified above. This is noted without interpretation.

***true north*** (P1b.1.9)
Used in navigation (geometric accuracy) and in leadership/self-help discourse (guiding values). Neither use is primarily about propositional truth, genuineness, or any of the other patterns identified above. The metaphorical use is too diffuse to constitute a grouping on its own.

---

***out of true*** (P2b.C1.3)
Used in engineering and carpentry to describe geometric misalignment. The connection to the truth-evaluation domain is etymological rather than functional in current usage.

---

***true-blue*** (P1b.1.7)
Used to describe political loyalty and genuine commitment. The sense is fidelity and identity rather than factual truth or genuineness of objects. It does not fit cleanly into Group 2 (genuineness) or Group 10 (political truth discourse).

---

***moment of truth*** (P1b.1.30)
Used to describe a decisive or climactic moment of testing. The sense is revelation of quality or character under pressure, which does not map onto any of the groups above without distortion.

---

***fake it till you make it*** (P2b.C9.2)
A proverbial expression in self-help and motivational discourse. It involves the concept of faking but in a context where the faking is presented as a legitimate strategy rather than a deception. It does not fit Group 3 (deliberate falsification) without distortion.

---

***right on*** (P2b.C8.7)
In its countercultural usage as an expression of enthusiastic endorsement, this expression has moved away from the truth-evaluation domain entirely. In its literal use (*right on target*) it belongs to Group 1, but the two uses coexist without clear separation.

---

***in vino veritas*** (P1b.2.16)
A Latin proverb used in English. It makes a social observation about candor and alcohol rather than asserting or evaluating a specific proposition. It is noted in Group 10 above but its fit there is loose.

# CLCA Protocol v3.2 — P1b ROOT EXPANSION
# Language: English (en)

---

## Step 1 — Identify Central Roots

---

**Root 1: *ver-* (L *verus* "true")**

- Root form: *ver-* (Latin origin; no native script distinction in English)
- P1 items participating: **verifiable** (#21), **veridical** (#15), **veracious** (#25), **bona fide / bona fides** (#22, #26) (via shared Latin *verus* ancestry in the truth-domain cluster), and the embedded stem in **verify** (cited within #21's derivational family)
- Strictly direct *ver-* items: **verifiable**, **veridical**, **veracious** = 3 direct items; the root also underlies *verify*, *veracity*, *verification* cited as derivational forms across multiple entries
- Core meaning contribution: Supplies the semantic nucleus of correspondence-to-fact, truth-telling, and truthfulness. The most etymologically concentrated "truth" root in the P1 set.

---

**Root 2: *true / truth* (OE *trēowe*, *trēowþ*)**

- Root form: *true*, *truth* (native English)
- P1 items participating: **true** (#1), **truthful** (#16), and the derivational families cited under both (*truth*, *truly*, *truism*, *untrue*, *truthfully*, *truthfulness*, *untruthful*) = 2 direct headwords, with the largest single derivational cluster in P1
- Core meaning contribution: The primary native-English carrier of truth-predication. Supplies both propositional truth (correspondence of statements to facts) and relational fidelity (loyalty, genuineness to type). Highly productive in compounding and derivation.

---

**Root 3: *fals-* (L *falsus* "deceived, feigned")**

- Root form: *fals-* (Latin origin)
- P1 items participating: **false** (#2), and the derivational family *falsity*, *falsify*, *falsification*, *falsely* cited under #2; also structurally related to the evaluative polarity established by **fake** (#11), **counterfeit** (#12), **spurious** (#13), **specious** (#24), **ersatz** (#27), **pseudo-** (#28) as the negative pole of the domain
- Direct *fals-* items: **false** = 1 direct headword, but the root is the most morphologically productive negative-truth stem in the set
- Core meaning contribution: Supplies the primary negative pole of truth-evaluation: inaccuracy, inauthenticity, deceptive appearance, and misrepresentation. Highly productive in derivation and compounding.

---

> **Note on selection rationale:** *Ver-*, *true/truth*, and *fals-* are selected because they are the three roots that most directly and recurrently build the propositional truth-evaluation vocabulary in English. Other roots present in P1 (e.g., *cred-*, *leg-*, *gen-*) are productive but serve adjacent domains (belief, law, origin) rather than truth-predication as a primary function.

## Step 2 — Expand Each Root

---

### ROOT 1: *ver-* (L *verus* "true")

---

**1.1**
- Form: verify
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: L *verus* "true" + *facere* "to make" → ML *verificare*
- Natural English translation: to establish or confirm the truth or accuracy of something
- Context of use: Used across scientific, journalistic, legal, and everyday contexts when checking a claim against evidence or an authoritative source. "Please verify your email address."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**1.2**
- Form: verification
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *verificare* + *-tion* (nominalization)
- Natural English translation: the process or act of establishing truth or accuracy
- Context of use: Used in technical, legal, administrative, and scientific registers to name the procedure of checking. "Independent verification of the results is required before publication."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**1.3**
- Form: veracity
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: L *verax* "truth-telling" (from *verus*) + *-ity*
- Natural English translation: habitual truthfulness; the quality of being accurate or truthful
- Context of use: Used in formal, legal, and literary registers to evaluate the truthfulness of a person or the accuracy of an account. "The court questioned the veracity of the witness."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**1.4**
- Form: verity
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: L *veritas* "truth" → OFr *verité* → ME *verite*
- Natural English translation: a true principle or belief; an established truth
- Context of use: Used in formal and literary registers, often in the plural (*eternal verities*), to refer to fundamental truths or axioms. "He spoke of the eternal verities of human experience."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**1.5**
- Form: veritable
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: OFr *veritable*, from *verité* "truth" + *-able*
- Natural English translation: genuine; used as an intensifier to assert that something really is what it is described as
- Context of use: Used in general and literary registers, often with a slightly emphatic or ironic tone. "The garden was a veritable paradise." "It was a veritable disaster."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**1.6**
- Form: veritably
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *veritable* + *-ly*
- Natural English translation: truly; in a genuine or real sense (adverbial intensifier)
- Context of use: Used as an adverb modifying adjectives or verbs to emphasize the reality or degree of something. Less common than *veritable* but attested in literary and formal prose.
- Epistemic status: **[MARGINAL]**

---

**1.7**
- Form: veridical
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: L *verus* "true" + *dicere* "to say" + *-al*
- Natural English translation: accurately corresponding to reality; truth-telling
- Context of use: Used in philosophical and psychological contexts, especially regarding perception. "A veridical hallucination would be a perception that happens to be accurate." (Already in P1 #15; included here as part of the root expansion.)
- Epistemic status: **[MARGINAL]**

---

**1.8**
- Form: veridicality
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *veridical* + *-ity*
- Natural English translation: the property of accurately corresponding to reality
- Context of use: Used in philosophy of mind and epistemology as a technical noun. "The veridicality of perceptual experience is a central problem in philosophy of perception."
- Epistemic status: **[MARGINAL]**

---

**1.9**
- Form: verisimilitude
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: L *verus* "true" + *similis* "like" + *-tude*
- Natural English translation: the appearance of being true or real; lifelike quality
- Context of use: Used in literary criticism, aesthetics, and philosophy to describe the quality of a narrative, representation, or theory that makes it seem true or realistic. "The novel's verisimilitude depends on its precise period detail."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**1.10**
- Form: verisimilar
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: L *verus* "true" + *similis* "like" + *-ar*
- Natural English translation: having the appearance of truth; seemingly true or real
- Context of use: Used in literary and philosophical registers as the adjectival form of *verisimilitude*. Less common than the noun. "The account is verisimilar but cannot be independently confirmed."
- Epistemic status: **[MARGINAL]**

---

**1.11**
- Form: verdict
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: ML *veredictum*, from L *verus* "true" + *dictum* "thing said"
- Natural English translation: a formal decision or judgment, especially in a legal proceeding
- Context of use: Used primarily in legal contexts for the formal finding of a jury or judge. Also used more broadly for any authoritative judgment. "The jury returned a verdict of not guilty."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**1.12**
- Form: aver
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: OFr *averer*, from ML *adverare*, from L *ad-* + *verus* "true"
- Natural English translation: to assert or affirm something as a fact with confidence
- Context of use: Used in formal, legal, and literary registers. "She averred that she had been present at the time." Carries a sense of confident, formal assertion.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**1.13**
- Form: averment
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *aver* + *-ment*
- Natural English translation: a formal or positive assertion; in law, a positive statement of fact in a pleading
- Context of use: Used primarily in legal and formal registers. "The averment in the complaint was not supported by evidence."
- Epistemic status: **[MARGINAL]**

---

**1.14**
- Form: verify against
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *verify* + prepositional phrase
- Natural English translation: to check (a claim or figure) by comparison with an authoritative source
- Context of use: Used in procedural, scientific, and administrative contexts. "Verify the figures against the original records."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**1.15**
- Form: verification procedure
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *verification* + *procedure*
- Natural English translation: a systematic method for confirming the truth or accuracy of something
- Context of use: Used in scientific, technical, and administrative contexts. "The laboratory follows a strict verification procedure for all submitted samples."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**1.16**
- Form: verificationism
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *verification* + *-ism*
- Natural English translation: the philosophical doctrine that a statement is meaningful only if it is empirically verifiable
- Context of use: Used in philosophy of language and logical positivism. "Verificationism was the central thesis of the Vienna Circle."
- Epistemic status: **[MARGINAL]**

---

**1.17**
- Form: verificationist
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *verification* + *-ist*
- Natural English translation: a proponent of verificationism; of or relating to verificationism
- Context of use: Used in philosophical discourse. "A verificationist criterion of meaning would exclude many metaphysical claims."
- Epistemic status: **[MARGINAL]**

---

**1.18**
- Form: verity (eternal verities)
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *verity* used in fixed collocation with *eternal*
- Natural English translation: fundamental, timeless truths
- Context of use: Used in literary, philosophical, and rhetorical registers to refer to truths considered permanent and universal. "He appealed to the eternal verities of justice and compassion."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**1.19**
- Form: in very truth
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *in* + *very* (from OFr *verai*, L *verus*) + *truth*
- Natural English translation: truly; in actual fact (archaic/literary intensifier)
- Context of use: Found in older literary and religious texts as an emphatic assertion. "In very truth, I tell you this."
- Epistemic status: **[MARGINAL]**

---

**1.20**
- Form: very (adjective, archaic/literary)
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: OFr *verai* "true," from L *verus*
- Natural English translation: actual, true (in its older adjectival use, as in "the very truth," "the very man")
- Context of use: Survives in fixed phrases such as *the very thing*, *the very idea*, *caught in the very act*. The truth-related sense is largely archaic but the form is highly frequent.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

> **Note:** The *ver-* root is moderately productive in English, with approximately 15–20 well-attested forms. Additional technical or rare forms exist (e.g., *veridically*, *veridicalism*) but are of low frequency.

### ROOT 2: *true / truth* (OE *trēowe*, *trēowþ*)

> **Note:** This root is highly productive in English. The following lists the 25 most common and analytically significant forms. Additional compounds and collocations exist beyond those listed here.

---

**2.1**
- Form: truth
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: OE *trēowþ*, from *trēowe* "faithful" + *-þ* (abstract noun suffix)
- Natural English translation: the quality or state of being true; that which is true
- Context of use: Used across all registers as the primary noun for the concept of correspondence to fact or reality. "Tell me the truth." "The truth of the matter is disputed."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**2.2**
- Form: truly
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *true* + *-ly*
- Natural English translation: in a true manner; genuinely; used as an intensifier
- Context of use: Used as an adverb in general speech and writing. Also appears in formal closings (*yours truly*). "I truly believe this is the right decision."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**2.3**
- Form: truism
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *true* + *-ism*
- Natural English translation: a statement that is obviously true and therefore not worth stating; a platitude
- Context of use: Used in general and intellectual discourse to dismiss a claim as trivially obvious. "It is a truism that prevention is better than cure."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**2.4**
- Form: untrue
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *un-* + *true*
- Natural English translation: not true; false
- Context of use: Used in general speech as a somewhat softer alternative to *false* or *wrong*. "That statement is simply untrue."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**2.5**
- Form: untruth
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *un-* + *truth*
- Natural English translation: a false statement; a lie (often used as a euphemism)
- Context of use: Used in formal and polite registers as a euphemism for *lie*. "He was accused of telling untruths."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**2.6**
- Form: untruthful
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *un-* + *truthful*
- Natural English translation: not truthful; given to telling falsehoods
- Context of use: Used in general and formal registers to characterize a person or statement. "The report was found to be untruthful in several key respects."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**2.7**
- Form: truthfulness
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *truthful* + *-ness*
- Natural English translation: the quality of being truthful
- Context of use: Used in moral, legal, and general evaluative contexts. "The truthfulness of the account was never seriously questioned."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**2.8**
- Form: truth-telling
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *truth* + *tell* + *-ing*
- Natural English translation: the act or practice of telling the truth
- Context of use: Used in philosophical, ethical, and general discourse, often as a gerund or attributive noun. "Truth-telling is considered a fundamental moral obligation in most ethical systems."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**2.9**
- Form: truth-teller
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *truth* + *tell* + *-er*
- Natural English translation: a person who tells the truth; one who speaks honestly
- Context of use: Used in general and philosophical discourse, sometimes with a heroic or admiring connotation. "She was known as a truth-teller who refused to soften her assessments."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**2.10**
- Form: truth value
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *truth* + *value*
- Natural English translation: the property of a proposition of being true or false
- Context of use: Used as a technical term in logic and philosophy of language. "In classical logic, every proposition has exactly one truth value: true or false."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**2.11**
- Form: truth table
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *truth* + *table*
- Natural English translation: a table showing the truth values of a compound proposition for all combinations of truth values of its components
- Context of use: Used in formal logic, mathematics, and computer science. "Students construct truth tables to evaluate the validity of logical arguments."

---

**2.12**
- Form: truth condition

---

**2.13**
- Form: truth-conditional
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *truth* + *condition* + *-al*
- Natural English translation: of or relating to truth conditions; describing a theory of meaning based on truth conditions
- Context of use: Used in formal semantics. "Truth-conditional semantics identifies the meaning of a sentence with its truth conditions."

---

**2.14**

- Transliteration: N/A

---

**2.15**

- Literal morpheme breakdown: *home* + *truth*

**2.16**

**2.17**

### ROOT 3: *fals-* (L *falsus* "deceived, feigned")

---

**3.1**
- Form: falsity
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *false* + *-ity*
- Natural English translation: the quality of being false; a false statement or belief
- Context of use: Used in formal, philosophical, and legal registers. "The falsity of the claim was established by documentary evidence."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**3.2**
- Form: falsely
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *false* + *-ly*
- Natural English translation: in a false manner; incorrectly; dishonestly
- Context of use: Used in general and legal registers. "He was falsely accused of theft."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**3.3**
- Form: falsify
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *false* + *-ify* (from L *falsificare*)
- Natural English translation: to alter or misrepresent fraudulently; to make false
- Context of use: Used in legal, scientific, and general registers to describe deliberate alteration of records, data, or evidence. "She was charged with falsifying financial records."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**3.4**
- Form: falsification
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *falsify* + *-ation*
- Natural English translation: the act of falsifying; in philosophy of science, the process of showing a hypothesis to be false
- Context of use: Used in legal contexts for fraudulent alteration, and in philosophy of science (Popperian usage) for the testing of hypotheses. "Falsification of data is a serious breach of research ethics."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**3.5**
- Form: falsifiability
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *falsify* + *-able* + *-ity*
- Natural English translation: the capacity of a hypothesis or theory to be shown false by observation or experiment
- Context of use: Used as a technical term in philosophy of science, associated with Karl Popper's criterion of demarcation. "Falsifiability is often cited as a criterion distinguishing science from pseudoscience."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**3.6**
- Form: falsifiable
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *falsify* + *-able*
- Natural English translation: capable of being shown to be false; testable
- Context of use: Used in philosophy of science and general intellectual discourse. "A scientific hypothesis must be falsifiable."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**3.7**
- Form: unfalsifiable
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *un-* + *falsifiable*
- Natural English translation: not capable of being shown to be false; not testable
- Context of use: Used in philosophy of science and critical discourse to characterize claims that cannot be empirically tested. "The claim is unfalsifiable and therefore outside the scope of scientific inquiry."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**3.8**
- Form: false alarm
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *false* + *alarm*
- Natural English translation: a warning or alert that turns out to be unfounded
- Context of use: Used in general speech and emergency management contexts. "The fire alarm turned out to be a false alarm."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**3.9**
- Form: false pretenses
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *false* + *pretenses*
- Natural English translation: misrepresentations made to deceive; fraudulent claims
- Context of use: Used in legal and general registers. "He obtained the money under false pretenses."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**3.10**

- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal morpheme breakdown: *false* + *start*
- Natural English translation: an invalid start in a race; more broadly, an unsuccessful attempt to begin something
- Context of use: Used in sports contexts literally, and figuratively in general speech. "After several false starts, the project finally got underway."

**3.11**

## Step 3 — Peripheral Extensions

The following expressions involve the three roots identified above but appear in contexts where the contribution of the root shifts away from its core truth-domain function. These are flagged without interpretation or categorization.

---

**From *ver-*:**

- **veritable** and **veritably** function primarily as intensifiers (*a veritable feast*, *veritably transformed*) with the truth-correspondence sense receding.
- **verdict** operates within a procedural and institutional frame (legal judgment) rather than a propositional truth frame.
- **very** (adjective/adverb) in modern English functions almost exclusively as a degree intensifier (*very good*, *very much*) with no synchronically active truth-meaning for most speakers.
- **verisimilitude** is used in aesthetic evaluation of narrative and artistic realism, where the question is not whether something is true but whether it appears true.

---

**From *true / truth*:**

- **true north** is used in leadership and motivational discourse as a metaphor for guiding values or purpose, with no direct propositional truth content.
- **true-blue** predicates loyalty and political allegiance rather than correspondence to fact.
- **true to form** and **true to type** predicate conformity to expected patterns or biological categories rather than propositional accuracy.
- **out of true** is used in craft and engineering contexts to describe physical alignment, with no propositional or epistemic content.
- **come true** (as in *dreams come true*) predicates the realization of a desired state rather than correspondence of a statement to fact.
- **tried and true** predicates reliability through experience rather than truth in the propositional sense.
- **true grit** predicates courage and determination, with no truth-domain content.
- **true love** predicates the genuineness or depth of an emotional bond.
- **ground truth** in machine learning contexts predicates empirical calibration data rather than propositional truth per se.
- **post-truth** predicates a social or political condition rather than a property of individual statements.
- **truth serum** predicates a pharmacological mechanism rather than a propositional or epistemic property.
- **moment of truth** predicates a temporal and dramatic quality (decisiveness, revelation) rather than a static correspondence relation.

---

**From *fals-*:**

- **false start** predicates procedural invalidity in athletic contexts, with no deception or misrepresentation implied.
- **false economy** predicates a mistaken practical judgment rather than a false proposition.
- **false note** in musical contexts predicates acoustic inaccuracy (pitch deviation) rather than propositional falsity; in figurative use, predicates social or rhetorical inappropriateness.
- **false modesty** predicates a social performance quality rather than a propositional or representational failure.
- **false consciousness** predicates a structural or ideological condition rather than the falsity of a specific proposition.
- **false friend** in linguistics predicates a cross-linguistic formal resemblance with semantic divergence, rather than deception or propositional falsity.
- **falsifiability** in Popperian philosophy predicates a methodological property of theories (testability) rather than actual falsity.
- **false alarm** predicates the absence of the predicted event rather than the falsity of a proposition in the standard sense.

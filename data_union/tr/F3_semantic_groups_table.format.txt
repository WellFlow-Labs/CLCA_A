| Evaluating statements as correct or incorrect | These terms are applied to propositions, claims, statements, and representations to judge whether they correspond to fact or to an accepted standard. | Affirming or questioning ontological genuineness | These terms are applied to objects, persons, identities, situations, and states of affairs to evaluate whether they are what they appear or claim to be — whether they have genuine existence or are counterfeit, simulated, or illusory. | Evaluating originality and authenticity of works and objects | These terms are applied specifically to works, documents, and objects to evaluate whether they are the genuine original rather than a copy, imitation, or derivative. | Characterizing claims as unfounded or baseless | These terms are applied to allegations, accusations, and claims to indicate that they lack supporting evidence or factual grounding. They appear primarily in legal and journalistic contexts. The emphasis is not merely that the claim is wrong but that it has no foundation at all. | *asılsız*; *asılsızlık*; *asılsız olduğu kanıtlanmak*; *asılsız olduğunu öne sürmek* | *asılsız* (also appears in Group 1 and Group 3) | *Asılsız* is the dominant term and its collocational range is narrow, centering on *asılsız iddia*. The group is distinguished from Group 1 (incorrect statements) by the specific emphasis on absence of foundation rather than mere incorrectness. *Asılsız* does not typically appear in philosophical or scientific discourse about truth values; it is primarily a legal and journalistic term. |
| Elevated-register expressions for truth and reality | These expressions cover similar semantic ground to items in Groups 1 and 2 but are distinguished by their consistent appearance in formal, literary, philosophical, or religious registers rather than in everyday speech. They are typically of Arabic origin and carry a weight of seriousness or philosophical depth that their everyday counterparts lack. They are used in contexts where the speaker wishes to invoke a more serious or elevated tone. | *hakikat*; *hakikaten*; *hakikati söylemek*; *hakikate ulaşmak*; *hakikat arayışı*; *hakikat sonrası*; *hakiki*; *hakiki dostluk*; *hakikaten öyle*; *hakikat cesaret ister*; *hakikatperest*; *hakikat karşısında*; *hakikat ortaya çıkmak*; *acı hakikat*; *hakikat acı olabilir*; *hakiki anlamda*; *sahih*; *sahihlik*; *sahih hadis* | *hakikat* (belongs here and in Group 2); *hakiki* (overlaps with Group 3 in commercial and interpersonal uses such as *hakiki deri*); *hakikat sonrası* (appears in contemporary journalistic usage but retains the formal weight of *hakikat* as its base) | This group does not represent a distinct semantic domain but a distinct register distribution. The items here overlap substantially with Groups 1, 2, and 3 in meaning; what distinguishes them is their consistent restriction to elevated registers. *Sahih* is the most domain-restricted item in this group, appearing almost exclusively in Islamic studies and textual scholarship. |
| Evaluating justification and rightfulness | These terms are applied to persons, actions, decisions, and claims to evaluate whether they are justified, rightful, or in accordance with what is owed. They appear in moral, legal, and everyday contexts. The central question is whether someone or something has the right on their side. | *haklı*; *haksız*; *haklılık*; *haksızlık*; *haklıca*; *haklı olmak*; *haklı çıkmak*; *haklı olduğunu kanıtlamak*; *haksız yere*; *haklı olarak*; *haksız olduğunu kabul etmek*; *hak vermek* / *sana hak veriyorum*; *kim haklı, kim haksız?*; *hak*; *hak etmek*; *hakkını vermek*; *hak ihlali* / *insan hakları*; *haksız kazanç*, *haksız rekabet*, *haksız fiil*; *haklı gerekçe*; *haklı eleştiri*; *haklı kılmak*; *haklı şikâyet*; *haksızca*; *haksız yere suçlamak*; *hak mı haksız mı?*; *hakkını teslim etmek*; *hak teslimi*; *haklı şikâyet / haklı eleştiri / haklı itiraz* | *haklı* and *doğru* (both translate as "right" in English but differ: *doğru* evaluates statements and facts, while *haklı* evaluates persons and positions as warranted); *hak etmek* (related but belongs more naturally to Group 7 because it concerns desert rather than the evaluation of a position as justified); *haklı çıkmak* (bridges Groups 1 and 6: involves both the vindication of a person and the confirmation that their claim was correct); *hak* (participates in both Group 6 and Group 7); *hakkaniyet* (placed in Group 7 rather than here because its usage focuses on distributive fairness rather than on whether a specific person is in the right) | *Haklı* and *doğru* both translate as "right" in English, but their usage patterns differ: *doğru* evaluates statements and facts, while *haklı* evaluates persons and positions as warranted. The *hak-* root also underlies *hakikat* (Group 5) and *hakkaniyet* (Group 7), but those terms are grouped separately because their primary usage contexts differ. *haklı çıkmak* bridges Groups 1 and 6. |
| Legal entitlement and rights | These expressions are used in legal, moral, and political contexts to refer to what a person is entitled to, what they deserve, or what is owed to them. They appear in legal, political, and administrative registers. | Evaluating legality, procedural validity, and legitimacy | These terms are applied to actions, regulations, rights, institutions, contracts, votes, arguments, and documents to evaluate whether they conform to written law, satisfy conditions required for legal or institutional recognition, or are sanctioned by norms and principles of fairness that may extend beyond written law. They appear in legal, political, and administrative contexts.

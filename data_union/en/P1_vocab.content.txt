# CLCA Protocol v3.2 — P1 CORE VOCABULARY COLLECTION
# Language: English (en)

---

**Note on form fields:** English uses the Latin script natively; no transliteration column is applicable. The "literal meaning or morphological breakdown" field is provided where etymology or morphology is informative.

---

**1. true**
- Form: *true*
- Morphological breakdown: Old English *trēowe* (faithful, trustworthy)
- Typical translations: true, correct, genuine, real
- Usage: Used to predicate accuracy or correspondence of statements, representations, or beliefs to facts. Also applied to persons or objects to indicate genuineness or fidelity to type (e.g., *a true friend*, *true north*). Used across propositional, moral, and ontological contexts to assert that something conforms to fact, is genuine, or is faithful to an original. Participates in productive derivation: *truth*, *truly*, *truism*, *untrue*, *truthful*.
- [CORE]

---

**2. truth**
- Form: *truth*
- Morphological breakdown: Old English *trēowth* (faithfulness; fidelity; quality of being true); nominalization of *true*
- Typical translations: truth, verity, fact
- Usage: Refers to the property of being true, or to a true statement or fact. Used in philosophical, legal, moral, and everyday contexts. Appears in fixed expressions such as *tell the truth*, *truth of the matter*. Participates in compounds: *truth-telling*, *truth-value*, *half-truth*, *untruth*.
- [CORE]

---

**3. accurate**
- Form: *accurate*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *accuratus* (done with care), from *ad-* + *cura* (care)
- Typical translations: accurate, precise, correct, exact
- Usage: Applied to descriptions, measurements, representations, and reports to indicate close correspondence with fact or reality. Implies precision and freedom from error. Commonly contrasted with *approximate* or *inaccurate*. Commonly used in scientific, journalistic, and everyday evaluative contexts. Productive: *accuracy*, *accurately*, *inaccurate*, *inaccuracy*.
- [CORE]

---

**4. genuine**
- Form: *genuine*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *genuinus* (native, natural), possibly from *genu* (knee, in the sense of placing a child on the knee to acknowledge paternity)
- Typical translations: genuine, authentic, real, sincere
- Usage: Applied to objects, persons, emotions, and documents to indicate that they are what they purport to be, not counterfeit or feigned or simulated. Contrasts with *fake*, *spurious*, *counterfeit*. Productive: *genuinely*, *genuineness*.
- [CORE]

---

**5. authentic**
- Form: *authentic*
- Morphological breakdown: Greek *authentikos* (original, genuine), from *authentes* (one acting on one's own authority)
- Typical translations: authentic, genuine, original, authoritative
- Usage: Used of documents, artworks, persons, and experiences to indicate that they are original, not copied or falsified, or that they express a person's true character. In philosophical and popular usage, also applied to persons acting in accordance with their own values rather than external pressure. Appears in legal, aesthetic, and philosophical contexts. Productive: *authenticity*, *authentically*, *authenticate*, *authentication*, *inauthenticity*.
- [CORE]

---

**6. valid**
- Form: *valid*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *validus* (strong, effective), from *valere* (to be strong)
- Typical translations: valid, sound, legally effective, well-grounded
- Usage: In logic, predicates that an argument's conclusion follows necessarily from its premises. In legal and general usage, indicates that something is properly constituted, binding, or well-founded. Also used informally to mean well-founded or acceptable. Applied to arguments, contracts, claims, and procedures to indicate that they meet the relevant formal or substantive requirements for correctness or legal force. Productive: *validity*, *validate*, *invalid*, *invalidate*, *validation*.
- [CORE]

---

**7. correct**
- Form: *correct*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *correctus*, past participle of *corrigere* (to set right), from *com-* + *regere* (to rule, guide)
- Typical translations: correct, right, accurate, proper
- Usage: Applied to answers, behavior, procedures, and statements to indicate conformity with a standard, rule, or fact. Overlaps with *accurate* but more commonly used where a definite right answer or norm exists. Used in both descriptive and normative senses. Productive: *correctly*, *correctness*, *incorrect*, *correction*, *corrective*.
- [CORE]

---

**8. right**
- Form: *right*
- Morphological breakdown: Old English *riht* (straight, just, proper)
- Typical translations: right, correct, just, proper, true
- Usage: Covers a wide range of evaluative senses including factual correctness, moral rightness, and procedural propriety. Used predicatively (*that's right*) and attributively (*the right answer*). Productive: *rightly*, *rightness*, *rightful*, *rights*.
- [CORE]

---

**9. fake**
- Form: *fake*
- Morphological breakdown: origin uncertain; possibly from German *fegen* (to sweep, polish) via criminal slang or thieves' cant
- Typical translations: fake, counterfeit, false, sham, fraudulent
- Usage: Applied to objects, persons, documents, and statements that are presented as genuine but are not, produced or presented with intent to deceive. Functions as adjective, noun, and verb. Productive: *faker*, *fakery*, *faked*.
- [CORE]

---

**10. false**
- Form: *false*
- Morphological breakdown: Old English *fals*, from Latin *falsus* (deceived, feigned), past participle of *fallere* (to deceive)
- Typical translations: false, untrue, incorrect, counterfeit, deceitful
- Usage: Applied to propositions, beliefs, appearances, and persons to indicate untruth, deception, or inauthenticity. Also used of objects or persons to indicate inauthenticity or deceptive appearance (e.g., *false teeth*, *false pretenses*). Covers both epistemic falsity and moral duplicity. Productive: *falsely*, *falseness*, *falsity*, *falsify*, *falsification*, *falsehood*.
- [CORE]

---

**11. falsehood**
- Form: *falsehood*
- Morphological breakdown: *false* + *-hood* (condition, state)
- Typical translations: falsehood, lie, untruth, deception
- Usage: Refers to a false statement or the condition of being false. Used in moral and legal contexts, often with connotations of deliberate deception. Distinguished from *error* by typically implying intent.
- [CORE]

---

**12. counterfeit**
- Form: *counterfeit*
- Morphological breakdown: Old French *contrefait*, from *contrefaire* (to imitate), from *contre-* + *faire* (to make)
- Typical translations: counterfeit, forged, fake, imitation
- Usage: Primarily applied to currency, documents, and goods made in fraudulent imitation of genuine items. Carries a stronger implication of deliberate deception than *fake* in many contexts. Carries strong legal connotations of fraud. Used as noun, verb, and adjective. Productive: *counterfeiter*, *counterfeiting*.
- [CORE]

---

**13. spurious**
- Form: *spurious*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *spurius* (illegitimate, false)
- Typical translations: spurious, false, illegitimate, counterfeit, not genuine
- Usage: Applied to claims, arguments, documents, and attributions that are not what they appear to be or that lack genuine basis. More formal and often used in academic, legal, or scholarly registers. Productive: *spuriously*, *spuriousness*.
- [MARGINAL]

---

**14. veracious**
- Form: *veracious*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *verax* (stem *verac-*), truthful, from *verus* (true) + *-acious* (tending to)
- Typical translations: truthful, veracious, accurate
- Usage: Applied to persons and statements to indicate habitual truthfulness or factual accuracy. More formal and less common than *truthful*; found chiefly in literary and elevated registers. Productive: *veraciously*, *veracity*.
- [MARGINAL]

---

**15. veracity**
- Form: *veracity*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *verax* + *-itas*; Medieval Latin *veracitas*, from *verax* (truthful); quality of being truthful
- Typical translations: truthfulness, veracity, accuracy, honesty
- Usage: Refers to the quality of being truthful or accurate, applied to persons, accounts, and statements. Commonly used in legal and journalistic contexts when assessing the reliability of testimony or reporting, particularly in evaluating witness testimony. Derives from the same root as *veracious*.
- [CORE]

---

**16. reliable**
- Form: *reliable*
- Morphological breakdown: *rely* (from Old French *relier*, to bind back) + *-able*
- Typical translations: reliable, dependable, trustworthy, credible
- Usage: Applied to persons, sources, instruments, and methods to indicate that they can be consistently trusted to perform correctly or provide accurate information. Commonly used in evaluations of testimony, evidence, and character. Productive: *reliability*, *reliably*, *unreliable*, *unreliability*.
- [CORE]

---

**17. credible**
- Form: *credible*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *credibilis* (worthy of belief), from *credere* (to believe)
- Typical translations: credible, believable, plausible, trustworthy
- Usage: Applied to persons, accounts, and evidence to indicate that they are worthy of belief or trust, typically because they are plausible and the source is trustworthy. Distinct from *reliable* in focusing on believability rather than consistent performance. Used in legal contexts to assess witness testimony and in everyday contexts to evaluate claims. Productive: *credibility*, *credibly*, *incredible*, *incredibility*.
- [CORE]

---

**18. plausible**
- Form: *plausible*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *plausibilis* (deserving applause), from *plaudere* (to applaud)
- Typical translations: plausible, believable, seemingly reasonable, credible
- Usage: Applied to explanations, claims, and persons to indicate that they appear reasonable or probable on the basis of available evidence or general knowledge, without asserting that they are actually true. Occupies a weaker epistemic position than *credible* in many uses. Often carries a slight implication that the appearance may not match reality. Productive: *plausibility*, *plausibly*, *implausible*, *implausibility*.
- [CORE]

---

**19. legitimate**
- Form: *legitimate*
- Morphological breakdown: Medieval Latin *legitimatus*, from *legitimus* (lawful), from *lex* (law)
- Typical translations: legitimate, lawful, valid, genuine, rightful
- Usage: Applied to claims, authority, procedures, and persons to indicate conformity with law, rules, or accepted standards. Also used more loosely to mean genuine, real, or reasonable in informal contexts. Used in legal, political, and general evaluative contexts. Productive: *legitimacy*, *legitimately*, *illegitimate*, *legitimize*, *legitimation*.
- [CORE]

---

**20. fraudulent**
- Form: *fraudulent*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *fraudulentus*, from *fraus* (stem *fraud-*) (fraud, deceit, trickery)
- Typical translations: fraudulent, deceitful, dishonest, false
- Usage: Applied to acts, documents, and representations made with intent to deceive for unlawful gain. Primarily a legal and formal term but used broadly. Productive: *fraudulently*, *fraud*, *fraudulence*.
- [CORE]

**21. sincere**
- Form: *sincere*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *sincerus* (whole, pure, genuine); etymology debated (possibly *semel* "once" + *cera* "wax," referring to unrepaired pottery, or from *sine* "without" + *cera*)
- Typical translations: sincere, genuine, honest, heartfelt
- Usage: Applied to persons, emotions, expressions, and intentions to indicate that they are genuinely felt or meant, without pretense or dissimulation. Distinct from *truthful* in focusing on the match between expression and inner state rather than between statement and external fact. Contrasts with *insincere* and *hypocritical*. Productive: *sincerely*, *sincerity*, *insincerity*, *insincere*.
- [CORE]

---

**22. bona fide**
- Form: *bona fide*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *bona fide* (in good faith), from *bonus* (good) + *fides* (faith)
- Typical translations: genuine, authentic, in good faith, legitimate
- Usage: Used as an adjective or adverb to indicate that something is genuine or done in good faith, without fraud or deception. Common in legal and formal contexts. Also used informally to mean simply *real* or *genuine*. Functions as a fixed expression with limited internal modification.
- [CORE]

---

**23. apocryphal**
- Form: *apocryphal*
- Morphological breakdown: Greek *apokryphos* (hidden, obscure), from *apo-* (away) + *kryptein* (to hide)
- Typical translations: apocryphal, of doubtful authenticity, legendary, unverified
- Usage: Applied to stories, sayings, and texts whose authenticity or historicity is doubtful, though they are widely circulated. Originally referred to texts excluded from the biblical canon. Carries a connotation that the item is probably not genuine but has achieved cultural currency, though it may be difficult to disprove. Productive: *apocryphally*, *Apocrypha*.
- [CORE]

**24. verisimilitude**
- Form: *verisimilitude*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *verisimilitudo*, from *verus* (true) + *similis* (like) + *-tudo* (abstract noun suffix)
- Typical translations: verisimilitude, appearance of truth, plausibility, lifelikeness
- Usage: Refers to the quality of appearing to be true or real, especially in literary and artistic contexts where a work convincingly represents reality or seems lifelike and believable. Also used in philosophy of science to describe how closely a theory approximates truth. Primarily a formal and academic term.
- [MARGINAL]

**25. sound**
- Form: *sound* (adjective)
- Morphological breakdown: Old English *sund* (healthy, whole); also attested as *gesund*
- Typical translations: sound, valid, well-founded, reliable, correct
- Usage: Applied to arguments, reasoning, judgments, and advice to indicate that they are well-grounded, free from error, and based on solid principles. In formal logic, a *sound* argument is both valid and has true premises. In general usage, applied to reasoning, judgment, or structures to indicate reliability and freedom from flaw. Productive: *soundly*, *soundness*, *unsound*.
- [CORE]

**26. trustworthy**
- Form: *trustworthy*
- Morphological breakdown: *trust* (Old Norse *traust*, confidence) + *worthy* (deserving)
- Typical translations: trustworthy, reliable, dependable, honest
- Usage: Applied to persons, sources, and institutions to indicate that they can be relied upon to be honest and to act as expected. Commonly used in assessments of character and in evaluations of information sources. Productive: *trustworthiness*, *untrustworthy*.
- [CORE]

---

**27. fictitious**
- Form: *fictitious*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *ficticius* (artificial, feigned), from *fingere* (to shape, feign)
- Typical translations: fictitious, invented, imaginary, false, fabricated
- Usage: Applied to names, accounts, persons, and entities that are invented or fabricated rather than real. Used in legal contexts (e.g., *fictitious name*) and in literary contexts. Related to *fiction*, *fictive*. Productive: *fictitiously*, *fictitiousness*.
- [CORE]

**28. fabricated**
- Form: *fabricated*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *fabricatus*, past participle of *fabricare* (to construct), from *fabrica* (workshop); extended to mean invented or falsified
- Typical translations: fabricated, invented, made up, falsified, forged
- Usage: Applied to evidence, stories, and claims to indicate that they have been invented or falsified, typically with intent to deceive. Used in legal and journalistic contexts. Productive: *fabricate*, *fabrication*, *fabricator*.
- [CORE]

---

**29. rightful**
- Form: *rightful*
- Morphological breakdown: *right* (Old English *riht*) + *-ful* (having the quality of)
- Typical translations: rightful, legitimate, lawful, just, proper
- Usage: Applied to claims, ownership, and positions to indicate that they are held or exercised in accordance with right, law, or justice. Commonly used in legal and moral contexts to assert that something belongs to or is owed to someone by right. Productive: *rightfully*, *rightfulness*.
- [CORE]

**30. veridical**
- Form: *veridical*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *veridicus* (truth-telling), from *verus* (true) + *dicere* (to say)
- Typical translations: (source language)
- Usage: Used primarily in philosophical and psychological contexts to describe perceptions, reports, or representations that accurately correspond to reality. Contrasts with *illusory* or *hallucinatory* in perceptual contexts. Productive: *veridicality*, *veridically*.
- [MARGINAL]

**31. truthful**
- Form: *truthful*
- Morphological breakdown: *truth* (Old English *trēowþ*) + *-ful*
- Typical translations: (source language)
- Usage: Applied to persons or statements to indicate a disposition or tendency toward telling the truth, or that they tell or reflect the truth. Focuses on the agent's honesty rather than the correspondence of a specific proposition to fact. Distinguished from *true* in that it more commonly characterizes persons and their habitual disposition toward honesty. Productive: *truthfully*, *truthfulness*, *untruthful*.
- [CORE]

**32. honest**
- Form: *honest*
- Morphological breakdown: Old French *honeste*, from Latin *honestus* (honorable), from *honor*
- Typical translations: (source language)
- Usage: Applied to persons, statements, and conduct to indicate freedom from deception, fraud, or concealment. Broader in scope than *truthful*, encompassing fairness and integrity in dealings as well as verbal accuracy. Productive: *honesty*, *honestly*, *dishonest*, *dishonesty*.
- [CORE]

---

**33. faithful**
- Form: *faithful*
- Morphological breakdown: *faith* (Old French *feid*, Latin *fides* "trust") + *-ful*
- Typical translations: (source language)
- Usage: Applied to translations, reproductions, accounts, and persons to indicate close adherence to an original, a commitment, or a relationship. In evaluative contexts, often predicates accuracy of representation relative to a source. A *faithful* translation or reproduction preserves the essential features of its source. Productive: *faithfully*, *faithfulness*, *unfaithful*, *unfaithfulness*.
- [CORE]

**34. exact**
- Form: *exact*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *exactus* (driven out, precise), past participle of *exigere* (to drive out, demand, measure)
- Typical translations: (source language)
- Usage: Predicates precise correspondence or conformity, often with an implication of leaving no margin for error or approximation. Applied to measurements, reproductions, quotations, and descriptions to indicate complete correspondence with a standard or original, without deviation. Productive: *exactly*, *exactness*, *exactitude*, *inexact*.
- [CORE]

**35. verifiable**
- Form: *verifiable*
- Morphological breakdown: *verify* (Old French *verifier*, Medieval Latin *verificare*, from Latin *verus* "true" + *facere* "to make") + *-able*
- Typical translations: (source language)
- Usage: Predicates that a claim or statement is capable of being checked against evidence or observation. Commonly used in scientific, journalistic, and philosophical contexts. Productive: *verifiability*, *verify*, *verification*, *unverifiable*.
- [CORE]

**36. bona fides**
- Form: *bona fides*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin "good faith" (noun phrase)
- Typical translations: good faith, credentials, genuineness
- Usage: Used as a noun to refer to the sincerity or genuineness of a person's intentions, or to evidence establishing that someone is who they claim to be. Distinct in function from the adjectival *bona fide*, though sharing the same Latin source. Fixed expression.
- [CORE]

**37. ersatz**
- Form: *ersatz*
- Morphological breakdown: German *Ersatz* (replacement, substitute)
- Typical translations: (source language; borrowed)
- Usage: Applied to objects or experiences that are inferior substitutes for the genuine article, often with a connotation of inadequacy or artificiality. Borrowed into English from German, primarily in the twentieth century. Derivational productivity in English is limited.
- [CORE]

**38. pseudo-**
- Form: *pseudo-*
- Morphological breakdown: Greek *pseudēs* (false, lying)
- Typical translations: (source language; productive prefix)
- Usage: A productive prefix attached to nouns and adjectives to indicate that something falsely claims or resembles the category named (e.g., *pseudoscience*, *pseudo-intellectual*, *pseudonym*). Widely used in academic, scientific, and general registers. Not a free-standing lexical item but included as a highly productive bound form in this evaluative domain.
- [CORE]

**39. canonical**
- Form: *canonical*
- Morphological breakdown: Greek/Latin *canonicus* (according to rule), from *kanon* (rule, standard)
- Typical translations: (source language)
- Usage: Applied to texts, interpretations, versions, or examples that are recognized as authoritative, standard, or definitively representative of a category. Used in literary, religious, philosophical, and general academic contexts. Productive: *canonically*, *canon*, *canonize*, *canonization*.
- [CORE]

**40. specious**
- Form: *specious*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *speciosus* (good-looking, plausible), from *species* (appearance)
- Typical translations: (source language)
- Usage: Applied to arguments, claims, or reasoning that appear sound or well-founded on the surface but are actually flawed or misleading. Emphasizes the deceptive plausibility of the item being evaluated. Carries a negative evaluative force. Productive: *speciously*, *speciousness*.
- [CORE]

**41. bogus**
- Form: *bogus*
- Morphological breakdown: origin uncertain; possibly from a device for making counterfeit coins
- Typical translations: (source language)
- Usage: Applied to objects, claims, credentials, and persons that are not genuine or are fraudulent. More informal in register than *counterfeit* or *spurious*. Derives: no productive derivation widely attested.
- [CORE]

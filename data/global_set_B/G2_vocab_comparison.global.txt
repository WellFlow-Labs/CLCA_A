## Narrative Analysis

### Recurring Partition Patterns

**1. Propositional Truth versus Material Authenticity**

Six of eight languages show clear lexical or constructional distinctions between propositional truth (whether statements correspond to facts) and material authenticity (whether objects are genuine):

- **Chinese**: 实 (shí) for factual correspondence versus 真 (zhēn) for material authenticity, though both combine in 真实 (zhēnshí)
- **Greek**: αλήθεια (alítheia) for propositional truth versus γνήσιος (gnísios)/αυθεντικός (afthentikós) for material genuineness
- **English**: true for propositions versus genuine/authentic for objects
- **Tagalog**: totoo for factual truth versus tunay for genuineness/authenticity
- **Swahili**: kweli/ukweli for truth versus halisi for genuineness

**Exceptions**: 
- **Sanskrit** and **Quechua** show less clear lexical separation. Sanskrit सत्य (satya) and Quechua chiqan both span propositional and material domains without dedicated separate terms.
- **Hebrew** uses אמיתי (amiti) derived from the truth root for both domains.

This suggests a common semantic pressure to distinguish "statements matching facts" from "objects being genuine," but languages vary in whether this is lexicalized or handled constructionally.

**2. Correctness versus Error**

All eight languages show dedicated vocabulary for correctness and error, but with varying degrees of semantic specificity:

- **Binary correctness terms**: Chinese 对/错 (duì/cuò), Hebrew נכון/לא נכון (nakhon/lo nakhon), English right/wrong, Tagalog tama/mali, Swahili sahihi/makosa, Quechua allin/pantay
- **Precision/accuracy terms**: Chinese 准确 (zhǔnquè), Hebrew מדויק (meduyak), Greek ακριβής (akrivís), English accurate, Swahili madhubuti

**Notable pattern**: Most languages distinguish simple binary correctness (right/wrong answers) from gradable precision/accuracy. Chinese, Hebrew, Greek, and English show this most clearly through separate lexemes. Tagalog, Swahili, and Quechua show less elaboration in the precision domain.

**3. Honesty/Sincerity versus Deception**

All languages distinguish honest/sincere persons or speech from deceptive behavior, but through different lexical strategies:

- **Dedicated sincerity terms**: Chinese 诚 (chéng), Hebrew כנה (kene)/ειλικρινής (eilikrinís), Greek ειλικρινής (eilikrinís), English honest/sincere, Tagalog tapat, Swahili (uses halisi or Arabic sinsero)
- **Deception terms**: All languages have productive deception vocabulary (Chinese 欺骗, Hebrew רמאות, Sanskrit मिथ्या, Greek απατηλός, English deceive, Tagalog daya/linlang, Swahili -danganya, Quechua llullachiy)

**Asymmetry**: Several languages show richer vocabulary for deception and lying than for honesty. English has liar (suppletive) but no simple truth-teller. Greek has ψεύτης (pséftis) but no equivalent single-word truth-teller. This suggests negative moral evaluation may drive lexicalization more than positive.

### Fine-Grained Distinctions (Language-Specific Elaborations)

**1. Chinese Four-Way Root System**

Chinese uniquely elaborates the truth domain through four major roots with specialized distributions:

- **真 (zhēn)**: Authenticity of objects, genuineness of instances ("true friend"), material verification
- **实 (shí)**: Factual substantiality, practical implementation, concrete reality versus empty show
- **正 (zhèng)**: Normative correctness, orthodoxy, spatial/temporal precision
- **诚 (chéng)**: Personal sincerity, honesty in conduct

This creates distinctions not lexicalized elsewhere. For example, the 实/虚 (shí/xū) opposition (substantial versus empty/hollow) has no clear equivalent in other languages, though Quechua hunt'a/ch'usaq (full/empty) shows some parallel.

**2. Sanskrit Epistemological Elaboration**

Sanskrit uniquely develops extensive epistemological vocabulary distinguishing:

- **Valid knowledge sources** (प्रमाण pramāṇa): perception, inference, testimony, comparison
- **Valid versus erroneous cognition** (प्रमा/भ्रम pramā/bhrama)
- **Conventional versus ultimate truth** (व्यवहार/परमार्थ vyavahāra/paramārtha)
- **Knowledge versus ignorance** (विद्या/अविद्या vidyā/avidyā) in soteriological contexts

No other language shows comparable elaboration of epistemological categories. Greek shows some philosophical vocabulary (epistemic terms) but not the systematic pramāṇa-śāstra framework. This reflects Sanskrit's role as a philosophical metalanguage.

**3. Greek Pseudo-X Productivity**

Greek uniquely shows extensive ψευδ- (pseud-) compounding for creating critical/polemical terms:

- **Academic/intellectual**: ψευδοεπιστήμη (pseudoscience), ψευδοφιλόσοφος (pseudo-philosopher), ψευδοδιανοούμενος (pseudo-intellectual)
- **Political**: ψευδοδημοκρατία (pseudo-democracy), ψευδοπατριωτισμός (false patriotism)
- **Media**: ψευδοπληροφορία (misinformation), ψευδοείδηση (fake news)

English has borrowed pseudo- but shows much less productivity. Other languages lack equivalent productive patterns for "false pretenders to categories." This may reflect Greek's role in academic and critical discourse.

**4. Hebrew Root Polysemy**

Hebrew shows unique conflation within the א-מ-נ (ʾ-m-n) root family:

- **אמת (emet)**: Truth, factual accuracy
- **אמין (amin)**: Reliable, trustworthy
- **נאמן (ne'eman)**: Faithful, loyal
- **אמונה (emuna)**: Faith, belief, trust

This represents a major semantic merger: truth, reliability, faithfulness, and belief are treated as a single conceptual family. Other languages separate these more clearly (though Swahili haki shows some parallel conflation of truth/justice/rights).

### Mergers and Conflations

**1. Truth-Justice Conflation**

Two languages show significant conflation of truth/correctness with justice/rights:

- **Hebrew צדק (tsedek)**: Covers both "being right/correct" in arguments and "justice/righteousness" in moral/legal domains
- **Swahili haki** (from Arabic ḥaqq): Covers "right/truth" and "rights/justice/entitlement"

Other languages maintain clearer separation:
- Chinese has separate 正义 (zhèngyì) for justice versus 正确 (zhèngquè) for correctness
- Greek has separate δικαιοσύνη (dikaiosýni) for justice versus αλήθεια (alítheia) for truth
- English separates justice/fairness from truth/correctness

This suggests Hebrew and Swahili (via Arabic influence) conceptually link factual correctness with moral/legal rightness more tightly than other languages.

**2. Authenticity-Truth Merger**

Sanskrit and Quechua show less clear lexical separation between propositional truth and material authenticity:

- **Sanskrit सत्य (satya)**: Used for both "true statements" and "genuine objects/persons"
- **Quechua chiqan**: Covers "true statements," "genuine objects," and "authentic persons" without internal lexical distinction

This contrasts with the six languages that maintain separate terms (Chinese 真/实, Hebrew אמת/אמיתי, Greek αλήθεια/γνήσιος, English true/genuine, Tagalog totoo/tunay, Swahili kweli/halisi).

**3. Correctness Polysemy**

Several languages show single terms spanning multiple correctness domains:

- **English right**: Epistemic correctness (right answer), moral rightness (right action), practical appropriateness (right tool)
- **Quechua allin**: Good, well, correct across moral, practical, and epistemic domains
- **Chinese 正 (zhèng)**: Correctness, orthodoxy, spatial precision, temporal exactness

Other languages maintain clearer separations through multiple terms (Greek σωστός for factual correctness versus δίκαιος for moral rightness).

### Gaps and Missing Contrasts

**1. Agentive Nominalization Asymmetries**

Most languages show productive agentive formation for "liar" but gaps for "truth-teller":

- **Productive for liar**: Hebrew שקרן (shakran), Sanskrit मिथ्यावादिन् (mithyāvādin), Greek ψεύτης (pséftis), Tagalog sinungaling, Swahili mwongo, Quechua llullakuq
- **Gaps for truth-teller**: English (compound truth-teller, not *truther), Chinese (circumlocution 说真话的人), Hebrew (participial circumlocution), Tagalog (no dedicated form), Swahili (possessive mwenye ukweli), Quechua (compound chiqan rimaq)

Only Sanskrit shows productive agentive for both poles (सत्यवादिन् satyavādin "truth-speaker" and मिथ्यावादिन् mithyāvādin "liar").

This asymmetry suggests negative moral evaluation (lying) drives lexicalization more strongly than positive evaluation (truth-telling) across languages.

**2. Epistemological Validity Vocabulary**

Sanskrit uniquely elaborates epistemological validity:

- **Dedicated terms**: प्रमाण (pramāṇa) "valid means of knowledge," प्रमा (pramā) "valid cognition," भ्रम (bhrama) "erroneous cognition"
- **Systematic framework**: Six pramāṇas (perception, inference, testimony, etc.) with dedicated vocabulary

Other languages lack equivalent systematic epistemological vocabulary:
- Hebrew has some terms (מוכח mukhaḥ "proven") but no pramāṇa-śāstra equivalent
- Greek has philosophical terms but less systematic
- Chinese, English, Tagalog, Swahili, Quechua lack dedicated epistemological validity vocabulary

This reflects Sanskrit's specialized role as a philosophical metalanguage.

**3. Two-Truths Doctrine Vocabulary**

Only Sanskrit shows systematic lexicalization of conventional versus ultimate truth:

- **व्यवहार (vyavahāra)**: Conventional/practical reality
- **परमार्थ (paramārtha)**: Ultimate/absolute truth
- **Dedicated adverbials**: व्यवहारतः/परमार्थतः marking philosophical standpoint

No other language shows equivalent systematic distinction, though:
- Chinese has some philosophical register distinctions (理 lǐ "principle" versus 事 shì "affairs")
- Greek philosophical vocabulary shows some elaboration
- Other languages lack this framework entirely

**4. Evidential Integration**

Only Quechua shows obligatory grammatical evidential marking integrated with truth vocabulary:

- **Evidentials**: -mi (direct knowledge), -si (reportative), -cha (conjectural)
- **Integration**: chiqapmi "it is truly so (I know directly)" versus chiqapsi "it is reportedly true"

All other languages, including Swahili (also spoken in regions with evidential languages), lack grammatical evidentials. They use optional epistemic modals or adverbs instead.

### Constructional Strategies for Shared Meanings

**1. "Speak the Truth" Constructions**

All languages show highly conventionalized constructions for truth-telling, but with different structural patterns:

- **Accusative + verb**: Chinese 说实话, Tagalog magsabi ng totoo, Swahili sema ukweli, Quechua chiqanta rimay
- **Definite object**: Hebrew לומר את האמת (lomar et ha'emet), Greek λέω την αλήθεια (léo tin alítheia), English tell the truth
- **Bare noun + verb**: Sanskrit सत्यं वद (satyaṃ vada)

Despite structural differences, all show high conventionalization of this specific collocation, suggesting universal pragmatic importance of the "truth-telling" speech act.

**2. Causative Verification Strategies**

Languages show three main strategies for "making true/verifying":

- **Dedicated causative morphology**: Hebrew הצדיק (lehatsדik), Sanskrit साधयति (sādhayati), Greek βεβαιώνω (vevaióno), Tagalog patunayan, Swahili thibitisha, Quechua chiqanchay
- **Resultative compounds**: Chinese 证实 (zhèngshí), 落实 (luòshí)
- **Periphrastic with separate roots**: English verify/prove/authenticate (from Latin roots, not from true)

The English pattern is unusual: it lacks morphological causative derivation from truth-domain roots, instead using borrowed verbs from different etymological sources.

**3. Negation Strategies**

Languages show two main negation strategies with different distributions:

- **Morphological negation highly productive**: All languages (Chinese 不, Hebrew לא, Sanskrit अ-, Greek α-/δεν, English un-/in-, Tagalog hindi/di-, Swahili mana/si, Quechua mana)
- **Lexical antonyms coexist**: Most languages have both (Chinese 真/假 and 不真, English true/false and untrue, etc.)

**Pattern**: Morphological negation more productive for derived/abstract terms (Chinese 不正常, Hebrew לא מוצדק, English incorrect); lexical antonyms more common for basic oppositions (true/false, good/bad).

**4. Discourse Marker Development**

Seven languages show grammaticalization of truth vocabulary into discourse markers:

- **Introducing frank statements**: Chinese 说真的, Hebrew באמת, Greek αλήθεια?, English to be honest, Tagalog totoo lang, Swahili kwa kweli, Quechua chiqap
- **Seeking confirmation**: Hebrew נכון?, Greek ορθός?, Tagalog totoo ba?, Swahili kweli?
- **Expressing surprise**: Hebrew באמת?, Greek αλήθεια?, English really?

This represents a universal pragmatic pathway: truth-asserting terms grammaticalize into markers of speaker stance, sincerity, or epistemic attitude.

### Language-Specific Elaborations

**1. Chinese Substantiality Dimension**

Chinese uniquely elaborates a substantiality versus emptiness dimension through 实/虚 (shí/xū):

- **实 (shí)**: Solid, substantial, concrete, practical, implemented
- **虚 (xū)**: Empty, hollow, false, theoretical, unimplemented

This creates distinctions like:
- 华而不实 (huá ér bù shí) "flashy but lacking substance"
- 脚踏实地 (jiǎo tà shí dì) "feet on solid ground" = down-to-earth
- 虚虚实实 (xū xū shí shí) "mix of feint and reality" (military strategy)

No other language shows equivalent systematic elaboration of this dimension, though Quechua hunt'a/ch'usaq (full/empty) shows some parallel.

**2. Sanskrit Ontological-Epistemological Integration**

Sanskrit uniquely integrates ontological and epistemological concerns:

- **सत्/असत् (sat/asat)**: Being/non-being (ontological)
- **सत्य/मिथ्या (satya/mithyā)**: Truth/illusion (spans both domains)
- **प्रमा/भ्रम (pramā/bhrama)**: Valid/erroneous cognition (epistemological)
- **व्यवहार/परमार्थ (vyavahāra/paramārtha)**: Conventional/ultimate truth (metaphysical)

This creates a philosophical vocabulary where truth-evaluation operates simultaneously at ontological (what exists), epistemological (how we know), and metaphysical (levels of reality) levels. Other languages separate these domains more clearly.

**3. Greek Technical Domain Extensions**

Greek ορθ- (orth-) root uniquely extends into multiple technical domains:

- **Linguistic**: ορθογραφία (orthography), ορθοεπεία (orthoepy)
- **Religious**: ορθοδοξία (orthodoxy), ορθοπραξία (orthopraxy)
- **Medical**: ορθοπεδική (orthopedics), ορθοδοντική (orthodontics)
- **Geometric**: ορθογώνιος (right-angled), ορθογώνιο (rectangle)
- **Philosophical**: ορθολογισμός (rationalism)

This represents extensive semantic extension from "straight/correct" into specialized technical vocabularies. Other languages show less systematic technical elaboration from truth-domain roots.

**4. Tagalog Particle System**

Tagalog uniquely shows extensive particle system modulating truth-domain terms:

- **Interrogative**: ba (totoo ba? "is it true?")
- **Emphatic**: nga (totoo nga "it's really true")
- **Limitative**: lang (totoo lang "honestly, just the truth")
- **Concessive**: naman (totoo naman "it is true though")
- **Additive**: pa (totoo pa "even more true, what's more")

This creates fine-grained pragmatic distinctions not available through lexical or morphological means in other languages. The particle system allows modulation of illocutionary force and epistemic stance without changing core lexical items.

### Asymmetries in Semantic Coverage

**1. Justice Domain**

Languages vary in whether justice/righteousness is integrated with or separated from truth/correctness:

- **Integrated**: Hebrew צדק (tsedek), Swahili haki conflate truth and justice
- **Separated**: Chinese 正确 (correctness) versus 正义 (justice), Greek αλήθεια versus δικαιοσύνη, English truth versus justice
- **Absent**: Quechua lacks dedicated justice term (uses allin or Spanish préstamos like justicia)

**2. Reliability/Trustworthiness**

Languages vary in lexicalization of reliability as distinct from truth:

- **Dedicated terms**: Chinese 可靠 (kěkào), Hebrew אמין (amin)/נאמן (ne'eman), Greek αξιόπιστος (axiópistos), English reliable/trustworthy, Swahili madhubuti
- **Less elaborated**: Tagalog (uses mapagkakatiwalaan, complex derivation), Quechua (uses allin or circumlocution)
- **Root integration**: Hebrew integrates reliability into א-מ-נ truth root family

**3. Precision/Accuracy**

Languages vary in whether precision is lexically distinguished from simple correctness:

- **Clear distinction**: Chinese 准确 (zhǔnquè) versus 对 (duì), Hebrew מדויק (meduyak) versus נכון (nakhon), Greek ακριβής (akrivís) versus σωστός (sostós), English accurate versus correct
- **Less clear**: Tagalog (uses tumpak or eksakto, latter from Spanish), Swahili (uses madhubuti or kamili), Quechua (uses allin or hunt'a)

This suggests precision/accuracy as a distinct dimension is more elaborated in languages with strong scientific/technical registers.

### Constructional Compensation for Lexical Gaps

**1. Inchoative Meanings**

Languages lacking productive inchoative morphology use periphrastic constructions:

- **English**: come true (lexically restricted), become + adjective (general), prove + adjective (evidential)
- **Chinese**: 变 (biàn) + adjective (general), 成为 (chéngwéi) + noun (more formal)
- **Hebrew**: להפוך ל- (hafakh le-) "turn into" (general), התברר (hitbarer) "became clear" (evidential)

Languages with productive inchoative morphology use it freely:
- **Tagalog**: mag-ka- prefix (magkatotoo "come true")
- **Swahili**: -ika stative suffix (kamilika "become complete")
- **Quechua**: -ya- inchoative suffix (allinyay "become good")

**2. Agentive Meanings**

Languages lacking productive agentive nominalization use circumlocution:

- **English**: truth-teller (compound), liar (suppletive)
- **Chinese**: 说真话的人 (shuō zhēnhuà de rén) "person who speaks truth"
- **Hebrew**: דובר אמת (dover emet) "speaker of truth" (participial)
- **Swahili**: mwenye ukweli "one having truth" (possessive)

Languages with productive agentive patterns use them:
- **Sanskrit**: सत्यवादिन् (satyavādin) "truth-speaker"
- **Greek**: ψεύτης (pséftis) "liar" (though no single word for truth-teller)
- **Quechua**: llullakuq "liar" (agentive -q suffix)

### Summary of Major Typological Patterns

**1. Universal or Near-Universal Features**

- Morphological negation highly productive across all languages
- Abstract nominalization patterns present in all languages
- Speech act constructions ("speak truth/lies") highly conventionalized in all languages
- Discourse marker development from truth vocabulary in 7/8 languages
- Metaphorical revelation constructions (truth emerging/coming to light) in 6/8 languages

**2. Common but Not Universal Features**

- Propositional truth versus material authenticity distinction (6/8 languages)
- Dedicated causative morphology for verification (6/8 languages; English and Chinese use different strategies)
- Precision/accuracy distinguished from simple correctness (5/8 languages clearly)
- Honesty/sincerity terms separate from truth terms (7/8 languages; Quechua less clear)

**3. Rare or Unique Features**

- Two-truths doctrine vocabulary (Sanskrit only)
- Obligatory evidential marking (Quechua only)
- Extensive pseudo-X compounding (Greek uniquely productive)
- Four-way root system for truth domain (Chinese only)
- Truth-justice conflation (Hebrew and Swahili only)
- Substantiality versus emptiness elaboration (Chinese primarily; Quechua shows some parallel)

**4. Systematic Asymmetries**

- Agentive nominalization more productive for negative (liar) than positive (truth-teller) across languages
- Causative "make true/verify" more common than causative "make false" across languages
- Inchoative "become true" often restricted or uses different constructions than "become false"
- Deception vocabulary often more elaborate than honesty vocabulary

These patterns suggest:
1. **Negative moral evaluation** drives lexicalization more than positive
2. **Truth verification** is conceptualized as active process, while falsification is less agentive
3. **Truth emergence** is conceptualized differently from falsity emergence (revelation versus exposure)

# G5 — CROSS-LANGUAGE CONSTRUCTION COMPARISON

## 1. Main Recurring Constructional Functions

Based on F4 construction tables across all eight languages, the following constructional functions recur with varying degrees of systematicity:

### A. BASIC PREDICATION OF TRUTH-DOMAIN QUALITIES

All languages show constructions for attributing truth-domain properties to subjects (statements, objects, persons). Strategies vary:

- **Copular constructions**: Chinese 这是真的, Hebrew זה אמת, Greek αυτό είναι αλήθεια, English "this is true", Tagalog "totoo ang...", Swahili "ni kweli", Quechua "chiqan kay"
- **Zero copula**: Hebrew (present tense), Greek (present tense), Sanskrit (present tense optional)
- **Obligatory copula**: Chinese 是, English be, Tagalog copula inversion, Swahili ni/si

### B. NEGATION OF TRUTH-DOMAIN PREDICATION

All languages show systematic negation strategies:

- **Preverbal/proclitic negation**: Chinese 不是, Hebrew לא, Greek δεν, Tagalog hindi, Swahili si, Quechua mana
- **Negative copula**: Swahili si (dedicated negative copula), Tagalog hindi (negative copula form)
- **Coexistence with morphological negation**: Most languages show both analytic (not true) and synthetic (untrue) strategies

### C. ADNOMINAL MODIFICATION

All languages modify nouns with truth-domain terms, but strategies diverge:

- **Direct prenominal adjectives**: Chinese 真实的情况, Greek αληθινή ιστορία, English "true story"
- **Associative/genitive constructions**: Hebrew construct state, Tagalog linker na/ng, Swahili associative -a, Quechua juxtaposition
- **Postnominal possible but marked**: Greek, Hebrew (for emphasis)

### D. CAUSATIVE VERIFICATION/CONFIRMATION

Six languages show dedicated morphological causatives for "make true/verify":

- **Affixal causatives**: Hebrew hif'il, Greek -ώνω, Tagalog pa-, Swahili -isha, Quechua -cha-
- **Resultative compounds**: Chinese 证实, 落实
- **Periphrastic with separate roots**: English verify/prove/authenticate, Sanskrit uses separate roots

### E. INCHOATIVE/CHANGE-OF-STATE CONSTRUCTIONS

Languages show varying strategies for "become true/correct":

- **Dedicated inchoative morphology**: Tagalog mag-ka-, Swahili -ika, Quechua -ya-, Sanskrit भवति (context-dependent)
- **Periphrastic become**: Chinese 变, Hebrew להפוך, Greek γίνομαι, English become
- **Revelation/discovery constructions**: Hebrew התברר "became clear", Greek βγαίνει "comes out", English prove/turn out to be

### F. MANNER ADVERBIALS

All languages create adverbial expressions from truth-domain terms:

- **Suffixal adverbs**: Chinese -地, Greek -ά/-ως, English -ly
- **Prepositional phrases**: Hebrew ב-, Greek σε/κατά, Tagalog sa, Swahili kwa
- **Case marking**: Sanskrit ablative -तः, Quechua ablative -manta
- **Bare adverbial forms**: Chinese 真, Quechua chiqap

### G. DISCOURSE MANAGEMENT MARKERS

Seven languages (not Sanskrit) show grammaticalization into discourse markers:

- **Introducing frank statements**: Chinese 说真的, Hebrew באמת, Greek αλήθεια, English "to be honest", Tagalog "totoo lang", Swahili "kwa kweli", Quechua chiqap
- **Seeking confirmation**: Hebrew נכון?, Greek ορθός?, Tagalog "totoo ba?"
- **Expressing surprise**: Hebrew באמת?, Greek αλήθεια?, English "really?"

### H. LIGHT VERB + TRUTH-DOMAIN OBJECT

All languages show conventionalized verb-object constructions:

- **"Speak/tell truth"**: Universal pattern with language-specific verbs
- **"Make mistake/error"**: Present in 7/8 languages (not Sanskrit in same way)
- **Structural variation**: Definiteness marking, case marking, verb choice vary

### I. COMPARATIVE AND SUPERLATIVE CONSTRUCTIONS

All languages show comparison strategies:

- **Analytic comparatives**: Chinese 更, Hebrew יותר, Greek πιο/πιότερο, English more, Tagalog mas, Swahili zaidi
- **Analytic superlatives**: Chinese 最, Hebrew הכי, Greek πιο, English most, Tagalog pinaka-, Swahili -a pili
- **No synthetic forms**: None of the eight languages show synthetic comparative/superlative morphology for truth-domain terms

### J. INTENSIFICATION AND EMPHASIS

Languages show varying strategies:

- **Reduplication**: Chinese AABB pattern, Tagalog with linker, Greek/Swahili marginal
- **Intensifying particles**: Hebrew מאוד, Greek πολύ, Tagalog sobrang, Swahili sana, Quechua k'ari
- **Completive markers**: Greek kabisa, Swahili kabisa, Hebrew לחלוטין
- **Stacking**: Multiple languages allow stacking of emphatic elements

### K. EVIDENTIAL/EPISTEMIC MARKING

Only Quechua shows grammatical evidentials; others use optional strategies:

- **Obligatory evidentials**: Quechua -mi/-si/-cha
- **Optional epistemic modals**: All other languages use separate modal verbs or adverbs
- **Evidential framing**: Sanskrit uses instrumental case for epistemic means

### L. PASSIVE CONSTRUCTIONS

Six languages show productive passives of causative/transitive truth-domain verbs:

- **Morphological passives**: Hebrew, Greek, Tagalog, Swahili, Quechua
- **Periphrastic passives**: English be + past participle
- **Alternative strategies**: Chinese 被 construction

## 3. Narrative Analysis

### Recurring Cross-Language Constructional Patterns

**UNIVERSAL PATTERNS (All 8 Languages)**

**1. Basic Copular Attribution**

Every language shows a construction for predicating truth-domain qualities of subjects. The formal realization varies (zero copula vs. obligatory copula, word order differences), but the function is universal. This represents the most basic constructional need: asserting that something is true, correct, genuine, etc.

**Variation**: Zero copula in present tense (Hebrew, Greek, Sanskrit) versus obligatory copula (Chinese, English, Tagalog, Swahili, Quechua). Quechua uniquely adds obligatory evidential marking to all main clause predications.

**2. Systematic Negation**

All languages show productive negation strategies, typically with both analytic (not + adjective) and synthetic (un-/in- + adjective) options coexisting. The analytic strategy is universally productive; the synthetic strategy shows lexical restrictions in some languages.

**Pattern**: Morphological negation (prefixes/proclitics) more productive for derived/abstract terms; lexical antonyms more common for basic oppositions (true/false, good/bad).

**3. Light Verb Constructions for Speech Acts**

The "speak/tell truth" and "tell lies" constructions are universally conventionalized, despite structural differences. All languages treat truth-telling and lying as salient speech acts requiring dedicated expressions.

**Structural variation**: Definiteness marking (Hebrew, Greek, English require definite article), case marking (Sanskrit, Quechua use accusative), bare object (Chinese, Tagalog, Swahili). Despite variation, all show high frequency and conventionalization.

**Extension**: The pattern extends to "make mistake/error" in 7/8 languages, suggesting error-making is similarly salient across cultures.

**4. Adverbial Derivation**

All languages productively create manner adverbs from truth-domain terms, though strategies vary (suffixation, prepositional phrases, case marking). This represents universal need to modify actions with manner specifications (speak truly, act honestly, measure accurately).

**Grammaticalization**: Many adverbial forms grammaticalize into discourse markers (Hebrew באמת, Greek αλήθεια, English really, Tagalog totoo lang), showing a universal pragmatic pathway.

**5. Abstract Nominalization**

All languages show productive abstract nominalization (truth, honesty, accuracy as abstract concepts). Formal strategies vary (suffixation, circumfixation, periphrasis), but functional equivalence is clear. This represents universal need for philosophical and formal vocabulary.

**WIDELY ATTESTED PATTERNS (6-7 Languages)**

**1. Causative Verification Morphology**

Six languages show dedicated causative morphology for "make true/verify/confirm." The two exceptions use alternative strategies:

- **Chinese**: Resultative verb compounds (证实, 落实) instead of affixal causatives
- **English**: Separate borrowed roots (verify, prove, authenticate) instead of derivation from true

This suggests causative verification is a universal constructional need, but languages vary in whether they derive it from truth-domain roots or use separate lexemes.

**Asymmetry**: Causatives for verification are much more productive than causatives for falsification across all languages. This suggests truth-establishment is conceptualized as active process requiring dedicated morphology, while falsification is achieved through other means (deception verbs, corruption verbs).

**2. Discourse Marker Grammaticalization**

Seven languages (all except classical Sanskrit) show active grammaticalization of truth-domain terms into discourse markers. Functions include:

- Introducing frank statements (Chinese 说真的, English "to be honest")
- Seeking confirmation (Hebrew נכון?, Greek ορθός?)
- Expressing surprise (Hebrew באמת?, Greek αλήθεια?)

This represents a universal pragmatic pathway active in all living languages. The pathway: truth-assertion → speaker stance marker → discourse management particle.

**Sanskrit exception**: As a classical language with limited colloquial development, Sanskrit lacks this pragmatic grammaticalization. This suggests the pathway requires ongoing spoken language evolution.

**3. Passive Derivation**

Six languages show productive passive derivation from causative/transitive truth-domain verbs. Common in formal/legal registers for verification statements ("was proven," "has been confirmed").

**Exceptions**: Chinese uses 被 bèi construction rather than morphological passive. Sanskrit shows passive forms but less systematically in truth domain.

**4. Revelation/Emergence Metaphors**

Six languages use metaphorical constructions for truth revelation (truth coming out, coming to light, emerging):

- Chinese 真相大白 "truth becomes bright"
- Hebrew האמת תצא לאור "truth will come to light"
- Greek βγαίνει η αλήθεια στη φόρα "truth comes to surface"
- English "the truth will out"
- Tagalog lumabas ang katotohanan
- Swahili ukweli unadhihirika

**Less prominent**: Sanskrit (uses passive revelation less systematically), Quechua (less elaborated).

This suggests a common conceptual metaphor: TRUTH IS HIDDEN OBJECT THAT EMERGES. The metaphor appears cross-linguistically but is not universal.

### Important Asymmetries and Restrictions

**1. Agentive Nominalization Asymmetry**

**Pattern**: Productive agentive formation for "liar/deceiver" but gaps or circumlocution for "truth-teller" in most languages.

**Evidence**:
- **Productive negative**: Hebrew שקרן, Sanskrit मिथ्यावादिन्, Greek ψεύτης, Tagalog sinungaling, Swahili mwongo, Quechua llullakuq, English liar (suppletive)
- **Gaps positive**: English (compound truth-teller), Chinese (circumlocution), Hebrew (participial phrase), Tagalog (no form), Swahili (possessive phrase)
- **Productive both**: Sanskrit सत्यवादिन्, Quechua chiqan rimaq

**Interpretation**: This represents a systematic cross-linguistic asymmetry. Negative moral agents are more readily lexicalized than positive ones. Only Sanskrit and Quechua show productive formation for both poles.

**Cognitive/social explanation**: Negative agents (liars, deceivers) may be more salient for social monitoring and warning. Positive behavior may be seen as default or less marked, not requiring dedicated agent nouns.

**2. Causative Verification vs. Falsification Asymmetry**

**Pattern**: Causatives for "make true/verify" are productive; causatives for "make false/falsify" are rare or use different strategies.

**Evidence**:
- **Verification productive**: Hebrew אימת, Greek βεβαιώνω, Tagalog patunayan, Swahili thibitisha, Quechua chiqanchay
- **Falsification rare**: Most languages lack productive causative "make false" from false/lie roots
- **Alternative strategies**: English falsify (different root), Greek uses deception verbs, others describe falsification through corruption/deception

**Interpretation**: Truth-establishment is conceptualized as active process requiring causative morphology. Falsification is conceptualized differently—as passive state, as achieved through deception (different verb class), or as corruption/destruction rather than direct causation.

**3. Inchoative "Become True" Restrictions**

**Pattern**: Even in languages with productive inchoative morphology, "become true" shows restrictions not seen with "become good/better."

**Evidence**:
- **English**: come true (fixed), *come false (ungrammatical), *become true (marked)
- **Chinese**: 变好 "become good" productive, 变真 "become true" marginal
- **Hebrew**: Uses התברר "became clear" (different root) rather than inchoative of אמת
- **Productive languages**: Tagalog, Swahili, Quechua allow inchoatives but often prefer revelation constructions

**Interpretation**: "Becoming true" is conceptualized as revelation or discovery of pre-existing truth rather than truth coming into being. Languages prefer:
- Revelation metaphors (truth emerging, coming to light)
- Evidential constructions (proved true, turned out to be true)
- Realization of predictions (dreams came true)

This suggests truth is seen as pre-existing and discovered rather than created or achieved.

**4. Positive vs. Negative Elaboration**

**Pattern**: Across multiple constructional domains, negative pole (lying, deception, error, fakeness) shows richer elaboration than positive pole (truth-telling, honesty, correctness, genuineness).

**Evidence**:
- **Agentive nouns**: Liar more readily lexicalized than truth-teller
- **Deception vocabulary**: Multiple languages show elaborate deception vocabulary (Chinese 欺骗/诈骗/蒙骗, Greek απάτη/δόλος, Tagalog daya/linlang/palabás)
- **Error vocabulary**: Elaborate in most languages
- **Honesty vocabulary**: Less elaborate; often uses general "good person" terms

**Interpretation**: Negative moral evaluation and threat detection may drive lexicalization and constructional elaboration more than positive evaluation. This appears to be a universal cognitive or social bias.

### Substitutions and Constructional Alternatives

**1. Causative Strategies: Affixation vs. Compounding vs. Separate Roots**

Languages show three main strategies for causative verification, apparently in complementary distribution:

- **Affixal causatives**: Hebrew, Greek, Tagalog, Swahili, Quechua (6 languages)
- **Resultative compounds**: Chinese (1 language)
- **Separate roots**: English, Sanskrit (2 languages)

**Pattern**: Languages with productive causative morphology apply it to truth-domain. Languages without productive causatives (Chinese, English) use alternative strategies. Sanskrit has causative morphology but doesn't apply it systematically to truth-domain, using separate roots instead.

**Interpretation**: The function (establishing truth through active process) is universal, but formal realization depends on available morphological resources.

**2. Inchoative Strategies: Morphology vs. Periphrasis vs. Revelation**

Languages show three main strategies for change-of-state, with varying preferences:

- **Dedicated inchoative morphology**: Tagalog mag-ka-, Swahili -ika, Quechua -ya- (productive)
- **Periphrastic become**: Chinese 变, Hebrew להפוך, Greek γίνομαι, English become (productive)
- **Revelation/discovery constructions**: Hebrew התברר, Greek βγαίνει, English prove/turn out (often preferred over simple inchoative)

**Pattern**: Even languages with productive inchoative morphology often prefer revelation/discovery constructions for truth-domain. This suggests "becoming true" is conceptually distinct from general change-of-state.

**3. Adnominal Modification: Direct Adjectives vs. Associative Constructions**

Languages show two main strategies for modifying nouns with truth-domain terms:

- **Direct prenominal adjectives**: Chinese, Greek, English (standard in these languages)
- **Associative/genitive constructions**: Hebrew construct state, Tagalog linker, Swahili associative, Quechua juxtaposition (standard in these languages)

**Pattern**: The choice appears to correlate with general language typology rather than truth-domain specifics. Isolating and fusional languages prefer direct modification; Semitic, Austronesian, Bantu, and Quechuan languages prefer associative strategies.

**4. Adverbial Formation: Suffixation vs. Prepositional Phrases vs. Case Marking**

Languages show three main strategies for creating manner adverbs:

- **Suffixation**: Chinese -地, Greek -ά/-ως, English -ly (productive)
- **Prepositional phrases**: Hebrew ב-, Greek σε/κατά, Tagalog sa, Swahili kwa (productive)
- **Case marking**: Sanskrit ablative -तः, Quechua ablative -manta (productive)

**Pattern**: Languages use available morphological resources. Suffixation common in isolating and fusional languages; prepositional phrases in languages with rich prepositional systems; case marking in languages with extensive case systems.

**Overlap**: Some languages show multiple strategies (Greek has both suffixal adverbs and prepositional phrases; Quechua has both case marking and adverbial suffixes).

### Weakly Expressed or Constructionally Indirect Functions

**1. Causative Falsification**

**Observation**: While causative verification is productive in 6/8 languages, causative falsification is rare or uses different strategies across all languages.

**Evidence**:
- Most languages lack productive causative "make false" from false/lie roots
- Alternative strategies: Deception verbs (deceive, mislead), corruption verbs (corrupt, pervert), or separate roots (English falsify)
- Greek shows some symmetry (διαψεύδω "disprove") but this is exceptional

**Interpretation**: Falsification is not conceptualized as direct causation of falsity but rather as:
- Deception (causing false belief)
- Corruption (destroying truth)
- Forgery (creating false documents)

This suggests falsification is constructionally indirect: languages describe the means (deceiving, corrupting, forging) rather than the result (making false) directly.

**2. Positive Agentive Nominalization**

**Observation**: Most languages lack productive agentive formation for "truth-teller" and use circumlocution or compounds.

**Evidence**:
- English: truth-teller (marginal compound, not productive *truther)
- Chinese: 说真话的人 (circumlocution: "person who speaks truth")
- Hebrew: דובר אמת (participial phrase: "speaker of truth")
- Tagalog: No dedicated form
- Swahili: mwenye ukweli (possessive: "one having truth")

**Interpretation**: Positive moral agents are constructionally indirect in most languages. They are described through:
- Participial phrases (one who speaks truth)
- Possessive constructions (one having truth)
- Compounds (truth-speaker)

Rather than dedicated agentive derivation. This contrasts with productive negative agentives (liar).

**3. Inchoative "Become True"**

**Observation**: Even languages with productive inchoative morphology show restrictions or preferences for alternative constructions.

**Evidence**:
- **Lexical restrictions**: English come true (fixed), *come false (ungrammatical)
- **Marginal status**: Chinese 变真 (marginal), Hebrew prefers התברר "became clear"
- **Alternative preferences**: Most languages prefer revelation/discovery constructions (truth emerged, was revealed, came to light) over simple inchoative

**Interpretation**: "Becoming true" is constructionally indirect. Languages prefer to describe:
- Revelation (truth emerging, coming to light)
- Discovery (truth was found, became known)
- Verification (proved true, turned out to be true)

Rather than simple change-of-state "became true." This suggests truth is conceptualized as pre-existing and revealed rather than coming into being.

**4. Maintaining Truth Over Time**

**Observation**: Most languages lack dedicated constructions for "keep/maintain truth" and use general maintenance verbs or stative constructions.

**Evidence**:
- **General maintenance verbs**: Chinese 保持, Hebrew שמר, Greek διατηρώ, English keep/maintain
- **Stative constructions**: "Remains true" (Hebrew נשאר, Greek μένει, English remains) more common than active maintenance
- **Marginal**: Dedicated "preserve truth" constructions less common than "preserve authenticity" (for documents, artifacts)

**Interpretation**: Maintaining truth is constructionally indirect. Languages describe:
- Continued state (remains true)
- General preservation (keep, maintain)
- Resistance to change (doesn't change)

Rather than dedicated truth-maintenance constructions. This contrasts with productive verification and revelation constructions.

**5. Preventing Lies/Errors**

**Observation**: While prohibitives ("don't lie") are productive, dedicated constructions for preventing others from lying are rare or complex.

**Evidence**:
- **Prohibitives productive**: All languages show "don't lie" constructions (Quechua ama llullakychu, Greek μην ψεύδεσαι, etc.)
- **Preventing others**: Complex periphrastic constructions (Greek τον αποτρέπω από το να πει ψέματα "I prevent him from telling lies")
- **Alternative strategies**: Languages describe preventing deception through general prevention verbs rather than dedicated constructions

**Interpretation**: Preventing lies in others is constructionally indirect. Languages use:
- General prevention verbs (prevent, stop)
- Prohibitive commands (don't lie)
- Deception-blocking constructions

Rather than dedicated "make someone not lie" causatives.

### Cases Where One Strategy Replaces Another

**1. Chinese Resultative Compounds Replace Affixal Causatives**

Chinese uses resultative verb compounds (改正 "change-correct", 证实 "prove-solid") where other languages use affixal causatives (Hebrew hif'il, Greek -ώνω, Tagalog pa-, Swahili -isha, Quechua -cha-).

**Pattern**: Chinese lacks productive causative affixation in general and compensates through resultative compounding. This represents a typological substitution: compounding strategy replaces affixation strategy for same function (causative verification/correction).

**2. English Separate Roots Replace Causative Derivation**

English uses separate borrowed roots (verify, prove, authenticate, falsify) where other languages derive causatives from truth-domain roots.

**Pattern**: English lacks productive causative morphology in general (except -ify, which is borrowed and restricted). It compensates by borrowing Latin/French verbs for causative functions. This represents lexical substitution: separate roots replace derivational morphology.

**3. Revelation Constructions Replace Simple Inchoatives**

Most languages prefer revelation/discovery constructions (truth emerged, came to light, was revealed) over simple inchoative "became true."

**Pattern**: Even languages with productive inchoative morphology (Tagalog, Swahili, Quechua) show this preference. This represents constructional substitution: metaphorical revelation replaces literal change-of-state for truth-domain.

**Interpretation**: Truth is conceptualized as pre-existing and hidden rather than coming into being. Revelation metaphors are more natural than creation metaphors.

**4. Circumlocution Replaces Agentive Derivation (Positive Agents)**

Most languages use circumlocution (participial phrases, possessive constructions, compounds) for "truth-teller" where they have productive agentive derivation for "liar."

**Pattern**: 
- **Negative agents**: Productive morphology (Hebrew -an, Greek -της, Swahili m-, Quechua -q)
- **Positive agents**: Circumlocution (Chinese 说真话的人, Hebrew דובר אמת, Swahili mwenye ukweli)

This represents functional substitution: periphrastic description replaces morphological derivation for positive agents.

**5. Discourse Markers Replace Literal Adverbs**

In all living languages, truth-domain adverbs grammaticalize into discourse markers with weakened or shifted meanings:

- Hebrew באמת: "in truth" → "really?" (surprise marker)
- Greek αλήθεια: "truth" → "really?" (confirmation seeker)
- English really: "in a real manner" → intensifier/surprise marker
- Tagalog totoo lang: "true only" → "honestly" (frank statement introducer)

**Pattern**: Literal manner adverbs (truly, really, honestly) shift to pragmatic discourse management functions. This represents semantic substitution: pragmatic function replaces or supplements literal meaning.

### Summary of Major Findings

**UNIVERSAL CONSTRUCTIONAL NEEDS** (All 8 Languages):

1. Basic predication of truth-domain qualities
2. Systematic negation strategies
3. Adnominal modification
4. Adverbial derivation
5. Abstract nominalization
6. Light verb constructions for speech acts
7. Comparative/superlative constructions
8. Interrogative constructions
9. Conditional constructions

**WIDELY ATTESTED PATTERNS** (6-7 Languages):

1. Causative verification morphology (6/8)
2. Discourse marker grammaticalization (7/8)
3. Passive derivation (6/8)
4. Prepositional/case adverbials (6/8)
5. Revelation/emergence metaphors (6/8)

**SYSTEMATIC ASYMMETRIES**:

1. **Agentive nominalization**: Productive for negative (liar) but blocked/circumlocutory for positive (truth-teller) in 6/8 languages
2. **Causative directionality**: Verification productive, falsification rare or uses different strategies
3. **Inchoative restrictions**: "Become true" restricted or uses revelation constructions instead
4. **Elaboration bias**: Negative evaluation (lying, deception, error) more elaborated than positive (honesty, truth-telling)

**CONSTRUCTIONAL SUBSTITUTIONS**:

1. Chinese resultative compounds replace affixal causatives
2. English separate roots replace causative derivation
3. Revelation metaphors replace simple inchoatives for truth
4. Circumlocution replaces agentive derivation for positive agents
5. Discourse markers replace or supplement literal adverbial meanings

**LANGUAGE-SPECIFIC ELABORATIONS**:

1. Greek pseudo-X compounding (critical discourse)
2. Sanskrit ablative standpoint marking (philosophical framework)
3. Quechua evidential integration (typologically rare)
4. Hebrew binyan alternations (Semitic morphology)
5. Chinese resultative compounds (characteristic strategy)
6. Tagalog particle modulation (agglutinative strategy)

These patterns reveal both universal constructional needs (predication, negation, modification, causation) and language-specific morphological resources (affixation, compounding, case marking, particles) that shape how truth-domain meanings are expressed. The systematic asymmetries (agentive, causative, inchoative) suggest universal cognitive or conceptual biases in how truth and falsehood are understood across cultures.

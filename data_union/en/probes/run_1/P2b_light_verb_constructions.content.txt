# CLCA Protocol v3.4 — P2b Compositional Constructions
# Language: English (en)

---

**Note on scope:** This step documents attested multi-word constructions involving the 30 P1 base lexical items. Derivational forms already documented in P2a are excluded. Constructions documented in P1b (root expansion) are cross-referenced rather than re-described at length, since P1b already captured many fixed expressions. The focus here is on periphrastic, idiomatic, and syntactically-driven multi-word patterns not already fully treated. Transliteration is not applicable for English (Latin script native); the transliteration field is omitted throughout.

## Item 1: *true*

---

**C1.1**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *true to one's word*
- **English translation:** keeping one's promise, doing what one said one would do
- **Usage notes:** Applied to persons who fulfill commitments they have made. Used across all registers to commend reliability and integrity in following through on stated intentions.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.2**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *true to oneself*
- **English translation:** acting in accordance with one's own values and character, not compromising one's identity
- **Usage notes:** Used in philosophical, self-help, and everyday discourse to describe a person who acts in accordance with their authentic values rather than conforming to external pressure. Related to existentialist notions of authenticity.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.3**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *out of true*
- **English translation:** not straight, not properly aligned, deviating from the correct position
- **Usage notes:** Used in engineering, carpentry, and mechanical contexts to describe a surface, wheel, or component that is not correctly aligned or level. The sense is geometric accuracy rather than propositional truth.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.4**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *as true as*
- **English translation:** just as accurate or genuine as (something used as a standard of comparison)
- **Usage notes:** Used in comparative constructions to assert equivalence in truth or genuineness (*as true as anything I've ever said*). Common in informal and rhetorical speech.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.5**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *the true story*
- **English translation:** the actual account of events, as opposed to a fictional or distorted version
- **Usage notes:** A fixed attributive construction used in publishing, journalism, and everyday speech to signal that a narrative is factual rather than invented. Extremely common in book and film titles and promotional contexts.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.6**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *true to say*
- **English translation:** accurate to state, correct to assert
- **Usage notes:** Used as a parenthetical or introductory phrase to acknowledge the accuracy of a statement, often before introducing a qualification (*it is true to say that progress has been made, but...*). Common in formal and journalistic writing.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.7**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *it is true that*
- **English translation:** it is a fact that, it is the case that
- **Usage notes:** A standard propositional construction used to assert the truth of a following clause. Functions both as a straightforward assertion and as a concessive opener before a contrasting point. Extremely common across all registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.8**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *that's true*
- **English translation:** that is correct, I agree with that
- **Usage notes:** A fixed conversational response used to affirm the accuracy or correctness of a preceding statement. One of the most frequent truth-affirming expressions in spoken English.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.9**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *true enough*
- **English translation:** admittedly correct, fair enough, accurate as far as it goes
- **Usage notes:** Already documented in P1b (1.17). Cross-reference only.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.10**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *too true*
- **English translation:** unfortunately very accurate, regrettably correct
- **Usage notes:** Already documented in P1b (1.18). Cross-reference only.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.11**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *only too true*
- **English translation:** regrettably and entirely accurate
- **Usage notes:** An emphatic variant of *too true*, used to affirm with resignation or regret that something unpleasant is indeed the case. More emphatic than *too true* alone. Common in informal and journalistic usage.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.12**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *true enough, but*
- **English translation:** that is correct as far as it goes, however
- **Usage notes:** A concessive construction used to acknowledge the partial validity of a claim before introducing a counterpoint or qualification. Common in argumentation and debate across all registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

> **Note on *true*:** Many fixed expressions involving *true* were already documented in P1b (1.7–1.30), including *true-blue*, *true-life*, *true north*, *true to life*, *true to form*, *true to type*, *come true*, *ring true*, *hold true*, *prove true*, *truth be told*, *the truth of the matter*, *moment of truth*, *ground truth*, *home truth*, *half-truth*, and *post-truth*. Those are not repeated here. The constructions above supplement that inventory with additional attested patterns.

## Item 2: *truth*

---

**C2.1**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *tell the truth*
- **English translation:** to state what is actually the case, to be honest
- **Usage notes:** A fundamental fixed verb-object construction used across all registers to describe the act of honest disclosure. Appears in legal contexts (*tell the truth, the whole truth*), moral instruction, and everyday speech.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.2**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *the whole truth*
- **English translation:** the complete and unabridged facts, without omission
- **Usage notes:** Used in legal and moral contexts to emphasize that nothing relevant has been withheld. Appears in the courtroom oath (*the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth*) and in everyday emphasis on complete disclosure.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.3**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *nothing but the truth*
- **English translation:** only what is true, with no admixture of falsehood
- **Usage notes:** Used in legal oaths and in everyday emphasis on strict truthfulness. Most commonly encountered as part of the full courtroom formula (*the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth*).
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.4**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *speak the truth*
- **English translation:** to say what is true, to be honest
- **Usage notes:** A slightly more formal or literary variant of *tell the truth*, used in moral, religious, and rhetorical contexts. Common in biblical and ethical discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.5**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *speaking truth to power*
- **English translation:** honestly confronting those in authority with uncomfortable facts
- **Usage notes:** A fixed phrase used in political and activist discourse to describe the act of honestly challenging authority with inconvenient facts. Originated in Quaker tradition and became widely used in political commentary.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.6**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *the truth will out*
- **English translation:** the truth will eventually become known, facts cannot be permanently concealed
- **Usage notes:** A proverbial expression asserting that concealed facts will eventually come to light. Used across all registers, often as a reassurance or warning.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.7**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *in truth*
- **English translation:** actually, in fact, to be honest
- **Usage notes:** A prepositional phrase used as a discourse marker to introduce a frank or corrective statement, often contrasting with appearances or prior claims (*in truth, the situation was far worse than reported*). Common in formal and literary registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.8**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *to tell the truth*
- **English translation:** to be honest, frankly speaking
- **Usage notes:** Used as a parenthetical or introductory phrase to signal that the speaker is about to make a candid statement, sometimes one they might otherwise hesitate to make. Common in spoken and informal written English.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.9**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *the truth hurts*
- **English translation:** accurate information is sometimes painful to hear
- **Usage notes:** A proverbial expression used to acknowledge that honest statements can be uncomfortable or unwelcome. Common in everyday speech and moral discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.10**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *stretch the truth*
- **English translation:** to exaggerate or distort facts without outright lying
- **Usage notes:** Used to describe statements that are not entirely false but that exaggerate or distort the facts. Common in everyday and journalistic usage to describe mild dishonesty that falls short of outright lying.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.11**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *bend the truth*
- **English translation:** to distort or misrepresent facts without outright lying
- **Usage notes:** Used similarly to *stretch the truth* to describe statements that distort rather than directly contradict the facts. Implies deliberate but partial misrepresentation. Common in everyday and journalistic usage.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.12**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *economical with the truth*
- **English translation:** deliberately omitting relevant facts, misleading through selective disclosure
- **Usage notes:** A fixed phrase used in British English, particularly in political and journalistic discourse, to describe a person or statement that misleads through omission rather than outright falsehood. Often used ironically or euphemistically.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.13**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *a grain of truth*
- **English translation:** a small element of accuracy within a largely false or exaggerated claim
- **Usage notes:** Used to acknowledge that a claim, though largely inaccurate or exaggerated, contains some factual basis. Common in everyday and journalistic discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.14**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *kernel of truth*
- **English translation:** a small but genuine element of accuracy within a larger claim
- **Usage notes:** Used similarly to *a grain of truth*, with slightly more emphasis on the genuine core element. Common in analytical and journalistic discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.15**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *the truth of the matter*
- **English translation:** the actual facts of the situation
- **Usage notes:** Already documented in P1b (1.29). Cross-reference only.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.16**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *truth be told*
- **English translation:** to be honest, frankly speaking
- **Usage notes:** Already documented in P1b (1.28). Cross-reference only.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.17**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *gospel truth*
- **English translation:** something regarded as absolutely and unquestionably true
- **Usage notes:** Used to assert that something is completely and certainly true, with the force of religious certainty. Common in informal and emphatic speech (*I'm telling you the gospel truth*).
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.18**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *the plain truth*
- **English translation:** the simple, unadorned facts without embellishment
- **Usage notes:** Used to emphasize that a statement is straightforwardly factual, without rhetorical elaboration or softening. Common in direct and informal speech.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.19**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *the naked truth*
- **English translation:** the unvarnished, unembellished facts
- **Usage notes:** Used to emphasize that a statement presents the facts without softening, concealment, or embellishment. More emphatic than *the plain truth*. Common in rhetorical and journalistic usage.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.20**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *the uncomfortable truth*
- **English translation:** a fact that is accurate but unwelcome or difficult to accept
- **Usage notes:** Used in journalistic, political, and everyday discourse to describe accurate information that is difficult or unpleasant to acknowledge. Common in commentary and analysis.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.21**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *the inconvenient truth*
- **English translation:** an accurate fact that is unwelcome because it requires difficult action or acknowledgment
- **Usage notes:** Used in political and environmental discourse, widely associated with Al Gore's 2006 documentary. Now used more broadly to describe any accurate but unwelcome fact that challenges comfortable assumptions.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.22**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *truth drug* / *truth serum*
- **English translation:** a substance believed to compel honest disclosure
- **Usage notes:** Used in espionage, legal, and popular culture contexts to refer to substances (such as sodium pentothal) believed to reduce a person's ability to withhold truthful information. *Truth serum* is more common in popular usage; *truth drug* is more common in British English.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.23**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *there's truth in*
- **English translation:** there is some accuracy in, there is a valid element to
- **Usage notes:** A construction used to acknowledge partial validity in a claim or observation (*there's truth in what you say*). Common in conversational and analytical discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

## Item 3: *accurate*

---

**C3.1**
- **Base Form:** *accurate*
- **Example:** *accurate to within*
- **English translation:** correct within a specified margin of error
- **Usage notes:** Used in scientific, technical, and measurement contexts to specify the degree of precision of a measurement or instrument (*accurate to within one millimeter*, *accurate to within 0.1%*). Common in technical and scientific writing.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C3.2**
- **Base Form:** *accurate*
- **Example:** *broadly accurate*
- **English translation:** correct in its general outlines, though possibly imprecise in detail
- **Usage notes:** Used to acknowledge that a description or account is substantially correct without asserting complete precision. Common in journalistic and analytical writing when assessing the overall correctness of a claim.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C3.3**
- **Base Form:** *accurate*
- **Example:** *reasonably accurate*
- **English translation:** sufficiently correct for practical purposes, approximately right
- **Usage notes:** Used to indicate that something is correct within acceptable limits, without claiming complete precision. Common in scientific, journalistic, and everyday contexts.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C3.4**
- **Base Form:** *accurate*
- **Example:** *factually accurate*
- **English translation:** correct with respect to the facts, not containing factual errors
- **Usage notes:** Used in journalistic, legal, and academic contexts to specify that a claim or account is free from factual errors, as distinct from being accurate in other respects (e.g., tone, interpretation). Common in editorial and legal assessments.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C3.5**
- **Base Form:** *accurate*
- **Example:** *historically accurate*
- **English translation:** correct with respect to historical facts
- **Usage notes:** Used in assessments of historical narratives, films, and accounts to indicate that they correctly represent historical events and conditions. Common in academic, journalistic, and popular criticism.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C3.6**
- **Base Form:** *accurate*
- **Example:** *technically accurate*
- **English translation:** correct in technical terms, though possibly misleading in context
- **Usage notes:** Used to acknowledge that a statement is correct in a narrow technical sense while implying that it may be misleading or incomplete in a broader context. Common in legal, journalistic, and analytical discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

> **Note on *accurate*:** No idiomatic or fixed-phrase constructions specific to *accurate* beyond the adjectival modification patterns above are identified. The word participates in standard predicative and attributive constructions but does not generate distinctive fixed expressions in the way that *true* and *truth* do.

## Item 4: *genuine*

---

**C4.1**
- **Base Form:** *genuine*
- **Example:** *the genuine article*
- **English translation:** the real thing, not an imitation or substitute
- **Usage notes:** A fixed noun phrase used to assert that something is the authentic original rather than a copy or imitation. Common in everyday, commercial, and journalistic usage (*this is the genuine article, not a reproduction*).
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C4.2**
- **Base Form:** *genuine*
- **Example:** *genuine concern*
- **English translation:** real and sincere worry or interest, not feigned
- **Usage notes:** A common collocational construction used to distinguish sincere concern from performative or strategic expressions of concern. Common in political, journalistic, and everyday discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C4.3**
- **Base Form:** *genuine*
- **Example:** *genuine surprise*
- **English translation:** real and unfeigned astonishment
- **Usage notes:** Used to distinguish authentic emotional reaction from performed or strategic surprise. Common in journalistic and everyday discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C4.4**
- **Base Form:** *genuine*
- **Example:** *genuine mistake*
- **English translation:** an error made in good faith, not a deliberate deception
- **Usage notes:** Used to distinguish an honest error from a deliberate misrepresentation. Common in legal, professional, and everyday contexts where intent is relevant.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C4.5**
- **Base Form:** *genuine*
- **Example:** *genuine belief*
- **English translation:** a sincerely held conviction, not a pretended or strategic position
- **Usage notes:** Used in legal, philosophical, and everyday contexts to distinguish sincere conviction from feigned or strategic assertion. Common in assessments of intent and good faith.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

> **Note on *genuine*:** The word participates primarily in standard attributive collocational patterns rather than in fixed idiomatic expressions. *The genuine article* is the most clearly fixed construction. Other patterns (*genuine concern*, *genuine surprise*, *genuine mistake*, *genuine belief*) represent strong collocational preferences rather than fully fixed idioms.

## Item 5: *authentic*

---

**C5.1**
- **Base Form:** *authentic*
- **Example:** *authentic self*
- **English translation:** one's true or genuine identity, as opposed to a performed or socially constructed persona
- **Usage notes:** Used in psychological, philosophical, and self-help discourse to refer to a person's genuine identity or character, as opposed to roles or masks adopted for social purposes. Common in existentialist philosophy and popular psychology.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C5.2**
- **Base Form:** *authentic*
- **Example:** *authentic experience*
- **English translation:** a genuine, unmediated encounter with something, as opposed to a staged or commodified version
- **Usage notes:** Used in philosophical, cultural, and tourism discourse to distinguish genuine encounters from staged or commercialized versions. Common in critiques of mass tourism and consumer culture.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C5.3**
- **Base Form:** *authentic*
- **Example:** *authentic voice*
- **English translation:** a distinctive and genuine mode of expression that reflects the speaker's or writer's true perspective
- **Usage notes:** Used in literary criticism, journalism, and cultural commentary to describe writing or speech that genuinely reflects the author's perspective rather than imitating conventions or performing for an audience.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C5.4**
- **Base Form:** *authentic*
- **Example:** *certified authentic*
- **English translation:** officially confirmed as genuine by a recognized authority
- **Usage notes:** Used in commercial, archival, and legal contexts to indicate that an item's genuineness has been formally verified by a recognized authority. Common in art markets, memorabilia, and document authentication.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C5.5**
- **Base Form:** *authentic*
- **Example:** *authentic reproduction*
- **English translation:** a copy made with faithful adherence to the original's characteristics
- **Usage notes:** Used in museum, publishing, and manufacturing contexts to describe a reproduction that faithfully replicates the original's materials, methods, or appearance. The construction is notable for combining *authentic* with *reproduction*, which might otherwise seem contradictory.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

> **Note on *authentic*:** The word participates in strong collocational patterns, particularly in philosophical and cultural discourse, but generates fewer fully fixed idiomatic expressions than *true* or *truth*. The constructions above represent the most common and analytically significant patterns.

## Item 6: *valid*

---

**C6.1**
- **Base Form:** *valid*
- **Example:** *valid point*
- **English translation:** a well-founded observation or argument that deserves acknowledgment
- **Usage notes:** An extremely common conversational and analytical construction used to acknowledge that an argument or observation is well-grounded. Used across all registers, including as a standalone response (*valid point*) in informal discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C6.2**
- **Base Form:** *valid*
- **Example:** *valid argument*
- **English translation:** an argument whose conclusion follows necessarily from its premises
- **Usage notes:** A technical construction in formal logic referring to an argument whose logical form is correct, regardless of whether the premises are true. Also used informally to mean a well-founded argument. Common in academic, legal, and everyday discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C6.3**
- **Base Form:** *valid*
- **Example:** *legally valid*
- **English translation:** having legal force and effect
- **Usage notes:** Used in legal contexts to indicate that a document, contract, or act has the force of law. Common in legal writing and formal administrative contexts.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C6.4**
- **Base Form:** *valid*
- **Example:** *still valid*
- **English translation:** continuing to have force or applicability
- **Usage notes:** Used to assert that a rule, argument, document, or claim continues to apply or have effect despite the passage of time or changed circumstances. Common in legal, administrative, and analytical contexts.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C6.5**
- **Base Form:** *valid*
- **Example:** *valid concern*
- **English translation:** a well-founded worry or objection that deserves to be taken seriously
- **Usage notes:** Used in political, professional, and everyday discourse to acknowledge that a concern or objection is legitimate and deserves attention. Common in diplomatic and concessive speech.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C6.6**
- **Base Form:** *valid*
- **Example:** *valid reason*
- **English translation:** a sufficient and legitimate justification
- **Usage notes:** Used to indicate that a justification or explanation is adequate and legitimate. Common in administrative, legal, and everyday contexts (*a valid reason for absence*).
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

> **Note on *valid*:** The word participates primarily in attributive collocational patterns rather than in fixed idiomatic expressions. No idioms specific to *valid* are identified beyond the collocational patterns above.

## Item 7: *correct*

---

**C7.1**
- **Base Form:** *correct*
- **Example:** *politically correct*
- **English translation:** conforming to social norms regarding language and behavior toward marginalized groups; (informally, often critically) excessively concerned with such conformity
- **Usage notes:** A fixed compound adjective used in social and political discourse. The term is contested: used approvingly to describe sensitivity to social norms and critically to describe perceived excessive restriction of speech. Common across all registers since the 1980s.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C7.2**
- **Base Form:** *correct*
- **Example:** *that is correct*
- **English translation:** that is right, that is accurate
- **Usage notes:** A standard predicative construction used to affirm the accuracy of a preceding statement. More formal in register than *that's right* or *yes*. Common in professional, legal, and formal conversational contexts.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C7.3**
- **Base Form:** *correct*
- **Example:** *correct me if I'm wrong*
- **English translation:** please tell me if I have made an error
- **Usage notes:** A fixed conversational phrase used to introduce a statement that the speaker is not entirely certain about, inviting correction. Common in spoken and informal written English. Often used rhetorically when the speaker is fairly confident they are right.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C7.4**
- **Base Form:** *correct*
- **Example:** *stand corrected*
- **English translation:** to acknowledge that one was wrong and accept the correction
- **Usage notes:** A fixed phrase used to formally acknowledge that one has been shown to be in error and accepts the correction (*I stand corrected*). Common in formal, professional, and academic discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C7.5**
- **Base Form:** *correct*
- **Example:** *correct in every detail*
- **English translation:** accurate in all particulars, without any error
- **Usage notes:** Used to assert complete accuracy across all aspects of a description or account. Common in formal and emphatic contexts.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C7.6**
- **Base Form:** *correct*
- **Example:** *broadly correct*
- **English translation:** substantially right, though possibly imprecise in detail
- **Usage notes:** Used to acknowledge that a claim is substantially accurate without asserting complete precision. Common in journalistic and analytical writing, parallel to *broadly accurate*.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

## Item 8: *right*

---

**C8.1**
- **Base Form:** *right*
- **Example:** *dead right*
- **English translation:** completely and entirely correct
- **Usage notes:** An emphatic construction used in informal speech to assert that something is absolutely correct. *Dead* functions as an intensifier. Common in British and American informal English.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C8.2**
- **Base Form:** *right*
- **Example:** *bang on right* / *bang right*
- **English translation:** exactly correct, precisely accurate
- **Usage notes:** Used in informal British English to assert that something is exactly correct. *Bang* functions as an intensifier. Common in informal speech.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C8.3**
- **Base Form:** *right*
- **Example:** *get it right*
- **English translation:** to do something correctly, to achieve the correct result
- **Usage notes:** A common verb-object construction used to describe achieving accuracy or correctness in a task or statement. Common across all registers (*it's important to get it right*).
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C8.4**
- **Base Form:** *right*
- **Example:** *put it right*
- **English translation:** to correct an error, to fix a problem
- **Usage notes:** Used to describe the act of correcting an error or rectifying a problem. Common in everyday and professional discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C8.5**
- **Base Form:** *right*
- **Example:** *set the record straight* / *set things right*
- **English translation:** to correct a mistaken account; to restore a correct state of affairs
- **Usage notes:** *Set the record straight* is a fixed phrase used to correct a false or misleading account. *Set things right* is used more broadly to describe restoring a correct or just state of affairs. Both are common across all registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C8.6**
- **Base Form:** *right*
- **Example:** *in the right*
- **English translation:** having a correct or just position, not being at fault
- **Usage notes:** A prepositional construction used to assert that a person or party holds the correct or morally justified position in a dispute. Common in legal, moral, and everyday discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C8.7**
- **Base Form:** *right*
- **Example:** *right on*
- **English translation:** exactly correct; (informally) an expression of enthusiastic agreement
- **Usage notes:** Used as an affirmation of correctness or agreement. In American English, particularly associated with 1960s–70s countercultural usage as an expression of enthusiastic endorsement. Also used straightforwardly to mean exactly correct (*right on target*).
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C8.8**
- **Base Form:** *right*
- **Example:** *am I right?*
- **English translation:** is that correct?, do you agree?
- **Usage notes:** A tag-question construction used to seek confirmation of a preceding statement. Extremely common in spoken English across all registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C8.9**
- **Base Form:** *right*
- **Example:** *you're right*
- **English translation:** what you have said is correct, I agree
- **Usage notes:** A standard predicative construction used to affirm the correctness of another person's statement. One of the most frequent truth-affirming expressions in spoken English.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C8.10**
- **Base Form:** *right*
- **Example:** *as it rightly should*
- **English translation:** as is correct and proper
- **Usage notes:** A fixed adverbial construction used to assert that a state of affairs is as it ought to be. Common in formal and journalistic writing.

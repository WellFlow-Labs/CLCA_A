1. **truth**
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **Literal meaning / morphology:** from Old English *trīewþ, trēowþ* ‘faith, fidelity, truth’
- **Typical English translations:** truth; verity

**Usage:** *Truth* is a basic and very common term for what is the case, especially in evaluating statements, beliefs, testimony, or representations. It is also used in moral, religious, legal, and philosophical contexts, as in “tell the truth,” “the truth of the claim,” or “truth and reconciliation.”  
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

2. **true**
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **Literal meaning / morphology:** from Old English *trēowe* ‘faithful, trustworthy’; related historically to fidelity
- **Typical English translations:** true; correct; genuine; faithful

**Usage:** *True* is widely used to evaluate statements as accurate, reports as correct, and objects or copies as genuine or faithful to an original. It also appears in expressions about identity or essence, such as “his true nature” or “a true friend.”  
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

3. **correct**
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **Literal meaning / morphology:** from Latin *correctus*, past participle of *corrigere* ‘set right’
- **Typical English translations:** correct; right; accurate

**Usage:** *Correct* is common in educational, technical, and everyday settings for answers, procedures, facts, and forms judged to be free from error. It can also describe socially or formally proper conduct, though that use is somewhat narrower than the accuracy-related one.  
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

4. **right**
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **Literal meaning / morphology:** historically ‘straight, just, proper’
- **Typical English translations:** right; correct; just; proper

**Usage:** *Right* is extremely common for judging answers, decisions, actions, and beliefs as correct or justified. It also has strong moral and legal uses, as in “the right thing to do” or “a right judgment,” so its evaluative range is broad.  
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

5. **accurate**
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **Literal meaning / morphology:** from Latin *accuratus* ‘done with care’
- **Typical English translations:** accurate; exact; precise

**Usage:** *Accurate* is used especially for descriptions, measurements, reports, models, and representations that closely match facts or reality. It is common in scientific, journalistic, bureaucratic, and everyday evaluative language.  
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

6. **accuracy**
- **Transliteration:** N/A
- **Literal meaning / morphology:** derived from *accurate* + *-cy*
- **Typical English translations:** accuracy; exactness; correctness

**Usage:** *Accuracy* names the quality of being factually or representationally correct. It is frequently used in discussions of data, testimony, translation, depiction, record-keeping, and performance.  
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

7. **genuine**
- **Transliteration:** N/A

**Usage:** *Genuine* is used for objects, emotions, intentions, and personal qualities judged to be real rather than fake, affected, or counterfeit. It is common both in commercial contexts (“genuine leather”) and in interpersonal evaluation (“genuine concern”).  
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

8. **authentic**

- **Typical English translations:** authentic; genuine; original

**Usage:** *Authentic* is common for documents, artworks, performances, identities, and experiences considered original, legitimate, or faithful to a source or tradition. It is especially frequent in legal, historical, aesthetic, and cultural discussions.  
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

9. **real**

- **Literal meaning / morphology:** from Latin *realis* ‘actual, pertaining to things’
- **Typical English translations:** real; actual; genuine

**Usage:** *Real* is a very common term for what actually exists or is not fake, imagined, or merely apparent. It also appears in evaluative uses concerning essence or legitimacy, as in “the real issue” or “a real painting, not a copy.”

- **Typical English translations:** sound; well-founded; reliable

**Usage:** *Sound* is often used for reasoning, judgment, policy, or scholarship that is dependable and well grounded. In logic and criticism it can be more technical, but it is also common in general evaluative prose.

**Usage:** *Faithful* can describe persons as loyal, but it is also used for translations, reproductions, adaptations, and depictions that closely preserve an original. In that representational sense it evaluates resemblance and fidelity rather than mere factual correctness.  
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

# CLCA Protocol v3.4 — P6 CHANGE, CAUSATION, AND STABILITY
# Language: English (en)

## DYNAMIC EXPRESSIONS IN THE TRUTH DOMAIN

---

## 1. BECOMING TRUE / ACQUIRING TRUTH-RELATED STATES

### 1.1 Basic Inchoative: "become + adjective"

**Pattern:** Subject + become + truth-domain adjective

**Examples:**
- The rumor became true.
- The claim became false over time.
- His testimony became unreliable.
- The document became invalid after the deadline.

**Usage notes:** "Become" is the general inchoative verb expressing change of state. Fully productive with all truth-domain adjectives. Neutral register.

**Temporal specification:** Can be modified with temporal adverbs (gradually, suddenly, eventually, over time).

---

### 1.2 Specialized Inchoative: "come true"

**Pattern:** Subject + come true

**Examples:**
- Her prediction came true.
- My worst fears came true.
- The prophecy came true.

**Usage notes:** Highly specialized construction limited to predictions, wishes, dreams, hopes, and fears. Cannot be used with other truth-domain adjectives (*"The claim came false" is ungrammatical). Very common in narrative contexts.

**Restrictions:** Subject must be a prediction, wish, dream, hope, or fear. Cannot be negated as *"come untrue" or *"come false."

---

### 1.3 Discovery/Revelation Inchoative: "turn out to be"

**Pattern:** Subject + turn out to be + truth-domain adjective

**Examples:**
- The story turned out to be true.
- His alibi turned out to be false.
- The painting turned out to be genuine.
- The document turned out to be counterfeit.

**Usage notes:** Emphasizes discovery or revelation of a pre-existing state rather than actual change. Implies the truth-value existed before but was unknown. Very common in narrative and explanatory contexts.

**Epistemic implication:** Suggests prior uncertainty or contrary expectation.

---

### 1.4 Evidential Inchoative: "prove (to be)"

**Pattern:** Subject + prove (to be) + truth-domain adjective

**Examples:**
- The theory proved true.
- His claims proved false.
- The method proved reliable.
- The document proved authentic.

**Usage notes:** Emphasizes evidential basis for the change in epistemic status. "To be" is optional and often omitted. Implies that evidence accumulated to establish the truth-value.

**Evidential strength:** Stronger evidential commitment than "turn out to be."

---

### 1.5 Gradual Change: "grow/get + adjective"

**Pattern:** Subject + grow/get + truth-domain adjective

**Examples:**
- The account grew more accurate with each revision.
- His testimony got less reliable over time.
- The distinction grew less clear.

**Usage notes:** "Grow" emphasizes gradual change; "get" is more neutral. Typically used with comparative forms. Less common than "become" but attested with gradable truth-domain adjectives.

**Restrictions:** Primarily used with gradable adjectives (accurate, reliable, clear) rather than absolute ones (true, false).

---

### 1.6 Aspectual Variation: "remain/stay + adjective"

**Pattern:** Subject + remain/stay + truth-domain adjective

**Examples:**
- The claim remained true despite challenges.
- His testimony stayed consistent.
- The document remained valid.
- The account stayed accurate.

**Usage notes:** Expresses continuation of state rather than change. Included here as the negative of change—stability over time. "Remain" is more formal than "stay."

**Contrast with change:** Explicitly marks absence of change that might have been expected.

---

## 2. BECOMING FALSE / LOSING TRUTH-RELATED STATES

### 2.1 Basic Inchoative to Negative State

**Pattern:** Subject + become + negative truth-domain adjective

**Examples:**
- The claim became false.
- The document became invalid.
- His testimony became unreliable.
- The account became inaccurate.

**Usage notes:** Same construction as positive inchoative but with negative adjectives. Fully productive.

---

### 2.2 Cessation of Validity/Truth

**Pattern:** Subject + cease to be + truth-domain adjective

**Examples:**
- The license ceased to be valid.
- The claim ceased to be true.
- The method ceased to be reliable.

**Usage notes:** Formal construction emphasizing definite cessation. More common with "valid," "reliable," and similar terms than with "true/false."

---

### 2.3 Expiration (specialized for validity)

**Pattern:** Subject + expire

**Examples:**
- The ticket expired.
- The license expired.
- The passport expired.

**Usage notes:** Specialized verb for loss of validity through passage of time. Cannot be used with other truth-domain concepts (*"The truth expired" is ungrammatical). Common with documents, licenses, tickets, and time-limited authorizations.

**Restrictions:** Limited to administrative/legal validity, not general truth.

---

### 2.4 Revelation of Prior Falsity

**Pattern:** Subject + turn out to be + negative adjective

**Examples:**
- The claim turned out to be false.
- The painting turned out to be fake.
- The document turned out to be counterfeit.
- His story turned out to be untrue.

**Usage notes:** Same construction as positive revelation but with negative adjectives. Very common in narrative contexts.

---

### 2.5 Evidential Discovery of Falsity

**Pattern:** Subject + prove (to be) + negative adjective

**Examples:**
- The theory proved false.
- The document proved counterfeit.
- His claims proved spurious.
- The method proved unreliable.

**Usage notes:** Same construction as positive evidential but with negative adjectives. Emphasizes evidential basis for discovering falsity.

---

## 3. CAUSATIVE CONSTRUCTIONS: MAKING TRUE/FALSE

### 3.1 Analytic Causative: "make + object + adjective"

**Pattern:** Agent + make + object + truth-domain adjective

**Examples:**
- The evidence made his claim true.
- The new data made the theory false.
- The revision made the account more accurate.
- The forgery made the document invalid.

**Usage notes:** General analytic causative construction. "Make" is neutral register. Fully productive with all truth-domain adjectives.

**Agent types:** Can be human agents, evidence, events, or abstract causes.

---

### 3.2 Formal Analytic Causative: "render + object + adjective"

**Pattern:** Agent + render + object + truth-domain adjective

**Examples:**
- The evidence rendered his claim true.
- The discovery rendered the theory false.
- The error rendered the results invalid.
- The forgery rendered the document inauthentic.

**Usage notes:** Formal register equivalent of "make." More common in written and technical contexts. Same productivity as "make."

---

### 3.3 Lexical Causative: "falsify"

**Pattern:** Agent + falsify + object

**Examples:**
- He falsified the records.
- The data falsified the hypothesis.
- They falsified the documents.

**Usage notes:** Lexical causative meaning "make false" or "prove false." Two distinct senses: (1) alter to make false/fraudulent (records, documents); (2) prove false through evidence (hypotheses, theories). Sense (2) is technical usage in philosophy of science.

**Ambiguity:** Context determines whether meaning is "fraudulently alter" or "disprove through evidence."

---

### 3.4 Lexical Causative: "validate"

**Pattern:** Agent + validate + object

**Examples:**
- The test validated the theory.
- The signature validates the document.
- The evidence validated his claims.
- The system validates the data.

**Usage notes:** Lexical causative meaning "make valid" or "confirm validity." Very common in technical, legal, and everyday contexts. Can refer to logical validation, legal authorization, or empirical confirmation.

**Multiple senses:** Logical validity, legal validity, empirical confirmation, or emotional validation.

---

### 3.5 Lexical Causative: "invalidate"

**Pattern:** Agent + invalidate + object

**Examples:**
- The error invalidated the results.
- The court invalidated the contract.
- The new evidence invalidated the theory.
- The forgery invalidated the document.

**Usage notes:** Lexical causative meaning "make invalid" or "nullify." Common in legal, scientific, and technical contexts. Opposite of "validate."

---

### 3.6 Lexical Causative: "authenticate"

**Pattern:** Agent + authenticate + object

**Examples:**
- The expert authenticated the painting.
- The signature authenticates the document.
- The lab authenticated the artifact.
- The system authenticates the user.

**Usage notes:** Lexical causative meaning "verify as authentic" or "prove genuine." Very common in art, historical, legal, and digital security contexts.

**Note:** No corresponding *"inauthenticate" exists; loss of authentication expressed as "invalidate" or "prove inauthentic."

---

### 3.7 Lexical Causative: "verify"

**Pattern:** Agent + verify + object

**Examples:**
- They verified the claim.
- The test verified the results.
- We need to verify the information.
- The system verifies the data.

**Usage notes:** Lexical causative meaning "establish as true" or "confirm truth/accuracy." Very common across all contexts. Less formal than "validate" or "authenticate."

---

### 3.8 Lexical Causative: "confirm"

**Pattern:** Agent + confirm + object

**Examples:**
- The evidence confirmed the theory.
- The test confirmed the diagnosis.
- The witness confirmed the account.
- The data confirmed our suspicions.

**Usage notes:** Lexical causative meaning "establish as true" or "verify." Very common in everyday and formal contexts. Slightly weaker evidential commitment than "prove."

---

### 3.9 Lexical Causative: "prove"

**Pattern:** Agent + prove + object (+ adjective)

**Examples:**
- The evidence proved the claim true.
- The test proved the theory false.
- They proved the document authentic.
- The experiment proved the hypothesis correct.

**Usage notes:** Lexical causative meaning "demonstrate truth/falsity through evidence." Can take object alone ("prove the claim") or object + adjective ("prove the claim true"). Strong evidential commitment.

**Strength:** Stronger than "confirm" or "verify"; implies conclusive evidence.

---

### 3.10 Lexical Causative: "correct"

**Pattern:** Agent + correct + object

**Examples:**
- She corrected the error.
- The teacher corrected the test.
- He corrected the mistake.
- The editor corrected the manuscript.

**Usage notes:** Lexical causative meaning "make correct" or "fix errors in." Very common in educational and editorial contexts. Implies changing from incorrect to correct state.

---

### 3.11 Lexical Causative: "legitimize/legitimise"

**Pattern:** Agent + legitimize + object

**Examples:**
- The election legitimized his authority.
- The law legitimized the practice.
- The recognition legitimized the government.
- The approval legitimized the procedure.

**Usage notes:** Lexical causative meaning "make legitimate" or "give legitimacy to." Common in political, legal, and social contexts.

**Variant:** British "legitimise" vs. American "legitimize."

---

### 3.12 Lexical Causative: "realize/realise"

**Pattern:** Agent + realize + object

**Examples:**
- She realized her dreams.
- They realized the plan.
- He realized his ambitions.
- The project realized the vision.

**Usage notes:** Lexical causative meaning "make real" or "actualize." Distinct from epistemic sense "become aware of." Common in contexts of achieving goals or actualizing plans.

**Ambiguity:** Context distinguishes causative sense ("make real") from epistemic sense ("become aware").

---

### 3.13 Causative through Negation: "disprove"

**Pattern:** Agent + disprove + object

**Examples:**
- The evidence disproved the theory.
- The test disproved the hypothesis.
- The facts disproved his claims.
- The experiment disproved the assumption.

**Usage notes:** Lexical causative meaning "prove false" or "demonstrate falsity." Common in scientific and argumentative contexts. Opposite of "prove."

---

### 3.14 Causative through Negation: "refute"

**Pattern:** Agent + refute + object

**Examples:**
- The evidence refuted the claim.
- She refuted the argument.
- The data refuted the hypothesis.
- The facts refuted his theory.

**Usage notes:** Lexical causative meaning "prove false through argument or evidence." More formal than "disprove." Common in academic and argumentative contexts.

**Usage note:** Often misused to mean "deny" or "reject" rather than "prove false."

---

### 3.15 Causative through Negation: "debunk"

**Pattern:** Agent + debunk + object

**Examples:**
- The study debunked the myth.
- Scientists debunked the theory.
- The investigation debunked the claims.
- The article debunked the rumor.

**Usage notes:** Lexical causative meaning "expose as false" or "show to be false." Informal register. Common in journalistic and popular contexts. Often implies the falsity was widely believed.

---

### 3.16 Causative through Negation: "expose"

**Pattern:** Agent + expose + object + as + adjective

**Examples:**
- They exposed the claim as false.
- The investigation exposed the document as counterfeit.
- The analysis exposed the theory as spurious.
- The report exposed the account as inaccurate.

**Usage notes:** Lexical causative meaning "reveal to be false/fake." Requires "as + adjective" complement. Common in investigative and critical contexts.

---

## 4. PREVENTING CHANGE / MAINTAINING TRUTH-STATES

### 4.1 Preservation: "keep/maintain + adjective"

**Pattern:** Agent + keep/maintain + object + adjective

**Examples:**
- They kept the records accurate.
- We must maintain the account truthful.
- The system keeps the data valid.
- He maintained his testimony consistent.

**Usage notes:** Causative construction for preventing change or maintaining a state. "Keep" is more common in everyday usage; "maintain" is more formal.

---

### 4.2 Protection from Falsification: "preserve"

**Pattern:** Agent + preserve + object

**Examples:**
- They preserved the truth.
- The archive preserves the authentic documents.
- We must preserve the accuracy of the records.
- The system preserves data integrity.

**Usage notes:** Lexical causative meaning "keep from being lost or corrupted." Common in archival, historical, and technical contexts.

---

### 4.3 Ensuring Continuation: "ensure/insure"

**Pattern:** Agent + ensure + (that) clause / object + remains + adjective

**Examples:**
- They ensured that the records remained accurate.
- We must ensure the data stays valid.
- The process ensures the results remain reliable.
- The system ensures authenticity.

**Usage notes:** Causative construction for guaranteeing continuation of a state. "Ensure" is standard; "insure" is variant (primarily American).

---

## 5. RESISTANCE TO CHANGE

### 5.1 Persistence Despite Challenge: "remain/stay + adjective"

**Pattern:** Subject + remain/stay + adjective + despite/in spite of + challenge

**Examples:**
- The claim remained true despite the criticism.
- The theory stayed valid in spite of new evidence.
- His testimony remained consistent despite cross-examination.
- The account stayed accurate despite revisions.

**Usage notes:** Expresses stability in face of potential change. Emphasizes resistance to challenge or pressure.

---

### 5.2 Endurance: "hold (up)"

**Pattern:** Subject + hold (up) / hold true

**Examples:**
- The theory holds up under scrutiny.
- The claim holds true across contexts.
- His alibi didn't hold up.
- The principle holds regardless of circumstances.

**Usage notes:** Idiomatic expression for maintaining validity or truth under testing or over time. "Hold up" emphasizes testing; "hold true" emphasizes continued applicability.

---

### 5.3 Survival of Testing: "stand up (to)"

**Pattern:** Subject + stand up (to) + test/scrutiny/examination

**Examples:**
- The claim stands up to scrutiny.
- The theory stands up to testing.
- His story didn't stand up to examination.
- The evidence stands up in court.

**Usage notes:** Idiomatic expression for maintaining validity under rigorous testing. Common in legal and scientific contexts.

---

### 5.4 Withstanding Challenge: "withstand"

**Pattern:** Subject + withstand + challenge/test/scrutiny

**Examples:**
- The theory withstood criticism.
- The claim withstood scrutiny.
- The evidence withstood cross-examination.
- The account withstood verification.

**Usage notes:** Formal verb meaning "resist successfully" or "remain valid despite challenge." More formal than "hold up" or "stand up to."

---

## 6. GRADUAL CHANGE AND DEGREES

### 6.1 Increase in Truth-Related Quality

**Pattern:** Subject + become + more/increasingly + adjective

**Examples:**
- The account became more accurate.
- The theory became increasingly reliable.
- The evidence became more convincing.
- The claim became progressively clearer.

**Usage notes:** Expresses gradual increase in degree of truth-related quality. Common with gradable adjectives (accurate, reliable, clear) but less natural with absolute adjectives (true, false).

---

### 6.2 Decrease in Truth-Related Quality

**Pattern:** Subject + become + less + adjective

**Examples:**
- The account became less accurate over time.
- The theory became less reliable.
- The evidence became less convincing.
- The claim became less credible.

**Usage notes:** Expresses gradual decrease in degree of truth-related quality. Same restrictions as increase pattern.

---

### 6.3 Improvement: "improve"

**Pattern:** Subject + improve + in + quality / Agent + improve + object

**Examples:**
- The accuracy improved.
- The account improved in reliability.
- They improved the accuracy of the measurements.
- The revision improved the truthfulness of the report.

**Usage notes:** Lexical verb for positive change in quality. Can be intransitive (quality improves) or transitive (agent improves object).

---

### 6.4 Deterioration: "deteriorate/decline"

**Pattern:** Subject + deteriorate/decline + in + quality

**Examples:**
- The accuracy deteriorated over time.
- The reliability declined.
- The account deteriorated in truthfulness.
- The validity declined with age.

**Usage notes:** Lexical verbs for negative change in quality. More formal than "get worse." Primarily intransitive.

---

## 7. ASPECTUAL DISTINCTIONS

### 7.1 Sudden Change: "suddenly become"

**Pattern:** Subject + suddenly + become + adjective

**Examples:**
- The claim suddenly became false.
- The document suddenly became invalid.
- The theory suddenly became untenable.

**Usage notes:** Adverbial modification marking punctual change. Can be used with any inchoative construction.

---

### 7.2 Gradual Change: "gradually become"

**Pattern:** Subject + gradually + become + adjective

**Examples:**
- The account gradually became more accurate.
- The theory gradually became accepted as true.
- The document gradually became less reliable.

**Usage notes:** Adverbial modification marking durative change. Common with gradable adjectives.

---

### 7.3 Eventual Change: "eventually become"

**Pattern:** Subject + eventually + become + adjective

**Examples:**
- The rumor eventually became true.
- The theory eventually proved false.
- The claim eventually became accepted.

**Usage notes:** Adverbial modification marking delayed change after period of time. Very common in narrative contexts.

---

### 7.4 Completed Change: "have become"

**Pattern:** Subject + have + become + adjective

**Examples:**
- The claim has become false.
- The document has become invalid.
- The theory has become outdated.

**Usage notes:** Perfect aspect marking completed change with current relevance. Standard aspectual marking in English.

---

## 8. MODAL AND CONDITIONAL CHANGE

### 8.1 Potential Change: "could/might become"

**Pattern:** Subject + could/might + become + adjective

**Examples:**
- The claim could become false.
- The document might become invalid.
- The theory could prove true.

**Usage notes:** Modal marking of potential or possible change. Standard modal usage in English.

---

### 8.2 Necessary Change: "must become"

**Pattern:** Subject + must + become + adjective

**Examples:**
- The claim must become verifiable.
- The document must become valid.
- The account must become more accurate.

**Usage notes:** Modal marking of necessary or required change. Common in prescriptive contexts.

---

### 8.3 Conditional Change: "would become (if)"

**Pattern:** Subject + would + become + adjective + if + condition

**Examples:**
- The claim would become false if the evidence changed.
- The document would become invalid if unsigned.
- The theory would prove true if tested properly.

**Usage notes:** Conditional marking of hypothetical change. Standard conditional construction in English.

---

## 9. EXPRESSIONS NOT COMMONLY ATTESTED

### 9.1 No Common Expression: *"become genuine"

**Note:** While "become authentic" is attested, "become genuine" is much less common. Objects are typically described as "being genuine" or "proving genuine" rather than "becoming genuine." The change is usually expressed as discovery rather than actual transformation.

**Alternative expressions:**
- "prove (to be) genuine"
- "turn out to be genuine"
- "be revealed as genuine"

---

### 9.2 No Common Expression: *"make honest"

**Note:** While "make truthful" is possible, "make honest" is uncommon. Honesty is typically treated as a character trait that cannot be directly caused by external agents. 

**Alternative expressions:**
- "encourage honesty"
- "promote honesty"
- "foster honesty"

---

### 9.3 No Common Expression: *"become sincere"

**Note:** Sincerity is typically treated as an internal state that cannot be described as "becoming" in the same way as truth-values. Discovery of sincerity is more common than change to sincerity.

**Alternative expressions:**
- "prove sincere"
- "turn out to be sincere"
- "be revealed as sincere"

---

### 9.4 Limited Expression: "become real"

**Note:** "Become real" is attested but less common than "become true" or "come true." More commonly expressed through "realize" (make real) or "materialize."

**Examples:**
- The dream became real. (less common)
- The dream was realized. (more common)
- The dream materialized. (more common)

---

### 9.5 No Common Expression: *"become bona fide"

**Note:** "Bona fide" as a borrowed Latin phrase does not participate in standard English inchoative constructions. Objects are described as "being bona fide" but not "becoming bona fide."

**Alternative expressions:**
- "prove to be bona fide"
- "be established as bona fide"

---

## 10. SUMMARY OBSERVATIONS

### 10.1 Productivity Patterns

**Highly productive constructions:**
- "become + adjective" (fully productive with all truth-domain adjectives)
- "make + object + adjective" (fully productive)
- "prove (to be) + adjective" (fully productive)
- "turn out to be + adjective" (fully productive)

**Moderately productive constructions:**
- "grow/get + adjective" (restricted to gradable adjectives)
- Lexical causatives (lexically specific: falsify, validate, authenticate, etc.)

**Limited productivity constructions:**
- "come true" (restricted to predictions, wishes, dreams)
- "expire" (restricted to validity of documents/licenses)

### 10.2 Semantic Distinctions

**Change vs. Discovery:**
English distinguishes between:
- Actual change of state: "become," "make"
- Discovery of pre-existing state: "turn out to be," "prove to be"

**Causation Types:**
- Direct causation: "make," "render"
- Evidential causation: "prove," "confirm," "verify"
- Negative causation: "falsify," "invalidate," "disprove"

**Aspectual Distinctions:**
- Punctual: "suddenly become"
- Durative: "gradually become"
- Resultative: "have become"

### 10.3 Lexical Gaps

Several concepts lack dedicated causative verbs:
- No *"genuinize" (make genuine)
- No *"inauthenticate" (make inauthentic)
- No *"sincerize" (make sincere)
- No *"honestify" (make honest)

These gaps are filled by analytic causatives ("make genuine," "make sincere") or alternative expressions.

### 10.4 Register Variation

**Neutral register:**
- "become," "make," "get," "prove"

**Formal register:**
- "render," "maintain," "withstand," "deteriorate"

**Informal register:**
- "turn out," "hold up," "pan out," "check out"

**Technical register:**
- "validate," "authenticate," "falsify," "verify"

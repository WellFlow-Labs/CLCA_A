# CLCA Protocol v3.2 — P3 NATURAL SEMANTIC GROUPING
**Language: English (en)**

---

## Preliminary Note on Method

Groupings below are derived from observed patterns in P1 and P2: which items appear in the same syntactic frames, which are contrasted with each other in attested constructions, which share typical contexts of use (legal, logical, journalistic, etc.), and which collocate with the same nouns or verbs. No external semantic framework has been imposed. The number of groups, their sizes, and their boundaries reflect the data as documented.

Items from P1 are referenced by their P1 number. Items from P2 are referenced by their P2 code (e.g., P2a-9.7 for *falsifiable*).

---

## Group 1: Expressions used to evaluate statements and propositions as matching or not matching the facts

**Label:** Propositional truth and falsity

**Definition:** These expressions are used to predicate correspondence (or its absence) between a statement, belief, or claim and the actual state of affairs. They appear in the canonical frames *that is ___*, *it is ___ that…*, and *true or false?*, and they contrast directly with each other. They are applied to propositions, assertions, and answers rather than primarily to objects or persons.

**Representative expressions:**
- *true* (P1-1)
- *truth* (P1-2)
- *false* (P1-9)
- *falsehood* (P2a-9.4)
- *falsity* (P2a-9.3)
- *untruth* (P1b-1.3 / P2a-2.4)
- *untrue* (P1b-1.2 / P2a-1.3)
- *correct* (P1-7)
- *incorrect* (P2a-7.6)
- *right* (P1-8) — in its factual-accuracy sense
- *truth value* (P1b-1.10)
- *truth condition* (P1b-1.11)
- *true or false?* (P2b-9.8)
- *that is true / that is false* (P2b-1.2, P2b-9.1)
- *it is true that…* (P2b-1.1)
- *hold true* (P1b-1.30)

**Notes:** *Correct* and *right* overlap with this group but also appear in normative and procedural contexts (see Group 4). *Truth* and *falsity* are the nominal anchors of this group; *untruth* and *falsehood* are used more euphemistically or with a moral coloring (see also Group 3).

## Group 2: Expressions used in formal logical and philosophical contexts to evaluate arguments and claims

**Label:** Logical and epistemic evaluation

**Definition:** These expressions appear specifically in contexts where the concern is with the formal properties of arguments (whether conclusions follow from premises), the testability of hypotheses, or the conditions under which claims can be confirmed or refuted. They are concentrated in academic, philosophical, and scientific registers and are rarely used in casual speech with their technical meanings intact.

**Representative expressions:**
- *valid* (P1-6) — in its logical sense
- *validity* (P2a-6.2)
- *invalid* (P2a-6.6)
- *sound* (P1-22) — in its logical sense ("a sound argument is both valid and has true premises")
- *soundness* (P2a from P1-22)
- *unsound* (P2a from P1-22)
- *falsifiable* (P2a-9.7)
- *falsifiability* (P2a-9.8)
- *unfalsifiable* (P2a-9.9)
- *verifiable* (P2a-17.2 / P1b-2.2)
- *verifiability* (P2a-17.3 / P1b-2.3)
- *unverifiable* (P2a-17.5 / P1b-2.5)
- *verificationism* (P1b-2.19)
- *verificationist* (P1b-2.20)
- *truth table* (P1b-1.13)
- *truth value* (P1b-1.10)
- *truth condition* (P1b-1.11)
- *truth-conditional* (P1b-1.12)
- *veridical* (P1b-2.9)
- *verisimilitude* (P1b-2.11) — in its Popperian sense
- *the argument is valid but unsound* (P2b-6.3)

**Notes:** *Truth value*, *truth condition*, and *truth table* also appear in Group 1 as propositional-truth expressions, but their primary habitat is the technical logical register documented here. *Sound* in this group is distinct from its everyday use meaning "reliable" or "well-founded" (see Group 6). *Veridical* is used specifically of perceptual states in philosophy and cognitive science, which distinguishes it from the propositional-truth items in Group 1.

## Group 3: Expressions used to evaluate persons and their speech acts as honest or deceptive

**Label:** Personal honesty and deception in speech

**Definition:** These expressions are applied to persons and to their statements, testimony, or conduct to evaluate whether they are telling the truth or engaging in deception. They appear in contexts of moral evaluation, legal testimony assessment, and interpersonal judgment. The contrast is not primarily between a statement and the facts but between a person's inner state (genuine intent to communicate accurately) and their outward expression.

**Representative expressions:**
- *honest* (P1-15)
- *honesty* (P2a-15.2)
- *dishonest* (P2a-15.3)
- *dishonesty* (P2a-15.5)
- *sincere* (P1-16)
- *sincerity* (P2a-16.2)
- *insincere* (P2a-16.3)
- *insincerity* (P2a-16.5)
- *truthful* (P1-29)
- *truthfulness* (P2a-2.3)
- *untruthful* (P2a-2.5)
- *untruthfulness* (P2a-2.6)
- *veracious* (P1-18)
- *veracity* (P1-19)
- *truth-telling* (P1b-1.7)
- *truth-teller* (P1b-1.8)
- *to be honest* (P2b-15.1)
- *let's be honest* (P2b-15.6)
- *to tell the truth* (P2b-2.1)
- *truth be told* (P2b-2.14)
- *an honest mistake* (P2b-15.4)
- *honest to God / honest to goodness* (P2b-15.2, P2b-15.3)

- *falsehood* (P2a-9.4) — when used of deliberate lying
- *falsely accused* (P2a-9.1)

**Notes:** *Veracious* and *veracity* belong here rather than in Group 2 because their primary use is in evaluating persons and testimony (legal and journalistic contexts) rather than propositions or arguments. *Honest* and *sincere* are closely related but distinguishable: *honest* evaluates the absence of deception broadly, while *sincere* focuses specifically on whether feelings or expressions are genuinely meant. *An honest mistake* is notable because it uses *honest* to assert the absence of deceptive intent rather than the presence of truthfulness.

## Group 4: Expressions used to evaluate conformity with a rule, standard, or procedure

**Label:** Correctness as conformity with a standard

**Definition:** These expressions are used when the evaluation concerns whether something meets a required standard, follows a rule, or conforms to an established procedure. They appear in legal, administrative, educational, and procedural contexts. The standard of evaluation is external and explicit (a rule, a law, a correct procedure) rather than correspondence with facts.

**Representative expressions:**

- *right* (P1-8) — in its procedural sense ("the right way to do it")
- *rightful* (P2a-8.3)
- *rightfully* (P2a-8.4)
- *valid* (P1-6) — in its legal sense ("a valid contract," "a valid ticket")
- *validity* (P2a-6.2) — legal sense
- *invalid* (P2a-6.6) — legal sense
- *invalidate* (P2a-6.8)
- *legitimate* (P1-13)
- *legitimacy* (P2a-13.2)
- *illegitimate* (P2a-13.3)
- *legitimize* (P2a-13.6)

**Notes:** *Correct* and *right* appear in both Group 1 (propositional truth) and this group; the distinction is between evaluating a statement as factually accurate versus evaluating an action or procedure as conforming to a rule. *Valid* similarly spans Groups 2 and 4: in logic it evaluates argument form; in law and administration it evaluates whether conditions for legal force have been met. *Legitimate* is primarily at home in this group, though it overlaps with Group 5 (authenticity) when used to assert that something is what it purports to be.

## Group 5: Expressions used to assert that something is what it purports to be, not an imitation or substitute

**Label:** Genuineness and authenticity of objects, persons, and documents

**Definition:** These expressions are used when the question is whether an object, document, person, or experience is the real thing rather than a copy, imitation, forgery, or performance. They appear in legal, art-historical, archival, and consumer contexts. The contrast is between an original or genuine item and something that resembles it but is not it.

**Representative expressions:**

- *self-authenticating* (P1b-3b.8)
- *real* (P1-14) — in its genuineness sense ("is this real?")
- *the real deal* (P2b-14.2)
- *fake* (P1-10)
- *faker* (P2a-10.1)
- *fakery* (P2a-10.2)
- *fake news* (P2b-10.1)
- *counterfeit* (P1-11)

**Notes:** Within this group, there are usage-based distinctions worth noting. *Counterfeit* is used almost exclusively of physical objects (currency, documents, goods) made with intent to deceive, and carries strong legal connotations. *Spurious* is more common in academic and scholarly registers and applies to claims and arguments as well as objects. *Bogus* is more informal and journalistic. *Apocryphal* is specific to stories and accounts that are widely circulated but of uncertain origin, and does not imply deliberate fraud. *Fake* is the most general and register-neutral of the negative terms. *Authentic* and *genuine* are the primary positive terms; *authentic* tends to appear in more formal and philosophical contexts, while *genuine* is more evenly distributed across registers.

## Group 6: Expressions used to evaluate sources, persons, and methods as consistently trustworthy

**Label:** Reliability and trustworthiness of sources and persons

**Definition:** These expressions are used when the evaluation concerns whether a person, source, instrument, or method can be consistently depended upon to provide accurate information or perform correctly over time. They appear in journalistic, legal, scientific, and everyday contexts. The emphasis is on consistency and dependability rather than on a single instance of accuracy.

**Representative expressions:**

**Notes:** *Sound* in this group is used in its everyday evaluative sense ("sound advice," "a sound investment") rather than its technical logical sense (Group 2). *Credible* and *reliable* are closely related but distinguishable in usage: *credible* evaluates whether something is worthy of belief (epistemic standing), while *reliable* evaluates whether something can be consistently depended upon (behavioral consistency). *Faithful* and *fidelity* appear here in their loyalty sense and in Group 5 in their reproduction-accuracy sense.

## Group 7: Expressions used in the process of confirming or establishing accuracy

**Label:** Verification and confirmation procedures

**Definition:** These expressions are used to describe the act, process, or result of checking whether a claim, identity, or document is accurate or genuine. They appear in scientific, journalistic, legal, administrative, and digital security contexts. The focus is on the procedural activity of checking rather than on the property of being true.

**Representative expressions:**
- *verify* (P1-17)

- *independently verified* (P2b-17.2)
- *unable to verify* (P2b-17.3)
- *verify your identity* (P2b-17.4)
- *verify against* (P1b-2.16)
- *authenticate* (P2a-5.3)

- *authenticator* (P2a-5.5)
- *unauthenticated* (P2a-5.8)
- *self-authenticating* (P1b-3b.8)
- *accurate* (P1-3) — when used in the construction *accurate to within* (P2b-3.5)
- *ground truth* (P1b-1.15)

- *averment* (P1b-2.15)

**Notes:** This group overlaps with Group 5 (authenticity) in the case of *authenticate* and *authentication*, which are used both to describe the process of checking genuineness and to assert the result. *Verdict* is included here because its etymology (*veredictum*, "truly said") connects it to the *ver-* root, and its legal function is the formal declaration of a finding of fact; however, its primary usage is as a legal term of art rather than a verification-process term, and it could reasonably be left ungrouped. *Ground truth* is a technical term from remote sensing and machine learning that refers to empirically established reference data used to validate model outputs; it belongs here as a verification-standard term.

## Group 8: Expressions used to describe partial, qualified, or apparent truth

**Label:** Partial, apparent, or qualified correspondence

**Definition:** These expressions are used when a claim is evaluated as not fully true or false but as partially accurate, apparently true, or true in appearance only. They appear in journalistic, political, and philosophical contexts where simple binary evaluation is insufficient. They often carry an implication that the partial or apparent truth is being used to mislead.

**Representative expressions:**
- *half-truth* (P1b-1.9)
- *plausible* (P1-27)
- *plausibly* (from P1-27)
- *plausibility* (from P1-27)
- *implausible* (from P1-27)
- *a plausible explanation* (P2b-27.1)
- *plausible deniability* (P2b-27.2)
- *verisimilitude* (P1b-2.11)
- *there is a grain of truth in it* (P2b-2.5)
- *there is some truth in what she says* (P2b-2.4)
- *broadly accurate* (P2b-3.3)
- *reasonably accurate* (P2b-3.4)
- *stretch the truth* (P2b-2.11)
- *bend the truth* (P2b-2.12)
- *economical with the truth* (P2b-2.10)
- *true enough, but…* (P2b-1.12)
- *it is true to say that…* (P2b-1.11)
- *truism* (P2a-1.4) — a statement that is trivially true
- *apocryphal* (P1-26) — in the sense of uncertain rather than false

**Notes:** *Plausible* is the central item in this group: it consistently appears in contexts where a claim is evaluated as appearing credible without being confirmed. *Economical with the truth*, *stretch the truth*, and *bend the truth* all describe deliberate use of partial accuracy to mislead, placing them at the boundary between this group and Group 3 (personal honesty). *Truism* is included here because it describes a statement that is trivially true — true in a way that is not informative — which is a form of qualified or degenerate truth. *Broadly accurate* and *reasonably accurate* are hedging constructions that acknowledge partial correspondence.

## Group 9: Expressions used in discourse management to assert or signal candor

**Label:** Discourse markers signaling candor or frank disclosure

**Definition:** These expressions function primarily as sentence adverbials or discourse markers that signal to an interlocutor that what follows is a candid, unguarded, or frank statement. They do not primarily predicate truth of a proposition but perform a pragmatic function of framing the speaker's stance. They are concentrated in spoken and informal written registers.

**Representative expressions:**
- *to be honest* (P2b-15.1)
- *to tell the truth* (P2b-2.1)
- *truth be told* (P2b-2.14)
- *in truth* (P2b-2.6)
- *let's be honest* (P2b-15.6)
- *honestly* (P2a-15.1) — in its sentence-adverb use
- *truly* (P2a-1.1) — in its intensifier/sincerity use
- *really* (P2a-14.1) — in its assertion-of-genuineness use
- *frankly* — not a P1/P2 item; noted only to clarify the functional class
- *the truth is that…* (P2b-2.2)
- *the truth of the matter is that…* (P2b-2.3)
- *to be accurate* (P2b-3.1)
- *strictly accurate* (P2b-3.2)
- *correct me if I'm wrong, but…* (P2b-7.3)
- *if I am correct* (P2b-7.2)
- *speak truth to power* (P2b-2.8)

**Notes:** This group is defined by function rather than by the semantic content of the base items. The same items (*honestly*, *truly*, *really*) appear in other groups when used in their non-discourse-marker functions. *Speak truth to power* is included here because it describes a speech act of candid disclosure directed at authority, though it also belongs to Group 3 (personal honesty in speech). *To be accurate* and *strictly accurate* are more formal variants of the same discourse function, used to introduce precise corrections.

## Ambiguous or Overlapping Items

The following items from P1 and P2 present genuine difficulty in grouping and are noted here with the relevant overlaps identified.

---

**accurate** (P1-3)
Belongs primarily to Group 1 (propositional correspondence) when applied to statements and descriptions, and to Group 7 (verification procedures) when used in the construction *accurate to within* + measure. The degree-specification use (*accurate to within five percent*) is more procedural than propositional. No single group captures all its uses.

---

**right** (P1-8)
Spans Group 1 (factual accuracy: "that's right"), Group 4 (procedural correctness: "the right way to do it"), and Group 3 (moral rightness: "it was the right thing to do"). The moral sense generates a distinct derivational sub-family (*righteous*, *righteousness*) that is not shared by the factual or procedural senses. These uses are sufficiently distinct that *right* could be treated as three separate items for grouping purposes, but the P1 documentation treats it as a single polysemous item.

---

**valid** (P1-6)
Spans Group 2 (logical validity of arguments) and Group 4 (legal validity of documents and acts). The colloquial use ("that's a valid point," P2b-6.1) is closer to Group 6 (reliability/trustworthiness) than to either technical sense. The three uses are distinguished in practice by register and collocate: *valid argument* (Group 2), *valid contract/ticket* (Group 4), *valid point* (colloquial, closer to Group 6).

---

**faithful / fidelity** (P1-20, P1-21)
Spans Group 5 (genuineness of reproductions: "a faithful translation") and Group 6 (personal reliability and loyalty: "faithful to a commitment"). The reproduction sense and the loyalty sense are both well attested and cannot be reduced to each other. *Fidelity* in audio technology (*high fidelity*, P2b-21.1) is a further distinct use within the reproduction sense.

---

**real** (P1-14)
Spans Group 1 (propositional: "is it real?"), Group 5 (genuineness of objects: "the real deal"), and everyday intensifier use (*really*, *for real*) that does not fit cleanly into any truth-domain group. The derivational family (*realism*, *realist*, *realistic*) generates uses in philosophy, art criticism, and politics that are not straightforwardly truth-domain uses.

---

**sound** (P1-22)
Spans Group 2 (technical logical sense: "a sound argument is valid with true premises") and Group 6 (everyday evaluative sense: "sound advice," "sound judgment"). The two senses are distinguished by register and by the specific collocates: *sound argument* in logic pedagogy versus *sound advice/judgment/investment* in everyday and professional speech.

---

**bona fide** (P1-28)
Spans Group 4 (conformity with legal standards: "a bona fide purchaser") and Group 5 (genuineness: "a bona fide offer"). The legal sense emphasizes good faith and absence of fraud; the genuineness sense emphasizes that something is what it purports to be. In practice these overlap substantially in legal contexts.

---

**falsely** (P2a-9.1)
When used in *falsely accused*, the adverb modifies a speech act (an accusation) and belongs to Group 3 (personal honesty/deception). When used in *falsely stated*, it belongs to Group 1 (propositional truth). The surface form is the same; the group membership depends on what is being modified.

---

**post-truth** (P1b-1.20)
This item does not fit cleanly into any of the groups above. It describes a political and media environment rather than evaluating a specific statement, person, or object. It presupposes the categories of Groups 1 and 3 but operates at a meta-level, commenting on the social conditions under which truth claims are made and received. It is noted here as ungrouped.

---

**verisimilitude** (P1b-2.11)
Spans Group 2 (Popperian philosophy of science: degree of approximation to truth) and Group 8 (literary criticism: the quality of appearing realistic). The two uses are sufficiently distinct in register and meaning that the item could be placed in either group or neither. It is listed in Group 2 with a note, and also noted in Group 8.

---

**verdict** (P1b-2.12)
Noted in Group 7 (verification procedures) on etymological grounds, but its primary usage is as a legal term of art for a formal judgment. It does not typically appear in contexts where the other Group 7 items appear. It could reasonably be left ungrouped.

---

**truism** (P2a-1.4)
Placed in Group 8 (partial/qualified truth) on the grounds that it describes a statement that is trivially true. However, it is used critically to dismiss a statement rather than to evaluate its degree of correspondence, which distinguishes it from the other Group 8 items. It could also be left ungrouped.

---

## Items That Do Not Fit Cleanly into Any Group

The following items from P1 and P2 are noted as not fitting clearly into any of the groups identified above. This does not indicate that they are unimportant; it indicates that their usage patterns do not align with the clusters identified here.

- **true north** (P1b-1.23): Used in navigation (technical) and in motivational/leadership discourse (figurative). The figurative use applies *true* to personal values and direction rather than to propositional content or object genuineness.
- **true-blue** (P1b-1.24): Evaluates political loyalty; the truth-domain connection is not transparent in current usage.
- **true love** (P1b-1.27): Applies *true* to the quality of an emotional bond; not a truth-domain use in any of the senses documented in the groups above.
- **come true** (P1b-1.29): Describes the actualization of a wish or prediction; the *true* here relates to realization rather than correspondence or genuineness.
- **home truth** (P1b-1.16): Describes an unwelcome but accurate personal observation; combines elements of Groups 1 and 3 but is primarily a pragmatic category (a type of speech act) rather than a truth-evaluation category.
- **gospel truth** (P1b-1.17): Functions as an intensifier of certainty rather than as a truth-evaluation term; belongs to Group 9 (discourse markers) only loosely.
- **moment of truth** (P1b-1.18): Describes a decisive moment of revelation or testing; the *truth* here is metaphorical.
- **authentic cadence** (P1b-3b.14): A music theory term; the connection to the genuineness or truth domain is not transparent in current usage.
- **verismo / verism** (P1b-2.6, P1b-2.8): Art and music criticism terms; the *ver-* root contributes a sense of realistic depiction rather than propositional truth or object genuineness.
- **righteous / righteousness** (P2a-8.6, P2a-8.8): These derive from *right* but operate in a moral and religious register that is distinct from the truth-domain uses documented in the groups above. They are not straightforwardly truth-domain items.
- **authentic self / authentic experience / authentic leadership** (P1b-3b.9, P1b-3b.10, P1b-3b.11): These collocations apply *authentic* to personal identity, experiential quality, and management style in ways that are not primarily about correspondence to an original or absence of forgery. They are noted in P1b as peripheral extensions and remain ungrouped here.

# CLCA Protocol v3.2 — P1 CORE VOCABULARY COLLECTION
# Language: Swahili (sw)

1. **kweli**
   - Literal meaning: truth, truthfulness
   - Typical English translations: true, truth, really, truly, genuine
   - Usage: Used to affirm the accuracy or veracity of statements and to express genuineness. Commonly appears in both everyday speech ("ni kweli" = "it's true") and formal contexts to validate claims or emphasize sincerity.
   - [CORE]

2. **ukweli**
   - Literal meaning: u- (noun class prefix) + -kweli (truth root)
   - Typical English translations: truth, reality, fact
   - Usage: Nominal form referring to truth as a concept or state of affairs. Frequently used in philosophical, legal, and everyday contexts to denote factual accuracy or reality ("ukweli wa jambo" = "the truth of the matter").
   - [CORE]

3. **sahihi**
   - Literal meaning: correct, accurate (from Arabic ṣaḥīḥ)
   - Typical English translations: correct, accurate, right, proper, valid
   - Usage: Used to evaluate correctness of statements, answers, or procedures. Common in educational and administrative contexts to mark accuracy or approve documents ("jibu sahihi" = "correct answer").
   - [CORE]

4. **uongo**
   - Literal meaning: u- (noun class prefix) + -ongo (lie/falsehood root)
   - Typical English translations: lie, falsehood, untruth, deception
   - Usage: Refers to deliberate falsehood or deceptive statements. Used in moral and everyday contexts to identify dishonest speech or misrepresentation ("kusema uongo" = "to tell a lie").
   - [CORE]

5. **-a kweli**
   - Literal meaning: of truth (associative construction)
   - Typical English translations: true, genuine, real, authentic
   - Usage: Adjectival phrase modifying nouns to indicate genuineness or authenticity. Used to distinguish real from fake or to emphasize sincerity ("rafiki wa kweli" = "true friend").
   - [CORE]

6. **haki**
   - Literal meaning: right, justice (from Arabic ḥaqq)
   - Typical English translations: right, justice, truth, entitlement, fairness
   - Usage: Denotes both moral/legal rights and truth/justice as concepts. Used in legal, ethical, and everyday contexts to express what is right, just, or true ("haki yako" = "your right"; "haki ni kwamba..." = "the truth is that...").
   - [CORE]

7. **sawa**
   - Literal meaning: equal, same, level
   - Typical English translations: correct, right, okay, equal, same, appropriate
   - Usage: Indicates correctness, appropriateness, or agreement with standards. Commonly used to confirm accuracy or acceptability in everyday interactions ("ni sawa" = "it's correct/okay").
   - [CORE]

8. **halisi**
   - Literal meaning: genuine, pure (from Arabic khāliṣ)
   - Typical English translations: genuine, authentic, real, pure, true
   - Usage: Emphasizes authenticity and genuineness of objects, substances, or character. Used to distinguish authentic from counterfeit or to describe sincere qualities ("dhahabu halisi" = "genuine gold").
   - [CORE]

9. **kamili**
   - Literal meaning: complete, perfect (from Arabic kāmil)
   - Typical English translations: complete, perfect, whole, full, accurate
   - Usage: Indicates completeness, perfection, or full accuracy. Used in evaluative contexts to describe thoroughness or correctness ("taarifa kamili" = "complete/accurate information").
   - [CORE]

10. **-enye haki**
    - Literal meaning: having right/justice
    - Typical English translations: right, just, justified, righteous
    - Usage: Describes persons or positions as being in the right or justified. Used in moral and legal evaluations to identify who has justice on their side.
    - [CORE]

11. **dhahiri**
    - Literal meaning: apparent, evident (from Arabic ẓāhir)
    - Typical English translations: clear, evident, obvious, apparent, manifest
    - Usage: Indicates what is clearly evident or manifestly true. Used to point out observable truth or obvious facts ("ni dhahiri kwamba..." = "it is evident that...").
    - [CORE]

12. **batili**
    - Literal meaning: false, invalid (from Arabic bāṭil)
    - Typical English translations: false, invalid, null, void, untrue
    - Usage: Denotes falsity, invalidity, or nullity especially in legal and formal contexts. Used to declare statements or documents as without validity or truth.
    - [CORE]

13. **-a uongo**
    - Literal meaning: of falsehood (associative construction)
    - Typical English translations: false, fake, counterfeit, deceptive
    - Usage: Adjectival phrase marking something as false or fake. Used to identify deceptive representations or counterfeit items ("habari za uongo" = "false news").
    - [CORE]

14. **hoja**
    - Literal meaning: argument, reason, proof (from Arabic ḥujja)
    - Typical English translations: argument, reason, proof, evidence, justification
    - Usage: Refers to reasoned arguments or evidence supporting claims. Used in debates, legal contexts, and discussions to present justifications or proofs.
    - [CORE]

15. **ushahidi**
    - Literal meaning: u- (noun class prefix) + -shahidi (witness root, from Arabic shahīd)
    - Typical English translations: evidence, testimony, witness, proof
    - Usage: Denotes evidence or testimony establishing truth. Commonly used in legal contexts and investigations to refer to proof or witness statements.
    - [CORE]

16. **hakika**
    - Literal meaning: certainty, truth (from Arabic ḥaqīqa)
    - Typical English translations: certain, sure, definite, truly, indeed
    - Usage: Expresses certainty or definiteness about truth claims. Used to emphasize confidence in accuracy or to confirm statements emphatically ("kwa hakika" = "certainly, surely").
    - [CORE]

17. **-adilifu**
    - Literal meaning: just, fair (from Arabic ʿadl + Bantu derivational suffix)
    - Typical English translations: just, fair, honest, upright
    - Usage: Describes persons or actions as morally upright and fair. Used in ethical evaluations of character and conduct ("mtu adilifu" = "honest/just person").
    - [CORE]

18. **uadilifu**
    - Literal meaning: u- (noun class prefix) + -adilifu (just/fair)
    - Typical English translations: justice, fairness, honesty, integrity
    - Usage: Nominal form referring to the quality of being just or honest. Used in moral and legal discourse to denote integrity and fairness as virtues.
    - [CORE]

19. **-bovu**
    - Literal meaning: rotten, corrupt
    - Typical English translations: corrupt, rotten, bad, spoiled, false
    - Usage: Describes moral corruption or physical decay. Used in ethical contexts to characterize dishonest conduct or corrupted states ("rushwa ni mbovu" = "bribery is corrupt").
    - [CORE]

20. **-nyofu**
    - Literal meaning: straight, upright
    - Typical English translations: straight, honest, upright, direct, sincere
    - Usage: Describes straightforwardness and honesty in character or speech. Used to evaluate moral rectitude and truthfulness ("mwenendo mnyofu" = "upright conduct").
    - [CORE]

21. **thibitisha**
    - Literal meaning: to confirm, verify (from Arabic thabata)
    - Typical English translations: confirm, verify, validate, prove, establish
    - Usage: Verb meaning to establish truth or validity through verification. Used in formal and everyday contexts to describe the act of confirming accuracy or authenticity.
    - [CORE]

22. **-a kweli kabisa**
    - Literal meaning: of truth completely (intensified form)
    - Typical English translations: absolutely true, completely genuine, very real
    - Usage: Intensified expression emphasizing complete truthfulness or authenticity. Used for strong affirmation of genuineness beyond ordinary claims.
    - [CORE]

23. **-danganyifu**
    - Literal meaning: deceptive (from -danganya "to deceive" + -fu suffix)
    - Typical English translations: deceptive, deceitful, fraudulent, misleading
    - Usage: Describes persons or things characterized by deception. Used in moral evaluations to identify dishonest or misleading qualities.
    - [CORE]

24. **usahihi**
    - Literal meaning: u- (noun class prefix) + -sahihi (correct)
    - Typical English translations: correctness, accuracy, validity, authenticity
    - Usage: Nominal form referring to the quality of being correct or accurate. Used in formal contexts to discuss accuracy standards or validity.
    - [CORE]

25. **-a haki**
    - Literal meaning: of right/justice (associative construction)
    - Typical English translations: rightful, just, legitimate, proper
    - Usage: Adjectival phrase indicating legitimacy or rightfulness. Used to mark claims, ownership, or positions as morally or legally justified.
    - [CORE]

26. **-a dhati**
    - Literal meaning: of essence/nature (from Arabic dhāt)
    - Typical English translations: essential, inherent, genuine, natural, real
    - Usage: Describes what is essential or inherent to something's nature. Used to identify fundamental or genuine characteristics ("sifa za dhati" = "essential qualities").
    - [CORE]

27. **-tupu**
    - Literal meaning: empty, mere, bare
    - Typical English translations: mere, empty, bare, hollow, false
    - Usage: Can indicate emptiness or lack of substance, sometimes used to characterize claims as hollow or without real content. Used in evaluative contexts to dismiss superficial appearances.
    - [CORE]

28. **-a busara**
    - Literal meaning: of wisdom/reason
    - Typical English translations: reasonable, sensible, wise, rational, sound
    - Usage: Describes judgments or conduct as reasonable and sound. Used in evaluative contexts to characterize thinking or decisions as properly reasoned.
    - [CORE]

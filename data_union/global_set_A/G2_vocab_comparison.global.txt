## Narrative Analysis

### Recurring Partition Patterns

**The propositional truth / personal honesty split is present in all eight languages.** Every language maintains a lexical distinction between evaluating whether a statement is true and evaluating whether a person is honest. The terms used for these two functions are never identical, though they may share a root. Georgian uses *simarṭle/č̣ešmariteba* for propositional truth and *gulc̣rpeli* for sincerity; Yoruba uses *òtítọ́* for propositional truth and *ìwà rere* for moral character; Finnish uses *tosi/totuus* for propositional truth and *rehellinen* for honesty; Basque uses *egia* for propositional truth and *benetakoa* for genuine sincerity; Tamil uses *uṇmai* for propositional truth and *nērmai* for honesty/integrity; Indonesian uses *benar* for correctness and *jujur* for honesty; Turkish uses *doğru* for correctness and *dürüst* for uprightness; Korean uses *사실* for factual accuracy and *정직* for honesty. The split is consistent across all eight languages, though the degree of lexical elaboration on each side varies considerably.

**The source-trustworthiness / content-credibility distinction is present in all eight languages.** Georgian explicitly distinguishes *sarc̣muno* (inherent trustworthiness of a source) from *damajerebeliˈ* (persuasive effect on a hearer). Yoruba distinguishes *fidagarri* (trustworthy source) from *sinesgarri* (believable content). Finnish distinguishes *luotettava* (reliable/dependable over time) from *uskottava* (believable for a specific claim). Basque distinguishes *fidagarria* (trustworthy source) from *sinesgarria* (believable content). Tamil distinguishes *nampakamāna* (reliable) from *nampattakka* (worth trusting for a specific claim). Indonesian distinguishes *terpercaya* (trusted/reliable) from *kredibel* (credible). Turkish distinguishes *güvenilir* (reliable) from *inanılır* (believable). Korean distinguishes *신뢰성* (reliability of a source) from *신빙성* (credibility of a specific claim). The distinction is lexically encoded in all eight languages, though the degree of elaboration varies.

**The neutral-copy / fraudulent-fake distinction is present in at least six languages.** Finnish distinguishes *jäljitelmä* (imitation, without necessarily implying fraud) from *väärennös* (forgery, implying fraudulent intent). Basque distinguishes *kopiakoa* (copy) from *faltsua* (fake). Tamil distinguishes *nakal* (copy, neutral) from *pōli* (fake, negative). Indonesian distinguishes *tiruan* (imitation, neutral) from *palsu* (fake, fraudulent). Turkish distinguishes *taklit* (imitation) from *sahte* (counterfeit). Korean distinguishes *모조품* (imitation goods) from *위조품* (forged item). Georgian and Yoruba show less elaboration of this three-way contrast: Georgian has *namdvili* vs. *q̇albi* as a binary, and Yoruba lacks a dedicated forgery vocabulary.

**The legal/procedural validity vs. moral/substantive legitimacy distinction is present in all eight languages.** Georgian distinguishes *kanonieri* (lawful, tied to positive law) from *legitimuri* (legitimate, with possible normative dimension). Finnish distinguishes *pätevä* (valid/competent) from *oikeudenmukainen* (just/fair). Basque distinguishes *baliozko* (valid) from *zilegi* (lawful/permissible) from *zuzen* (correct/just). Tamil distinguishes *caṭṭappaṭi* (according to law) from *niyāyam* (justice/rightness). Indonesian distinguishes *sah* (legally valid) from *adil* (fair/just). Turkish distinguishes *yasal* (legal) from *meşru* (legitimate, extending beyond legality). Korean distinguishes *합법* (lawful) from *정당* (legitimate/justified). The distinction is present in all eight languages, though the degree of lexical elaboration and the specific terms used vary considerably.

**The intransitive/passive framing of truth-revelation is cross-linguistically consistent.** All eight languages show constructions in which truth is the grammatical subject of an intransitive or passive verb expressing emergence or disclosure: Georgian *simarṭle gamovida* (the truth came out), Yoruba *ó farahàn pé òtítọ́ ni* (it became clear that it is the truth), Finnish *totuus tulee ilmi* (the truth comes to light), Basque *egia argitara atera* (truth brought to light), Tamil *uṇmai veḷippaṭṭatu* (the truth came to light), Indonesian *kebenaran akan terungkap* (the truth will come out), Turkish *gerçek ortaya çıkmak* (the truth comes out), Korean *진실이 드러나다* (the truth emerges). This framing — treating truth as an entity that moves from concealment to visibility — is consistent across all eight languages.

**The proverbial assertion that truth ultimately prevails is present in all eight languages.** Georgian *simarṭle gamarjvebs* (truth prevails), Yoruba *òtítọ́ kò ní kú* (truth will not die), Finnish *totuus tulee ilmi* (used in this sense), Basque *egia beti ateratzen da azalera* (the truth always comes to the surface), Tamil *uṇmai tāṉ vellum* (truth alone will prevail), Indonesian *kebenaran akan terungkap pada akhirnya* (the truth will come out in the end), Turkish *doğruluk her zaman ödüllendirilmez* (a more pessimistic variant, but the proverbial frame is present), Korean *진실은 하나다* (there is only one truth) and related expressions. The specific framing varies, but the proverbial register is present in all eight languages.

**No language has a lexicalized single-word agentive for "truth-teller."** All eight languages have lexicalized agentives for habitual liars (Georgian *mt'q̇uari*, Yoruba *aṣírọ́*, Finnish *valehtelija*, Basque *gezurti*, Tamil *poyyaṉ*, Indonesian *pembohong*, Turkish *yalancı*, Korean *거짓말쟁이*), but none has a parallel single-word agentive for "truth-teller." The concept is expressed periphrastically in all languages. This asymmetry is consistent across all eight languages and across unrelated language families.

**No language has a dedicated lexical item for "half-truth" as a distinct category.** All eight languages can express the concept of a partially true statement, but none has a single lexicalized term for it. The concept is expressed through compositional phrases: Georgian *naxevari č̣ešmariteba*, Yoruba (no fixed phrase documented), Finnish *puoli totuus*, Basque *egia erdi*, Tamil *uṇmai zati bat*, Indonesian (no fixed phrase documented), Turkish (no fixed phrase documented), Korean (no fixed phrase documented). This gap is consistent across all eight languages.

### Where Languages Split Distinctions More Finely

**Korean splits the truth domain most finely**, maintaining distinct Sino-Korean roots for factual accuracy (*사실*), truthfulness/sincerity (*진실*), precision/exactness (*정확*), honesty in conduct (*정직*), trustworthiness (*신뢰*), and authenticity of inner state (*진정성*). These are not merely register variants of a single concept but distinct evaluative categories with different collocational patterns, different semantic groups, and different constructional behaviors. No other language in the dataset shows this degree of internal differentiation within the truth domain.

**Basque splits the verifiability domain most finely**, distinguishing *egiaztagarria* (verifiable, capable of being verified), *egiaztagaitza* (difficult to verify), and *egiaztaezina* (impossible to verify) as three distinct forms within the same derivational paradigm. The *-garri*/*-gaitz*/*-ezin* three-way distinction — potential/difficult/impossible — is not paralleled in any other language in the dataset, which typically show only a binary verifiable/unverifiable opposition.

**Turkish splits the verification/falsification domain most finely**, maintaining a productive morphological opposition between *doğrulamak* (to verify) and *yanlışlamak* (to falsify) with parallel derivational stacking generating *doğrulanabilirlik* (verifiability) and *yanlışlanabilirlik* (falsifiability) as technical philosophical terms. While other languages have vocabulary for verification and for showing something to be false, only Turkish shows this as a productive morphological pair built on contrasting truth-domain bases.

**Finnish splits the checking/verification domain most finely**, explicitly distinguishing *tarkistaa* (to check, everyday) from *tarkastaa* (to inspect, formal/official) as register-differentiated near-synonyms where the distinction is semantic (institutional authority) rather than merely stylistic. No other language in the dataset documents this register distinction at the same level of explicitness.

**Georgian splits the register dimension of propositional truth most finely**, maintaining *č̣ešmariteba* (philosophical/theological register) and *simarṭle* (everyday/legal register) as distinct nominals for truth, with different collocational patterns, different discourse functions, and different derivational families. Finnish has a similar distinction (*totuus* vs. *tosi*) but the Georgian distinction is more elaborated in the data.

**Georgian and Basque split the appearance-versus-reality contrast most finely.** Georgian has *moč̣venebiti* (apparent/seeming/ostensible) as a dedicated adjective, and Basque has *itxurazko* (apparent/seeming) and *itxurakeria* (pretense/hypocrisy) as a dedicated cluster. Finnish has *näennäinen* (apparent/seeming). In Yoruba, Tamil, Indonesian, Turkish, and Korean, the contrast is expressed through constructions and discourse markers rather than dedicated adjectives.

### Where Languages Merge Distinctions

**Yoruba merges propositional correctness and procedural correctness** under the single *tọ́* root, which covers both "this statement is correct" and "this procedure is proper." The same predicative construction *ó tọ́* (it is correct/right/proper) applies across factual, moral, and procedural evaluation without lexical differentiation. Other languages (Finnish, Korean, Georgian) maintain distinct vocabulary for these functions.

**Indonesian merges the innocent-error and deliberate-falsehood distinction less sharply than some other languages**, though it does distinguish *keliru* (innocent error) from *bohong* (deliberate lie). The *salah* root covers both error and fault/blame without a dedicated term for the innocent-error pole, relying on context and the *keliru*/*salah* distinction to convey the difference. Georgian, Finnish, and Korean show more explicit lexical differentiation between error-without-intent and deliberate deception.

**Tamil merges the classical/literary and everyday registers of truth** under *uṇmai* more completely than Georgian merges *simarṭle* and *č̣ešmariteba*. While *mey* (classical truth) exists as a literary near-synonym, it is documented as marginal in everyday use, and the *uṇmai* root covers both philosophical and everyday truth without the sharp register split seen in Georgian.

**Turkish merges epistemic truth and legal entitlement** under the *hak* root (from Arabic *ḥaqq*), which simultaneously generates *haklı* (justified/in the right), *haksız* (unjust), *hakkaniyet* (equity), *hak etmek* (to deserve), and *insan hakları* (human rights). While other languages show some overlap between truth and justice vocabulary (Georgian *marṭal-* covers both correctness and justice; Yoruba *òdodo* covers both righteousness and justice), the Turkish *hak* root shows the most complete merger of epistemic and entitlement senses within a single productive root.

**Basque merges the sincerity and genuineness dimensions** under the *benetako* cluster more completely than other languages. *Benetako* covers both "genuine (not fake)" and "sincere (not performed)," and *benetan* functions as both an authenticity marker and a sincerity marker. Other languages (Korean, Finnish, Indonesian) maintain more distinct vocabulary for these two functions.

### Cases Where a Language Appears to Lack a Clear Lexical Contrast Found Elsewhere

**Yoruba lacks a dedicated vocabulary for documentary forgery and counterfeiting** as a distinct legal category. While Yoruba has vocabulary for lying (*irọ́*), false testimony (*ẹlẹ́rìí irọ́*), and deception (*ìtànjẹ́*), the data does not document a dedicated term for the act of producing a forged document or counterfeit currency parallel to Georgian *gaq̇albeba*, Finnish *väärentää*, Basque *faltsifikatu*, Tamil *pōli āvaṇam*, Indonesian *pemalsuan*, Turkish *sahte belge*, or Korean *위조*. The concept would presumably be expressed through general deception vocabulary applied to documents.

**Yoruba lacks a dedicated vocabulary for legal/procedural validity** as a distinct category. While Yoruba has *ẹ̀tọ́* (right/entitlement) and *òdodo* (righteousness/justice), the data does not document a dedicated term for the validity or invalidity of legal instruments parallel to Georgian *kanonieri/legitimuri*, Finnish *pätevä/pätemätön*, Basque *baliozkoa/baliogabea*, Tamil *caṭṭappaṭi*, Indonesian *sah/tidak sah*, Turkish *geçerli/geçersiz*, or Korean *유효/무효*.

**Tamil lacks a dedicated vocabulary for the philosophical/technical truth domain** (truth values, truth conditions, truth tables) that is documented in Finnish (*totuusarvo*, *totuusehdot*, *totuustaulu*), Turkish (*doğruluk değeri*, *doğruluk koşulu*, *doğruluk tablosu*), and Korean (*진리값*). While Tamil has *uṇmai* in philosophical contexts, the data does not document technical logical vocabulary built on the *uṇmai* root.

**Indonesian lacks a dedicated vocabulary for the appearance-vs.-reality distinction** as elaborated as Basque *itxura* (appearance/semblance) or Georgian *moč̣venebiti* (apparent/seeming/ostensible). While Indonesian has *nyata* (real/manifest) and *sebenarnya* (actually/in truth), the data does not document a dedicated adjectival term for "merely apparent" or "ostensible" parallel to these.

**Georgian lacks a dedicated vocabulary for the inner-state authenticity dimension** as elaborated as Korean *진정성* (authenticity of inner state). While Georgian has *gulc̣rpeli* (sincere/straight-hearted) and *tvaltmaqcoba* (hypocrisy/eye-deceiving), the data does not document a term specifically for the authenticity of expressed feelings or political commitments as a public evaluative category with the same degree of elaboration seen in Korean.

### Distinguishing Cross-Language Patterns from Single-Language Observations

**Well-attested cross-language patterns** (present in five or more languages with explicit documentation):
- The propositional truth / personal honesty split (all eight languages)
- The source-trustworthiness / content-credibility distinction (all eight languages)
- The neutral-copy / fraudulent-fake distinction (at least six languages)
- The legal/procedural validity vs. moral/substantive legitimacy distinction (all eight languages)
- The intransitive/passive framing of truth-revelation (all eight languages)
- The proverbial assertion that truth ultimately prevails (all eight languages)
- The absence of a lexicalized single-word agentive for "truth-teller" (all eight languages)
- The absence of a dedicated lexical item for "half-truth" (all eight languages)
- The spatial metaphor of straightness underlying truth/correctness vocabulary (at least six languages: Georgian, Yoruba, Finnish, Basque, Tamil, Turkish, Korean; Indonesian *benar* is less clearly spatial)

**Patterns present in a minority of languages** (two to four languages):
- The register-differentiated split between philosophical/theological and everyday truth nominals (Georgian *č̣ešmariteba*/*simarṭle*, Finnish *totuus*/*tosi*, Korean *진리*/*진실*/*사실* — three languages)
- The explicit *doğrulamak*/*yanlışlamak* morphological opposition for verification/falsification (Turkish only, with partial parallels in Finnish *todistaa*/*osoittaa epätodeksi* and Korean *사실확인*/*허위*)
- The *있다/없다* existential frame as the primary predication strategy for abstract quality nominals (Korean only)
- The *-garri*/*-gaitz*/*-ezin* three-way verifiability distinction (Basque only)
- The bifurcation of a single form into "credibility" (secular) and "faith/religion" (theological) meanings (Georgian *sarwmunoeba* only, with a partial parallel in Yoruba *ìgbàgbọ́* covering both belief and faith)
- The dedicated appearance-versus-reality adjective cluster (Georgian *moč̣venebiti*, Basque *itxurazko*, Finnish *näennäinen* — three languages; absent or marginal in the other five)

**Single-language observations** (documented in only one language):
- The *ìwà* (character/manner of being) as the organizing concept of moral evaluation with the specific proverbial elaboration *ìwà ló ń ṣe ènìyàn* (Yoruba only)
- The *marṭl-* root extending into orthography (*marṭlc̣era*) and Orthodox Christianity (*marṭlmadidebeli*) (Georgian only)
- The *진정성* (authenticity of inner state) as a highly elaborated public evaluative category with a rich collocation network (Korean only, though the concept exists in other languages)
- The *ziurrenik* semantic shift from "certainly" to "probably/most likely" (Basque only)
- The *ikhlas* (selflessly sincere, from Arabic *ikhlāṣ*) as a distinct sincerity term with specifically religious connotations of selfless acceptance (Indonesian only, though Arabic-derived terms appear in Turkish and Georgian as well)
- The *salah* + verb/noun compounding pattern generating a large family of misaction compounds (*salah paham*, *salah kaprah*, *salah sasaran*, *salah tafsir*) (Indonesian only, though Turkish *yanlış anlamak* is a partial parallel)
- The *sarwmunoeba* semantic bifurcation into "credibility" and "faith/religion" (Georgian only)
- The *kebenaran formal*/*kebenaran materiil* (procedural vs. substantive truth) as a documented technical legal distinction (Indonesian only)
- The *doğrulamak*/*yanlışlamak* productive morphological pair encoding the verification/falsification distinction with parallel *-abilirlik* nominalizations (Turkish only)

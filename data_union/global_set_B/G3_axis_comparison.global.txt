# G3 — Emergent Cross-Language Semantic Dimensions

---

## 1. Procedure

The dimensions below are inferred from the F3 semantic grouping tables and corroborating F1–F2 evidence across all eight languages. Each dimension is defined from the data, not from prior theoretical frameworks. Representative terms are drawn from at least two languages where available. Distribution notes record whether the dimension is clearly lexicalized, morphologically derived, merged with another grouping, or weakly attested.

---

## 2. Proposed Dimensions

---

### Dimension 1: Correspondence of a statement or representation to the facts

**Definition.** These expressions evaluate whether a verbal or written claim, report, or description matches what is actually the case in the world. The evaluative target is a proposition, account, or representation, not a physical object or a person's character. The positive pole asserts correspondence; the negative pole asserts non-correspondence, which may or may not imply intent.

**Representative terms:**
- zh: 真实, 失实, 属实, 事实, 如实, 真话, 实话
- he: *ʾemet*, *nakhon*, *ʾamiti*, *lo nakhon*
- sa: *satya*, *tathya*, *yathārtha*, *asatya*
- el: *αληθής*, *αλήθεια*, *αναληθής*, *ψευδής*
- en: *true*, *false*, *accurate*, *inaccurate*, *truthful*
- tl: *totoo*, *katotohanan*, *hindi totoo*, *kasinungalingan*
- sw: *kweli*, *ukweli*, *si kweli*, *uongo*
- qu: *cheqa*, *cheqam*, *mana cheqachu*, *llulla*

**Distribution.** Clearly lexicalized in all eight languages. This is the most consistently attested dimension in the corpus. In all languages, the positive and negative poles are expressed through a primary lexical pair that participates in the same construction types (predication, modification, nominalization). The dimension is the foundational evaluative axis of the domain in every language examined.

---

### Dimension 2: Genuineness of an object, document, or person as an instance of its kind

**Definition.** These expressions evaluate whether a physical object, document, credential, or person is what it presents itself as being, rather than a copy, imitation, forgery, or impostor. The evaluative target is an entity, not a proposition. The positive pole asserts that the entity is the genuine article; the negative pole asserts that it is counterfeit, forged, or inauthentic.

**Representative terms:**
- zh: 真伪, 真品, 真迹, 假冒, 伪造, 仿冒
- he: *meqori*, *ʾotenti*, *mezuyaf*, *ziyyuf*
- sa: *satya* (in object-evaluation contexts), *pratirūpa*
- el: *γνήσιος*, *αυθεντικός*, *πλαστός*, *νόθος*, *κίβδηλος*
- en: *genuine*, *authentic*, *fake*, *counterfeit*, *spurious*, *forged*
- tl: *tunay*, *orihinal*, *awtentiko*, *huwad*, *peke*
- sw: *halisi*, *bandia*, *ghushi*
- qu: *cheqa* (in object-evaluation collocations), *cheqa qelqa*, *llulla qelqa*

**Distribution.** Clearly lexicalized in all eight languages. In all languages, this dimension is maintained as distinct from Dimension 1, even when a single root participates in both (e.g., zh 真, tl *totoo/tunay*, qu *cheqa*). The degree of lexical separation varies: Hebrew and Greek show the sharpest lexical differentiation between the two dimensions; Quechua and Tagalog show more overlap through a single root covering both. The negative pole (counterfeit, forged) is more consistently lexicalized as a single word than the positive pole in several languages (zh 假冒, 伪造; el *πλαστός*; en *fake*, *counterfeit*; tl *huwad*, *peke*; sw *bandia*, *ghushi*).

---

### Dimension 3: Conformity of an answer, procedure, or action to a standard or norm

**Definition.** These expressions evaluate whether an answer, method, calculation, procedure, or action conforms to a relevant standard, rule, or norm, rather than simply whether it corresponds to external reality. The evaluative standard is internal to a domain (educational, procedural, grammatical, moral) rather than being the world as it is. The positive pole asserts conformity; the negative pole asserts deviation or error.

**Representative terms:**
- zh: 正确, 错误, 准确, 准确性
- he: *nakhon*, *meduyaq*, *lo nakhon*, *taʿut*
- sa: *sādhu*, *asādhu*, *yukta*, *ayukta*, *ucita*, *anucita*
- el: *σωστός*, *λανθασμένος*, *ορθός*, *ακριβής*
- en: *correct*, *incorrect*, *right*, *wrong*, *accurate*, *exact*
- tl: *tama*, *mali*, *wasto*, *tumpak*
- sw: *sahihi*, *si sahihi*, *usahihi*
- qu: *chiqan*, *allin*, *pantasqa*, *pantay*

**Distribution.** Clearly lexicalized in all eight languages. In all languages, this dimension is maintained as at least partially distinct from Dimension 1, though the degree of lexical separation varies. Chinese maintains the clearest distinction (真实 for correspondence to reality vs. 正确 for conformity to a standard). Sanskrit elaborates this dimension most finely, distinguishing grammatical correctness (*sādhu*), contextual appropriateness (*yukta*, *ucita*), and aesthetic fittingness (*aucitya*) as sub-groupings. In Quechua, *allin* covers both moral goodness and practical correctness, merging this dimension with moral evaluation. In Tagalog, *tama* (correct) and *wasto* (proper, conforming to standard) are distinguished by register and degree of normative emphasis.

---

### Dimension 4: Reliability and credibility of a person, source, or institution over time

**Definition.** These expressions evaluate whether a person, institution, or source of information can be consistently trusted to provide accurate and honest information, or to perform their role faithfully. The evaluative target is the entity as a source, not a single statement or object. The evaluation concerns consistent performance across situations rather than a single instance of accuracy.

**Representative terms:**
- zh: 可靠, 可靠性, 不可靠
- he: *ʾamin*, *ʾaminut*, *mehiman*, *mehimanut*, *neʾeman*, *neʾemanut*
- sa: *pramāṇabhūta* (in Buddhist usage), *satyavādin*
- el: *αξιόπιστος*, *αξιοπιστία*, *αναξιόπιστος*
- en: *reliable*, *reliability*, *credible*, *credibility*, *trustworthy*, *trustworthiness*
- tl: *mapagkakatiwalaan*, *kredible*, *tiwala*
- sw: *uaminifu*, *amini*, *mwaminifu*
- qu: *iñinapaq*, *mana iñinapaq*

**Distribution.** Clearly lexicalized in all eight languages. In all languages, this dimension is maintained as distinct from both Dimension 1 (propositional truth) and Dimension 2 (object genuineness), though it overlaps with Dimension 5 (sincerity) in several languages. Hebrew shows the most elaborate lexical differentiation within this dimension, distinguishing general reliability (*ʾamin*), formal legal credibility (*mehiman*), and personal faithfulness (*neʾeman*). Sanskrit has the least elaborated vocabulary for this dimension among the eight languages, with *pramāṇabhūta* being restricted to a specific Buddhist usage.

---

### Dimension 5: Sincerity and honesty of a person's inner disposition and speech

**Definition.** These expressions evaluate whether a person's expressed feelings, intentions, or statements reflect their actual inner states, rather than being performed or strategic. The evaluative target is the alignment between inner state and outward expression. This dimension is applied to persons and their speech acts, not to propositions or objects.

**Representative terms:**
- zh: 诚实, 真诚, 真心, 表里如一
- he: *kanut*, *yashar*, *yosher*, *ken*
- sa: *satya* (in ethical speech contexts), *satyavādin*
- el: *ειλικρίνεια* (implied by data), *αληθινός* (in personal contexts)
- en: *sincere*, *sincerity*, *honest*, *honesty*, *truthful*
- tl: *tapat*, *katapatan* (in honesty sense)
- sw: *uaminifu* (in moral dimension)
- qu: *cheqa sonqo*, *cheqa sonqoyoq*

**Distribution.** Clearly lexicalized in all eight languages, though the degree of lexical separation from Dimension 4 (reliability) varies. In Tagalog, *tapat* and *katapatan* merge Dimensions 4 and 5, covering both honest speech and faithful commitment to persons. In Hebrew, *neʾeman* covers both faithfulness (Dimension 5) and reliability (Dimension 4). In Quechua, the *cheqa sonqo* (true-hearted) construction locates this dimension in the inner self (*sonqo*), a culturally specific framing not paralleled in most other languages. In Sanskrit, the *satya* root covers this dimension in ethical speech contexts, with *satyavādin* (truth-speaker) as the primary agentive form.

---

### Dimension 6: Deliberate deception and intentional falsification

**Definition.** These expressions evaluate acts, persons, or representations as involving deliberate intent to create false impressions in another's mind, or to produce fraudulent objects or documents. The distinguishing feature is the implication of agency and intent, which separates this dimension from simple error (Dimension 3) or mere inauthenticity (Dimension 2). The evaluative target may be an act, a person, or a product of deliberate falsification.

**Representative terms:**
- zh: 欺骗, 伪造, 弄虚作假, 造假, 冒充
- he: *ramaʾut*, *honaʾah*, *mirmah*, *lezayef*
- sa: *asatya* (in legal speech contexts), *asatyavādin*, *anṛtavacana*
- el: *απάτη* (implied), *πλαστογραφία*, *δόλος* (implied)
- en: *fraudulent*, *fabricated*, *counterfeit*, *deceive*, *forge*
- tl: *linlang*, *mapanlinlang*, *pagsisinungaling*
- sw: *udanganyifu*, *kudanganya*, *ulaghai*, *ghushi*
- qu: *llullakuy*, *llullachiy*, *pantachiy*

**Distribution.** Clearly lexicalized in all eight languages. In all languages, this dimension is distinguished from simple error or inaccuracy by the implication of intent. The distinction between deliberate deception (this dimension) and mere error (Dimension 3) is consistently maintained across the corpus. In Chinese, the distinction is marked by the contrast between 错误 (error, no intent implied) and 欺骗/伪造 (deception/forgery, intent implied). In Quechua, *pantay* (to err) and *llullakuy* (to lie) are maintained as distinct, with *pantachiy* (to cause to err) occupying an intermediate position. In Sanskrit, *asatya* in legal contexts implies deliberate false speech, while *bhrama* (error) does not.

---

### Dimension 7: Legal or institutional validity of documents, procedures, and claims

**Definition.** These expressions evaluate whether a document, contract, decision, credential, or claim has the formal status required for it to be recognized as operative or binding within a legal or institutional framework. The evaluative standard is formal and procedural rather than factual or moral. The positive pole asserts that something meets the required formal conditions; the negative pole asserts that it lacks legal force or has been annulled.

**Representative terms:**
- zh: 合法, 违法, 合法性, 合法化
- he: *taqef*, *batel*, *kasher*, *pasul*, *ḥuqi*, *legitimi*
- sa: *dharma* (in legal contexts), *nyāya*
- el: *έγκυρος*, *άκυρος*, *εγκυρότητα*, *νόμιμος*
- en: *valid*, *invalid*, *legitimate*, *illegitimate*, *lawful*
- tl: *lehitimo*, *lehitimidad*
- sw: *halali*, *uhalali*, *haramu*
- qu: (not documented as a distinct lexical grouping in the available data)

**Distribution.** Clearly lexicalized in six of the eight languages (zh, he, sa, el, en, tl, sw). Not documented as a distinct lexical grouping in Quechua in the available data. Hebrew shows the most elaborate lexical differentiation within this dimension, with *kasher/pasul* merging ritual fitness with legal admissibility in a way not paralleled in other languages. Swahili's *halali/haramu* pair carries both Islamic legal and broader secular evaluative meanings, reflecting the Arabic loanword stratum. In Sanskrit, *dharma* and *nyāya* cover some of the same territory in legal-religious contexts but are not restricted to formal institutional validity.

---

### Dimension 8: Epistemic certainty and the source of knowledge

**Definition.** These expressions evaluate the degree of certainty with which a claim is held, or mark the source from which the speaker's knowledge derives (direct observation, reported information, inference, or conjecture). In some languages this dimension is expressed through dedicated lexical items; in one language (Quechua) it is grammaticalized as an obligatory morphological category.

**Representative terms:**
- zh: 确凿, 千真万确 (high certainty); no dedicated source-marking vocabulary
- he: *uhakika*, *safek*, *bila shaka* (certainty/doubt); no grammaticalized source-marking
- sa: *pramāṇa*, *prāmāṇya*, *svataḥprāmāṇya*, *parataḥprāmāṇya* (source of validity)
- el: *αμφίβολος*, *αδιαμφισβήτητος*, *χωρίς αμφιβολία* (certainty/doubt)
- en: *certain*, *doubtful*, *allegedly*, *reportedly* (lexical source-marking)
- tl: *sigurado*, *hindi sigurado*; *daw* (reportative particle)
- sw: *uhakika*, *shaka*, *bila shaka*; *inasemekana* (lexical reportative)
- qu: *-m/-mi* (direct), *-s/-si* (reportative), *-chá* (conjectural) — grammaticalized

**Distribution.** Present in all eight languages, but with a major structural difference: in Quechua, the source-of-knowledge distinction is grammaticalized as an obligatory morphological category (evidential enclitics), while in all other languages it is expressed lexically or constructionally. The certainty/doubt sub-dimension (positive: certain, without doubt; negative: doubtful, uncertain) is clearly lexicalized in all eight languages. Sanskrit elaborates the source-of-validity sub-dimension most finely through the *pramāṇa* family, distinguishing the means of knowledge, the property of being a valid means, and the debate between intrinsic and extrinsic validity.

---

### Dimension 9: Appearance of truth or genuineness without assertion of actual truth

**Definition.** These expressions evaluate whether something has the appearance or semblance of being true or genuine, without asserting that it actually is. The evaluative target may be a narrative, argument, representation, or object. The dimension is distinct from Dimension 1 (propositional truth) in that it does not commit the speaker to the truth of the claim, and from Dimension 2 (object genuineness) in that it concerns the impression produced rather than the actual status of the entity.

**Representative terms:**
- zh: 逼真, 真实感, 失真, 以假乱真
- he: *ʾemunat shaʾv* (misplaced belief), *ḥatsi ʾemet* (half-truth)
- sa: *ābhāsa*, *pratibhāsa*, *pratirūpa*, *hetvābhāsa*
- el: *αληθοφανής*, *αληθοφάνεια*, *φαινομενικός*, *δήθεν*
- en: *plausible*, *verisimilitude*, *apocryphal*, *specious*
- tl: *parang totoo*, *mukhang totoo*
- sw: *hina* (implied in similative constructions); no dedicated lexical item documented
- qu: *cheqa hina*, *llulla hina rikukun*

**Distribution.** Clearly lexicalized in five languages (zh, sa, el, en, tl). Present constructionally in Tagalog (*parang totoo*, *mukhang totoo*) and Quechua (*cheqa hina*). Not documented as a distinct lexical grouping in Swahili in the available data. Sanskrit elaborates this dimension most systematically through the *ābhāsa* element, which marks pseudo-instances across multiple sub-domains (*pramāṇābhāsa*, *hetvābhāsa*). Greek explicitly lexicalizes the distinction between appearing true (*αληθοφανής*) and being true (*αληθής*). In Chinese, the aesthetic sub-dimension (lifelike representation) is covered by 逼真 and 真实感, while the deceptive sub-dimension (using fakes to confuse the genuine) is covered by 以假乱真.

---

### Dimension 10: Correspondence between a name, label, or description and the actual nature of the thing

**Definition.** These expressions evaluate whether a designation, title, reputation, or description accurately corresponds to the actual properties or nature of the person or thing it is applied to. The evaluative question is not whether a proposition is true but whether a label or category fits its referent. The positive pole asserts that the designation is deserved; the negative pole asserts a mismatch between label and reality.

**Representative terms:**
- zh: 名副其实, 名不副实, 本质, 实质, 真正
- he: *ʾamiti* (in category-membership contexts), *mahut*, *ʿetsem*
- sa: *svarūpa*, *tattva*, *mukhya*, *gauṇa*, *aupacārika*
- el: *ουσιαστικός*, *τυπικός*, *κατ' ουσίαν*, *φαινομενικός*
- en: *genuine* (in category-membership contexts), *real*, *so-called*, *nominal*
- tl: *tunay na* (in category-membership contexts), *tunay na layunin*
- sw: *halisi* (in category-membership contexts), *hali halisi*
- qu: *cheqa runa*, *cheqa kawsay*, *cheqa kaynin*

**Distribution.** Present in all eight languages, but the degree of lexical elaboration varies considerably. Chinese shows the most explicit lexicalization of this dimension through the 名副其实/名不副实 pair and the 本质/实质 cluster. Sanskrit elaborates this dimension through the *mukhya/gauṇa* distinction (primary vs. secondary/figurative use of a term) and the *svarūpa/tattva* cluster (intrinsic nature vs. essential reality). In most other languages, this dimension is covered by the same vocabulary as Dimension 2 (object genuineness) or Dimension 1 (propositional truth), without a dedicated lexical grouping. The dimension is most clearly distinct from Dimensions 1 and 2 in Chinese and Sanskrit.

---

### Dimension 11: Ontological status of being (real vs. unreal vs. indeterminate)

**Definition.** These expressions evaluate whether something genuinely exists or has genuine being, as opposed to being unreal, illusory, or of indeterminate ontological status. The evaluative question is not whether a statement is true or an object is genuine, but whether the entity itself has genuine existence. This dimension is most elaborately developed in Sanskrit, where it constitutes a distinct semantic group with named intermediate categories.

**Representative terms:**
- sa: *sat*, *asat*, *mithyā*, *sattā*, *sattva*, *pratibhāsa*, *pratibhāsika*
- zh: 真实 (in ontological contexts), 现实, 虚假 (in ontological contexts)
- he: *ʾamiti* (in ontological contexts)
- el: *αληθινός* (in ontological contexts), *φαινομενικός*
- en: *real*, *unreal*, *illusory*, *fictitious*
- tl: *tunay* (in ontological contexts)
- sw: *halisi* (in ontological contexts)
- qu: *cheqa* (in ontological contexts)

**Distribution.** Present in all eight languages at the level of basic real/unreal vocabulary, but only Sanskrit develops this into a distinct semantic group with named intermediate categories (*mithyā* as neither real nor unreal) and named levels of analysis (*paramārtha*, *vyāvahārika*, *pratibhāsa*). In all other languages, the ontological dimension is covered by the same vocabulary as Dimension 1 (propositional truth) or Dimension 2 (object genuineness), without a dedicated lexical grouping. The *mithyā* category (neither real nor unreal) and the *paramārtha/vyāvahārika* distinction are specific to Sanskrit in the corpus.

## 4. Evaluative Summary

### Strongly supported cross-linguistically (all 8 languages)

- **D1 (Propositional truth of statements):** Present and clearly lexicalized in all eight languages. The foundational binary of the domain.
- **D2 (Genuineness of objects and documents):** Present and clearly lexicalized in all eight languages. Maintained as distinct from D1 in all languages, though the degree of lexical separation varies.
- **D3 (Conformity to a standard or norm):** Present and clearly lexicalized in all eight languages. The degree of sub-distinction within this dimension varies considerably.
- **D4 (Reliability and credibility of sources):** Present and clearly lexicalized in all eight languages.
- **D5 (Sincerity and honesty of inner disposition):** Present and clearly lexicalized in all eight languages, though merged with D4 in some languages.
- **D6 (Deliberate deception and intentional falsification):** Present and clearly lexicalized in all eight languages. Consistently distinguished from D3 (error without intent).

### Supported in most languages (6–7 of 8)

- **D7 (Legal or institutional validity):** Clearly lexicalized in 7 of 8 languages; not documented as a distinct grouping in Quechua.
- **D8 (Epistemic certainty and source of knowledge):** Present in all 8 languages, but structurally different in Quechua (grammaticalized evidentiality) vs. all others (lexical or constructional). The certainty/doubt sub-dimension is clearly lexicalized in all 8; the source-of-knowledge sub-dimension is grammaticalized only in Quechua.
- **D9 (Appearance of truth without assertion of actual truth):** Clearly lexicalized in 5 languages; constructional in 2 more; not documented in 1 (Swahili).

### Tentative or weakly attested

- **D10 (Correspondence between label and actual nature):** Present in most languages but only clearly lexicalized as a distinct grouping in Chinese and Sanskrit. In most other languages, this territory is covered by D1 or D2 vocabulary without a dedicated grouping.
- **D11 (Ontological status of being):** All languages have real/unreal vocabulary, but only Sanskrit develops this into a distinct semantic group with named intermediate categories. The dimension is weakly attested as a distinct grouping outside Sanskrit.

### Dimensions that split, merge, or vary across specific languages

- **D4 and D5 merge** in Tagalog (*tapat* covers both reliability and sincerity) and Hebrew (*neʾeman* covers both faithfulness and reliability).
- **D3 and moral goodness merge** in Quechua (*allin* covers both correctness and moral goodness) and to some extent in Swahili (*haki* covers both rightness and justice).
- **D7 and religious permissibility merge** in Swahili (*halali/haramu*) and Hebrew (*kasher/pasul*), reflecting the Arabic/Islamic legal stratum in both languages.
- **D8 is grammaticalized** only in Quechua; in all other languages it is expressed lexically or constructionally.
- **D11 is elaborated into a distinct multi-level grouping** only in Sanskrit; in all other languages it is subsumed under D1 or D2.
- **D9 is most systematically lexicalized** in Sanskrit (through the *ābhāsa* element as a cross-domain marker) and Greek (through the *αληθοφανής/αληθής* distinction); it is constructional rather than lexical in Tagalog and Quechua.
- **D10 is most explicitly lexicalized** in Chinese (名副其实/名不副实, 本质/实质) and Sanskrit (*mukhya/gauṇa*, *svarūpa/tattva*); in most other languages it is not maintained as a distinct grouping.

# CLCA Protocol v3.2 — P1b ROOT EXPANSION
**Language: English (en)**

---

## Step 1 — Identify Central Roots

English uses the Latin script natively; transliteration is not applicable for any item below.

---

Examining the P1 items for shared morphological material, three roots emerge as the most frequent structural contributors across the set.

---

**Root 1: *ver-* (Latin *verus* "true")**

Participates in: *verifiable* (item 20), *veridical* (item 18), *veracity* (item 28), and is embedded in *bona fide* only indirectly; more directly, it underlies the productive stem *ver-* visible in *verify*, *verification*, *verifiability* (all derivationally present in item 20), and *veracious* (item 28). Additionally present in *veridical* (item 18).

**Count:** 3 items directly (*verifiable*, *veridical*, *veracity*), with further derivational presence across those entries.

**Core meaning contribution:** Marks correspondence with fact or reality as a property of statements, perceptions, or persons. The root contributes the sense of "being true" as an objective state, particularly in formal, philosophical, and evidential registers.

---

**Root 2: *true* / *truth* (Old English *trēowe*, Proto-Germanic *trewwaz*)**

Participates in: *true* (item 1), *truth* (item 2), *faithful* (item 19, via semantic overlap and derivational family), and is the base for the productive family *truly*, *truthful*, *untruth*, *truthfulness*, *truth-telling*, etc. This is the primary native English root in the domain.

**Count:** 2 items directly (*true*, *truth*), with the widest derivational and collocational productivity of any root in the set.

**Core meaning contribution:** The foundational native-English marker of correspondence with fact, sincerity, and genuineness. Operates across epistemic, moral, and relational domains.

---

**Root 3: *fals-* / *fals-* (Latin *falsus*, from *fallere* "to deceive")**

Participates in: *false* (item 8), *falsify* / *falsely* / *falsehood* / *falsity* (all derivationally present in item 8), and is semantically central to the negative pole of the truth-evaluation domain. Also structurally present in *fraudulent* (item 25) via the shared Latin root *fallere* → *fraus*, though this is an indirect etymological connection rather than a shared surface morpheme.

**Count:** 1 item directly (*false*), but with the highest negative-pole derivational productivity in the set.

**Core meaning contribution:** Marks failure of correspondence with fact, or active deception. The root anchors the falsity/deception end of the truth-evaluation axis.

---

**Decision on scope:** Given that *ver-* and *true/truth* are both highly productive and central, and *fals-* anchors the opposing pole, all three are carried forward. The expansion below covers each in turn.

---

## Step 2 — Expand Each Root

---

### ROOT 1: *ver-* (Latin *verus* "true")

This root surfaces in English primarily through Latin and Old French borrowings. The core productive stem in modern English is *ver-* as found in *verify*, *verity*, *veracity*, *verdict*, *veridical*, *verifiable*, and related forms. It is moderately productive; the forms below represent the full range of well-attested common usage.

---

**1.**
- Form: *verify*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: Latin *verificare* → *verus* "true" + *facere* "to make"
- Natural English translation: to establish or confirm the truth or accuracy of something
- Context of use: Used across scientific, administrative, legal, and everyday contexts to describe the act of checking a claim, identity, or measurement against evidence. "Please verify your email address." "The lab results were independently verified."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**2.**
- Form: *verification*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *verify* + *-ation* (nominalization suffix)
- Natural English translation: the process or result of establishing that something is true or accurate
- Context of use: Used in scientific methodology (verification of hypotheses), administrative procedure (identity verification), and legal contexts (verification of documents). "The verification of the signature took three days."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**3.**
- Form: *verifiability*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *verifiable* + *-ity*; ultimately *verus* + *facere* + *-able* + *-ity*
- Natural English translation: the property of being capable of verification
- Context of use: Used primarily in philosophy of science (the verifiability criterion of meaning, associated with logical positivism) and in epistemological and methodological discussions. "The verifiability of a claim is central to its scientific status."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**4.**
- Form: *unverifiable*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *un-* (negation) + *verifiable*
- Natural English translation: not capable of being verified or checked against evidence
- Context of use: Used in epistemological, journalistic, and legal contexts to flag claims that cannot be confirmed. "The witness's account was unverifiable without additional evidence."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**5.**
- Form: *veracity*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: Medieval Latin *veracitas* → *verax* "truthful" (*verus* + *-ax*) + *-ity*
- Natural English translation: habitual truthfulness; the accuracy of a statement or account
- Context of use: Used in legal contexts to assess witness reliability and in general discourse to evaluate the trustworthiness of persons or reports. "Counsel questioned the veracity of the witness."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**6.**
- Form: *veracious*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *verax* "truthful" (*verus* + *-ax*) + *-ious*
- Natural English translation: habitually truthful; characterized by veracity
- Context of use: Used as a formal or literary adjective to describe persons or accounts that are consistently truthful. Less common than *truthful* or *honest* in everyday speech. "A veracious narrator whose account could be trusted."
- Epistemic status: **[MARGINAL]**

---

**7.**
- Form: *veraciously*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *veracious* + *-ly*
- Natural English translation: in a truthful or accurate manner
- Context of use: Adverbial form of *veracious*; used in formal or literary registers. Rare in everyday speech. "She reported veraciously on what she had witnessed."
- Epistemic status: **[MARGINAL]**

---

**8.**
- Form: *veridical*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: Latin *veridicus* → *verus* "true" + *dicere* "to say" + *-al*
- Natural English translation: corresponding to facts; accurately representing reality (especially of perceptions or experiences)
- Context of use: Used in philosophy of mind and psychology to distinguish perceptions or dreams that accurately represent external reality from illusory or hallucinatory ones. "The question was whether the near-death experience was veridical."
- Epistemic status: **[MARGINAL]**

---

**9.**
- Form: *veridicality*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *veridical* + *-ity*
- Natural English translation: the property of accurately representing reality
- Context of use: Technical term in philosophy of perception and psychology. "Researchers debated the veridicality of the reported experiences."
- Epistemic status: **[MARGINAL]**

---

**10.**
- Form: *veridically*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *veridical* + *-ly*
- Natural English translation: in a manner that accurately represents reality
- Context of use: Used in philosophical and psychological writing. "The subject perceived the scene veridically, without distortion."
- Epistemic status: **[MARGINAL]**

---

**11.**
- Form: *verity*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: Old French *verité*, Latin *veritas* → *verus* "true" + *-itas*
- Natural English translation: a true principle or belief; a fundamental truth
- Context of use: Used in formal, philosophical, and literary registers to refer to established truths or fundamental principles. Often appears in the plural ("eternal verities"). "He spoke of the verities that had guided his life."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**12.**
- Form: *eternal verities*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *eternal* + *verity* (plural)
- Natural English translation: fundamental, enduring truths
- Context of use: A fixed collocation used in philosophical, religious, and literary discourse to refer to truths held to be permanent and universal. "The sermon returned to the eternal verities of faith and duty."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**13.**
- Form: *verdict*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: Anglo-French *verdit* → *ver* (*verus* "true") + *dit* (*dictum* "said")
- Natural English translation: a formal decision or judgment, especially by a jury
- Context of use: Used primarily in legal contexts to refer to the formal finding of a jury or judge. Extended to any authoritative judgment or conclusion. "The jury returned a verdict of not guilty."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**14.**
- Form: *open verdict*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *open* + *verdict*
- Natural English translation: a verdict that does not specify the cause of death or the full facts of a case
- Context of use: Legal and coroner's terminology in British English. Used when evidence is insufficient to determine the precise cause or circumstances. "The inquest returned an open verdict."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**15.**
- Form: *split verdict*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *split* + *verdict*
- Natural English translation: a verdict in which the jury finds the defendant guilty on some counts and not guilty on others
- Context of use: Legal usage. "The jury returned a split verdict, convicting on two of the three charges."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**16.**
- Form: *verism*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: Italian *verismo* → Latin *verus* "true" + *-ism*
- Natural English translation: a style or doctrine in art and literature emphasizing faithful representation of reality, including its harsh or unpleasant aspects
- Context of use: Used in art criticism and literary history, particularly in reference to late nineteenth-century Italian opera and realist movements. "The opera exemplified verism in its unflinching depiction of peasant life."
- Epistemic status: **[MARGINAL]**

---

**17.**
- Form: *verist*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *verus* "true" + *-ist*
- Natural English translation: an adherent of verism; one committed to faithful representation of reality
- Context of use: Used in art criticism and literary discourse. "The verist painters rejected idealization in favor of documentary accuracy."
- Epistemic status: **[MARGINAL]**

---

**18.**
- Form: *verismo*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: Italian → Latin *verus* "true" + *-ismo*
- Natural English translation: a realist movement in Italian opera and literature of the late nineteenth century
- Context of use: Used as a loanword in musicology and literary criticism to refer specifically to the Italian movement. "Puccini's early works are associated with verismo."
- Epistemic status: **[MARGINAL]**

---

**19.**
- Form: *in very truth*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *in* + *very* (from Old French *verai*, Latin *verus*) + *truth*
- Natural English translation: truly; in actual fact
- Context of use: An archaic or literary fixed expression used for emphasis. "In very truth, I knew nothing of the matter."
- Epistemic status: **[MARGINAL]**

---

**Note:** The root *ver-* is moderately productive in English, with the most common forms concentrated in the *verify/verification/verifiable* cluster and the *veracity/veracious* cluster. The *verdict* branch is highly common but largely confined to legal usage. Total well-attested forms number approximately 19; this represents the full range of regular usage.

### ROOT 2: *true* / *truth* (Old English *trēowe*, Proto-Germanic *trewwaz*)

This is the most productive native English root in the truth-evaluation domain. The expansion below covers the 20–30 most common forms; additional compounds and collocations exist beyond those listed.

---

**1.**
- Form: *truly*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *true* + *-ly*
- Natural English translation: in a true manner; genuinely; used as an intensifier
- Context of use: Used as an adverb of manner ("truly representative"), as an intensifier ("truly remarkable"), and in epistolary closings ("Yours truly"). Extremely common across all registers.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**2.**
- Form: *truth*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: Old English *trēowth* → *trēowe* "true" + *-th* (abstract noun suffix)
- Natural English translation: the property of being true; a true statement or fact
- Context of use: Used across philosophical, legal, moral, and everyday contexts. Already in P1 (item 2); listed here as the primary nominalization of the root.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**3.**
- Form: *truthful*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *truth* + *-ful*
- Natural English translation: habitually telling the truth; characterized by truth
- Context of use: Applied to persons, statements, and accounts in moral and evaluative contexts. "She gave a truthful account of what had happened."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**4.**
- Form: *truthfully*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *truthful* + *-ly*
- Natural English translation: in a truthful manner; honestly
- Context of use: Used as an adverb in moral and evaluative contexts. "He answered every question truthfully."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**5.**
- Form: *truthfulness*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *truthful* + *-ness*
- Natural English translation: the quality of being truthful
- Context of use: Used in moral evaluation of persons and in assessments of accounts or testimony. "The court was satisfied with the truthfulness of her statement."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**6.**
- Form: *untruth*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *un-* (negation) + *truth*
- Natural English translation: a false statement; a lie (often used as a euphemism)
- Context of use: Used as a formal or euphemistic alternative to *lie* or *falsehood*, particularly in political and diplomatic discourse. "The report contained several untruths."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**7.**
- Form: *untruthful*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *un-* + *truthful*
- Natural English translation: not truthful; given to telling untruths
- Context of use: Applied to persons or statements in evaluative contexts. "The witness was found to be untruthful on several points."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**8.**
- Form: *untruthfully*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *un-* + *truthfully*
- Natural English translation: in an untruthful manner
- Context of use: Adverbial form; used in legal and evaluative contexts. "He had answered untruthfully under oath."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**9.**
- Form: *untruthfulness*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *un-* + *truthfulness*
- Natural English translation: the quality of being untruthful
- Context of use: Used in moral and legal evaluation. "The untruthfulness of the account was established by documentary evidence."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**10.**
- Form: *truth-telling*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *truth* + *tell* + *-ing*
- Natural English translation: the practice or act of telling the truth
- Context of use: Used in philosophical, ethical, and political discourse to refer to the practice of honest communication, often in contexts where it is difficult or costly. "Truth-telling in wartime carries significant risks."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**11.**
- Form: *truth-teller*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *truth* + *tell* + *-er*
- Natural English translation: a person who tells the truth, especially habitually or at personal cost
- Context of use: Used in moral, political, and literary discourse. "She was regarded as a truth-teller in an era of official deception."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**12.**
- Form: *truth value*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *truth* + *value*
- Natural English translation: the logical value (true or false) assigned to a proposition
- Context of use: Technical term in logic and philosophy of language. "The truth value of a compound sentence depends on the truth values of its components."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**13.**
- Form: *truth condition*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *truth* + *condition*
- Natural English translation: the condition under which a statement is true
- Context of use: Technical term in formal semantics and philosophy of language. "Knowing the meaning of a sentence involves knowing its truth conditions."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**14.**
- Form: *truth-conditional*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *truth* + *conditional*
- Natural English translation: relating to or based on truth conditions
- Context of use: Used in formal semantics and philosophy of language. "Truth-conditional semantics identifies meaning with truth conditions."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**15.**
- Form: *truth table*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *truth* + *table*
- Natural English translation: a table showing the truth values of a compound proposition for all combinations of truth values of its components
- Context of use: Standard tool in propositional logic and introductory logic courses. "Students were asked to construct a truth table for the conditional."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**16.**
- Form: *truth-apt*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *truth* + *apt* "fit, suitable"
- Natural English translation: capable of being true or false; having a truth value
- Context of use: Technical term in philosophy of language and metaethics, used to describe sentences or utterances that can bear truth values. "Whether moral claims are truth-apt is a central question in metaethics."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**17.**
- Form: *truth-aptness*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *truth-apt* + *-ness*
- Natural English translation: the property of being capable of being true or false
- Context of use: Technical term in philosophy of language and metaethics. "The truth-aptness of aesthetic judgments is disputed."
- Epistemic status: **[MARGINAL]**

---

**18.**
- Form: *half-truth*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *half* + *truth*
- Natural English translation: a statement that is only partially true, especially one used to mislead
- Context of use: Used in everyday, journalistic, and political discourse to describe statements that are technically accurate but misleading by omission or framing. "The advertisement was full of half-truths."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**19.**
- Form: *home truth*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *home* + *truth*
- Natural English translation: an unpleasant but true statement about a person, especially one that they need to hear
- Context of use: Used in everyday and literary English. Often appears in the plural. "She told him a few home truths about his behavior."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**20.**
- Form: *gospel truth*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *gospel* (Old English *gōdspel* "good news") + *truth*
- Natural English translation: something regarded as absolutely and unquestionably true
- Context of use: Used in everyday speech, often with irony or emphasis. "He took everything she said as gospel truth."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**21.**
- Form: *truth be told*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *truth* + *be* + *told* (subjunctive/passive construction)
- Natural English translation: to be honest; frankly speaking
- Context of use: A fixed discourse marker used to introduce an honest or candid admission, often one that qualifies or contradicts what has just been said. "Truth be told, I never liked the arrangement."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**22.**
- Form: *in truth*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *in* + *truth*
- Natural English translation: in fact; actually; used to introduce a correction or honest statement
- Context of use: A fixed prepositional phrase used as a discourse marker in formal, literary, and everyday registers. "In truth, the situation was far more complicated."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**23.**
- Form: *of a truth*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *of* + *a* + *truth*
- Natural English translation: truly; in fact
- Context of use: An archaic or literary fixed expression used for emphasis. "Of a truth, I have never seen its equal."
- Epistemic status: **[MARGINAL]**

---

**24.**
- Form: *to tell the truth*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *to tell* + *the* + *truth*
- Natural English translation: to be honest; frankly
- Context of use: A fixed expression used as a discourse marker to introduce a candid statement. "To tell the truth, I was not expecting to win."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**25.**
- Form: *the truth of the matter*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *the truth* + *of the matter*
- Natural English translation: what is actually the case; the real facts
- Context of use: A fixed collocation used in everyday and formal discourse to signal that one is about to state the actual facts. "The truth of the matter is that no decision has been made."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**26.**
- Form: *true to life*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *true* + *to* + *life*
- Natural English translation: accurately representing real life; realistic
- Context of use: Used in aesthetic and literary evaluation to describe representations, characters, or depictions that accurately reflect reality. "The novel's characters were remarkably true to life."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**27.**
- Form: *true to form*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *true* + *to* + *form*
- Natural English translation: behaving in a way that is consistent with one's established pattern or character
- Context of use: Used in everyday and journalistic discourse, often with a slightly ironic tone. "True to form, he arrived an hour late."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**28.**
- Form: *true to type*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *true* + *to* + *type*
- Natural English translation: conforming to the expected characteristics of a category or kind
- Context of use: Used in biological, agricultural, and general evaluative contexts. "The seedlings grew true to type."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**29.**
- Form: *true north*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *true* + *north*
- Natural English translation: geographic north as opposed to magnetic north; also used figuratively for a reliable guiding principle
- Context of use: Used in navigation for the literal sense. Extended figuratively in motivational and philosophical discourse to mean a reliable moral or personal compass. "She described her values as her true north."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**30.**

- Transliteration: N/A

- Natural English translation: genuinely loyal; staunchly committed to a cause or party
- Context of use: Used in British English to describe unwavering loyalty, especially to the Conservative Party. Also used more generally for steadfast loyalty. "He was a true-blue supporter of the club."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

**Additional well-attested forms not fully expanded here** (the root is highly productive): *trueness*, *true-hearted*, *true-heartedness*, *truelove*, *true-born*, *true-bred*, *true-life* (adjective), *come true*, *ring true*, *hold true*, *prove true*, *dream come true*, *too good to be true*, *the truth will out*, *truth serum*, *truth commission*, *post-truth*, *truthiness* (informal/satirical).

### ROOT 3: *fals-* (Latin *falsus*, from *fallere* "to deceive")

This root is the primary negative-pole anchor in the truth-evaluation domain. It is moderately productive in English.

---

**1.**
- Form: *false*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: Old English *fals*, Latin *falsus*, past participle of *fallere* "to deceive"
- Natural English translation: not true; deceptive; not genuine
- Context of use: Already in P1 (item 8); listed here as the base form of the root.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**2.**
- Form: *falsely*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *false* + *-ly*
- Natural English translation: in a false manner; incorrectly; deceptively
- Context of use: Used across legal, moral, and everyday contexts. "He was falsely accused of theft."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**3.**
- Form: *falseness*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *false* + *-ness*
- Natural English translation: the quality of being false or deceptive
- Context of use: Used in moral and evaluative contexts, often with reference to persons or relationships. "She sensed a falseness in his manner."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**4.**
- Form: *falsity*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *false* + *-ity* (via Latin *falsitas*)
- Natural English translation: the quality of being false; a false statement
- Context of use: Used in philosophical, legal, and formal contexts to refer to the property of being false or to a specific false claim. "The falsity of the allegation was quickly established."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**5.**
- Form: *falsehood*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *false* + *-hood* (Old English *-hād*, condition or state)
- Natural English translation: a false statement; the practice of lying
- Context of use: Used in formal, legal, and moral discourse as a more elevated alternative to *lie*. "The report was a tissue of falsehoods."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**6.**
- Form: *falsify*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *false* + *-ify* (via Medieval Latin *falsificare*)
- Natural English translation: to alter or misrepresent so as to make false; to forge
- Context of use: Used in legal, scientific, and administrative contexts to describe deliberate alteration of records, data, or documents. "He was charged with falsifying financial records."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**7.**
- Form: *falsification*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *falsify* + *-ation*
- Natural English translation: the act of falsifying; a falsified version
- Context of use: Used in legal, scientific, and philosophical contexts. In philosophy of science (Popper), also refers to the methodological process of testing and potentially disproving hypotheses. "The falsification of the data was discovered during the audit."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**8.**
- Form: *falsifiability*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *falsifiable* + *-ity*; ultimately *false* + *-ify* + *-able* + *-ity*
- Natural English translation: the property of being capable of being shown to be false by evidence
- Context of use: A central technical term in philosophy of science, associated with Karl Popper's criterion of demarcation between science and non-science. "Falsifiability is often cited as a hallmark of scientific claims."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**9.**
- Form: *falsifiable*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *falsify* + *-able*
- Natural English translation: capable of being shown to be false; testable
- Context of use: Used in philosophy of science and methodology. "A genuinely scientific hypothesis must be falsifiable."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**10.**
- Form: *unfalsifiable*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *un-* + *falsifiable*
- Natural English translation: not capable of being shown to be false; not testable
- Context of use: Used in philosophy of science and critical discourse to characterize claims that cannot be empirically tested. "Critics argued that the theory was unfalsifiable and therefore unscientific."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**11.**
- Form: *falsifier*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Morpheme breakdown: *falsify* + *-er*

## Step 3 — Peripheral Extensions

The following expressions are flagged because the root's contribution appears to shift meaning or function relative to its core truth-domain use. No interpretation or categorization is offered.

---

**From *ver-*:**

- *verdict* — the root *ver-* (true) combines with *dit* (said) to produce a term whose primary function is institutional and procedural rather than epistemic.
- *verism* / *verismo* — the root appears in an aesthetic movement context, applied to artistic style and representation rather than to propositions or testimony.
- *very* (adjective/adverb, from Old French *verai*, Latin *verus*) — in modern English, *very* functions primarily as an intensifier ("very large," "very quickly") with no direct truth-evaluation force; the etymological connection to *verus* is no longer semantically active for most speakers.

---

**From *true* / *truth*:**

- *true north* — used figuratively to mean a guiding moral or personal principle, extending beyond correspondence with fact.
- *true to type* — used in biological and agricultural contexts to describe genetic or phenotypic consistency across generations, where "truth" functions as conformity to a type rather than correspondence with fact.
- *true-blue* — the root marks loyalty and political allegiance rather than epistemic correspondence.
- *truelove* — the root marks the quality of a romantic relationship rather than any epistemic property.
- *come true* — the root appears in a construction describing the realization of hopes or predictions, where the relevant relation is between a prior state (wish, dream) and a subsequent event, rather than between a proposition and a fact.
- *ring true* — the root appears in a perceptual-evaluative idiom where "truth" is assessed through an impression of plausibility or authenticity rather than verified correspondence.
- *too good to be true* — the root appears in an expression about expectation and disappointment rather than direct truth-evaluation.
- *dream come true* — the root marks the fulfillment of a desired state.
- *truthiness* — a satirical/informal coinage (Colbert, 2005) referring to the quality of seeming true based on feeling rather than evidence; the root is deployed in a context that explicitly subverts its normal epistemic function.
- *post-truth* — the prefix *post-* combines with *truth* to produce a term describing a political and epistemic condition rather than a property of individual propositions.

---

**From *fals-*:**

- *falsetto* — from Italian *falsetto* (diminutive of *falso*); refers to a register of the singing voice above the normal range, where "false" marks a quality of vocal production rather than any epistemic or moral property.
- *false economy* — the root marks a mistaken practical judgment about costs and benefits rather than a failure of correspondence with fact.
- *false start* — in athletic contexts, the root marks a procedural violation (premature movement) rather than a propositional or representational failure.
- *false consciousness* — the root appears in a theoretical framework where "falseness" is attributed to a collective social understanding rather than to an individual proposition or statement.
- *false imprisonment* — the root marks legal illegitimacy rather than epistemic falsity.
- *infallible* — the root *fallere* ("to deceive, to fail") surfaces in a form where the primary sense is reliability and freedom from error rather than deception or falsity specifically; the negation *in-* + *fallible* marks a positive property (complete reliability) rather than simply the absence of deception.
- *fallacy* / *fallacious* — the root *fallere* appears in terms that mark logical error or flawed reasoning, where the primary sense is structural invalidity rather than intentional deception.

# CLCA Protocol v3.2 — P3 NATURAL SEMANTIC GROUPING
**Language: English (en)**

---

## Preliminary Notes on Method

Groupings below are derived from observed patterns in P1 and P2: how terms co-occur, what contexts they appear in, what they contrast with, what syntactic frames they enter, and what domains of use they cluster in. No theoretical framework has been imposed. The number of groups, their sizes, and their boundaries reflect the data. Overlaps are noted where they occur. Items that do not fit any identified group are listed at the end.

---

## Group 1: Terms used to evaluate statements and claims against facts

**Label:** Fact-correspondence evaluators

**Definition:** These terms are used to assess whether a statement, account, measurement, or representation matches what is actually the case. They appear in contexts where a claim is being checked against evidence, records, or observable reality. They contrast with each other primarily along dimensions of precision and register rather than meaning.

**Observed usage patterns:**
- *accurate*, *correct*, *exact*, *true* all appear in constructions like *an accurate/correct/exact/true account*, *accurately/correctly/exactly described*
- *accurate* and *exact* are distinguished in use: *accurate* tolerates a margin (*accurate to within three percentage points*, *broadly accurate*), while *exact* implies no deviation (*exact copy*, *exactly right*)
- *correct* is the most common in educational and procedural contexts (*correct answer*, *correct procedure*, *stand corrected*)
- *true* is the broadest and appears in the widest range of syntactic frames, including logical (*truth value*, *truth condition*), everyday (*that's true*, *ring true*), and fixed phrases (*come true*, *hold true*)
- *factually accurate* is a collocation that specifies the level at which accuracy is being assessed, suggesting that *accurate* alone does not always imply factual correspondence
- *sound* overlaps here when applied to arguments and reasoning (*sound argument*, *sound reasoning*, *sound judgment*) but differs in that it evaluates the structure and basis of reasoning rather than the correspondence of a single claim to fact

**Representative expressions from P1 and P2:**
- *true*, *truth*, *accurate*, *accuracy*, *correct*, *correctness*, *exact*, *exactness*, *exactitude*
- *factually accurate*, *accurate account*, *accurate reflection*, *correct answer*, *that is correct*, *exactly right*, *inexact*, *inaccurate*
- *sound argument*, *sound reasoning* (partial overlap)

## Group 2: Terms used to assess whether something is what it claims to be

**Label:** Genuineness assessors

**Definition:** These terms are used when the question is whether an object, document, person, emotion, or identity is what it purports to be, as opposed to being a copy, imitation, or performance. They appear in contexts of authentication, verification of origin, and detection of deception. They do not primarily evaluate propositional truth but rather the status of things and persons.

**Observed usage patterns:**
- *genuine*, *authentic*, *bona fide* all appear in constructions like *genuine article*, *authentic reproduction*, *bona fide offer*, and all contrast with *fake*, *counterfeit*, *spurious*, *bogus*
- *genuine* and *authentic* overlap heavily but show register differences: *authentic* is more common in aesthetic, philosophical (*authentic self*, *authentic voice*, *authentic experience*), and technical authentication contexts (*authenticate a document*, *authentication app*); *genuine* is more common in everyday and emotional contexts (*genuine concern*, *genuine surprise*, *genuine effort*)
- *bona fide* is concentrated in legal and formal contexts (*bona fide purchaser*, *bona fide offer*, *establish one's bona fides*) and implies good faith as well as genuineness
- *legitimate* overlaps here when used to mean "genuine" or "real" (*legitimate business*, *legitimate grievance*) but is primarily a legal-conformity term (see Group 3)
- *faithful* overlaps here when applied to reproductions and translations (*faithful to the original*, *faithful reproduction*, *faithful rendering*) — the question is whether the copy accurately represents the original
- *verify* and *authenticate* are the primary verbal forms for the process of establishing genuineness (*verify a claim*, *authenticate a document*)

**Representative expressions from P1 and P2:**
- *genuine*, *genuinely*, *genuineness*, *authentic*, *authenticity*, *authenticate*, *authentication*, *bona fide*, *bona fides*
- *genuine article*, *prove authentic*, *establish one's bona fides*, *faithful reproduction*, *faithful to the original*, *true copy*
- *fake*, *counterfeit*, *spurious*, *bogus* (as contrasts)

## Group 3: Terms used in legal and formal institutional contexts

**Label:** Legal and institutional validity terms

**Definition:** These terms appear predominantly in legal, administrative, and formal institutional contexts where the question is whether something meets the requirements of law, procedure, or recognized authority. They are used to assess contracts, claims, documents, procedures, and authority rather than to evaluate propositional truth or personal honesty.

**Observed usage patterns:**
- *valid*, *legitimate*, *bona fide*, *fraudulent*, *counterfeit*, *affidavit*, *aver*, *averment*, *verdict*, *true bill* all cluster in legal usage
- *valid* in legal use (*valid contract*, *legally valid*, *no longer valid*, *invalidate*) is distinct from its logical use (*valid argument*); in legal use it means "having legal force"
- *legitimate* in legal use (*legitimate government*, *legitimate expectation*, *legitimate use of force*) means "conforming to law or recognized authority"
- *fraudulent* is almost exclusively legal in its primary use (*fraudulent misrepresentation*, *fraudulent conveyance*, *fraudulent intent*, *fraudulent claim*)
- *counterfeit* carries strong legal connotations of deliberate criminal imitation (*counterfeit currency*, *counterfeiting*)
- *affidavit*, *aver*, *averment*, *verdict*, *true bill* are technical legal terms with no significant use outside legal contexts
- *bona fide* appears in both this group and Group 2; in legal use the emphasis is on good faith and meeting formal requirements (*bona fide purchaser*)
- *veracity* appears frequently in legal contexts specifically for evaluating witness testimony (*veracity of the testimony*, *impugn the veracity*, *vouch for the veracity*)
- *credible* in legal use (*credible witness*, *credible evidence*) overlaps with this group

**Representative expressions from P1 and P2:**
- *valid*, *validity*, *validate*, *invalidate*, *legitimate*, *legitimacy*, *illegitimate*, *fraudulent*, *fraud*, *defraud*, *counterfeit*, *counterfeiter*
- *affidavit*, *aver*, *averment*, *verdict*, *true bill*, *bona fide purchaser*, *fraudulent misrepresentation*, *fraudulent conveyance*, *legitimate expectation*
- *veracity of the testimony*, *credible witness*, *credible evidence* (partial overlap with other groups)

## Group 4: Terms used to evaluate sources, witnesses, and information providers

**Label:** Source trustworthiness evaluators

**Definition:** These terms are used to assess whether a person, source, or institution can be relied upon to provide accurate information consistently. They appear in journalistic, legal, and everyday contexts where the question is not just whether a single claim is true but whether the provider of information is dependable over time.

**Observed usage patterns:**
- *reliable*, *credible*, *trustworthy*, *veracious*, *veracity* all appear in constructions like *reliable source*, *credible witness*, *trustworthy source*, *veracious account*, *veracity of the testimony*
- *reliable* emphasizes consistency of performance (*reliable source*, *reliable narrator*, *reliable information*, *reliably informed*) and is used for instruments and methods as well as persons
- *credible* emphasizes worthiness of belief in a specific instance (*credible threat*, *credible accusation*, *credible evidence*) and is more commonly applied to testimony and claims than to persons as a general character assessment
- *trustworthy* and *trustworthiness* are applied to persons and institutions as a general character assessment (*trustworthy witness*, *trustworthiness of the evidence*)
- *veracity* is concentrated in formal and legal contexts for evaluating testimony (*veracity of the testimony*, *impugn the veracity*, *question the veracity*)
- *veracious* is the most formal and least frequent; it characterizes persons as habitually truthful
- *credibility gap* is a fixed phrase in this cluster, denoting a discrepancy between official claims and public belief
- *unreliable narrator* is a technical literary term in this cluster

**Representative expressions from P1 and P2:**
- *reliable*, *reliability*, *unreliable*, *credible*, *credibility*, *trustworthy*, *trustworthiness*, *veracious*, *veracity*
- *reliable source*, *credible witness*, *credible evidence*, *credibility gap*, *unreliable narrator*, *question the veracity*, *impugn the veracity*, *on good authority*

---

## Group 5: Terms used to describe deliberate deception or fraudulent imitation

**Label:** Deception and fraud descriptors

**Definition:** These terms are used when there is an element of deliberate intent to deceive — to present something false as genuine, or to obtain something through misrepresentation. They appear in legal, journalistic, and everyday contexts and typically imply agency and intent rather than mere error.

**Observed usage patterns:**
- *fake*, *counterfeit*, *fraudulent*, *spurious*, *bogus* all describe things presented as genuine when they are not, but differ in register and specificity
- *counterfeit* is the most legally specific, concentrated on currency, documents, and goods of value (*counterfeit currency*, *counterfeit goods*, *counterfeiting*)
- *fraudulent* is the broadest legal term, covering any deliberate deception for gain (*fraudulent misrepresentation*, *fraudulent claim*, *fraudulent intent*)
- *fake* is the most informal and versatile, used as adjective, noun, and verb across all registers (*fake news*, *fake smile*, *fake it*, *faker*, *fakery*)
- *bogus* is informal and applied broadly to credentials, claims, and persons (*bogus credentials*); it has no productive derivational morphology
- *spurious* is formal and academic, applied to arguments, attributions, and claims (*spurious argument*, *spurious correlation*, *spurious attribution*)
- *fictitious* overlaps here when used in legal contexts (*fictitious name*, *fictitious identity*) but differs in that it can describe invented things without implying intent to deceive (see Group 6)
- *confidence trick*, *con man*, *defraud*, *fraudster* are constructions and derivations that belong to this cluster
- *perfidy* and *perfidious* are formal/literary terms for deliberate betrayal of trust; they overlap with this group but are more specifically about breach of faith than about false representation

**Representative expressions from P1 and P2:**
- *fake*, *faker*, *fakery*, *counterfeit*, *counterfeiter*, *counterfeiting*, *fraudulent*, *fraud*, *defraud*, *fraudster*, *spurious*, *bogus*
- *fake news*, *confidence trick*, *con man*, *fraudulent misrepresentation*, *pass off as genuine*, *counterfeit currency*, *spurious claim*
- *perfidy*, *perfidious* (partial overlap)

## Group 6: Terms used in aesthetic, literary, and representational contexts

**Label:** Representational accuracy and realism terms

**Definition:** These terms are used to evaluate how accurately or convincingly a work, narrative, or representation captures reality or its source. They appear in literary criticism, translation studies, art history, and aesthetic evaluation, where the question is not whether a proposition is true but whether a representation is lifelike, faithful, or convincing.

**Observed usage patterns:**
- *verisimilitude*, *verisimilar*, *verism*, *verismo*, *faithful*, *true to life*, *authentic* all appear in aesthetic and literary contexts
- *verisimilitude* is the most specialized, used in literary criticism and philosophy of science (*air of verisimilitude*, *lend verisimilitude*, *achieve verisimilitude*)
- *faithful* in this context means accurate to the source (*faithful to the original*, *faithful reproduction*, *faithful rendering*, *faithful translation*)
- *true to life* is a fixed phrase meaning "realistic" in aesthetic evaluation
- *authentic* in this context means genuinely representative of a culture, period, or original (*authentic experience*, *authentic reproduction*, *authentic voice*)
- *verism* and *verismo* are specialized art-historical and musicological terms for movements committed to realistic depiction
- *veridical* in psychology and philosophy of perception belongs here when applied to perceptual experiences that accurately represent reality (*veridical perception*)
- *apocryphal* is adjacent to this group when applied to texts and stories of doubtful authenticity, but it is more about historical attribution than aesthetic realism

**Representative expressions from P1 and P2:**
- *verisimilitude*, *verisimilar*, *verism*, *verismo*, *veridical*, *faithful*, *authentic*, *authenticity*
- *air of verisimilitude*, *lend verisimilitude*, *faithful to the original*, *faithful reproduction*, *faithful rendering*, *true to life*, *authentic voice*, *authentic experience*

## Group 7: Terms used to describe the appearance of validity without actual validity

**Label:** Surface-plausibility terms

**Definition:** These terms are used when something appears to be true, valid, or well-founded but is not, or when its actual status is uncertain. They appear in critical, analytical, and journalistic contexts where the gap between appearance and reality is the focus. They differ from the deception group in that they do not necessarily imply deliberate intent.

**Observed usage patterns:**
- *specious*, *plausible*, *spurious*, *apocryphal* all describe things that have the appearance of truth or validity without necessarily having it
- *specious* specifically implies that the appearance of validity is misleading (*specious argument*, *specious reasoning*, *specious claim*); it always carries a negative evaluative force
- *plausible* is neutral or positive — it describes something that seems reasonable and worthy of belief without asserting that it is true (*plausible explanation*, *plausible scenario*, *plausible deniability*); it does not imply deception
- *spurious* implies that the apparent basis is false or without foundation (*spurious correlation*, *spurious attribution*); it is more negative than *plausible* but less focused on deliberate deception than *fraudulent*
- *apocryphal* describes stories and attributions that are widely circulated but of doubtful authenticity (*apocryphal story*, *apocryphal anecdote*); it does not imply deliberate fabrication
- *verisimilitude* overlaps here when used to describe the appearance of truth in a narrative that may not be factually accurate
- *half-truth* belongs here: it describes a statement that has enough truth to seem plausible but is used to mislead

**Representative expressions from P1 and P2:**
- *specious*, *speciousness*, *plausible*, *plausibility*, *implausible*, *spurious*, *apocryphal*, *half-truth*
- *specious argument*, *plausible deniability*, *plausible explanation*, *spurious correlation*, *apocryphal story*, *widely regarded as apocryphal*, *barely plausible*

---

## Group 8: Terms used in philosophical and technical epistemological discourse

**Label:** Technical epistemological and logical terms

**Definition:** These terms appear primarily in philosophical, logical, and scientific methodology contexts where truth, validity, and verification are treated as technical concepts with precise definitions. They are largely absent from everyday speech and concentrated in academic and specialist registers.

**Observed usage patterns:**
- *verifiable*, *verifiability*, *verification*, *verificationism*, *verificationist*, *verification principle*, *truth value*, *truth table*, *truth condition*, *truth-conditional*, *veridical*, *veridicality*, *sound* (in the logical sense) all cluster in this register
- *valid* in its logical sense (*valid argument* = conclusion follows necessarily from premises) belongs here, distinct from its legal and everyday uses
- *sound* in its logical sense (*sound argument* = valid and with true premises) belongs here
- *truth value* and *truth table* are technical tools of propositional logic
- *truth condition* and *truth-conditional* are central to formal semantics
- *verificationism* and *verification principle* are associated with logical positivism
- *veridical* and *veridicality* are used in philosophy of perception
- *verisimilitude* in Popperian philosophy of science (degree of approximation to truth) belongs here, distinct from its literary use
- *ground truth* in machine learning and remote sensing belongs here as a technical term for reference data used to validate models

**Representative expressions from P1 and P2:**
- *verifiable*, *verifiability*, *unverifiable*, *verification*, *verificationism*, *verificationist*, *verification principle*
- *truth value*, *truth table*, *truth condition*, *truth-conditional*, *valid argument*, *sound argument*, *veridical*, *veridicality*, *ground truth*

## Group 9: Terms used to describe personal honesty and sincerity

**Label:** Personal honesty and sincerity terms

**Definition:** These terms are used to evaluate persons and their expressions with respect to whether they genuinely mean what they say or habitually tell the truth. They appear in moral, personal, and interpersonal contexts and focus on the alignment between inner state and outward expression, or on habitual disposition toward truthfulness.

**Observed usage patterns:**
- *sincere*, *truthful*, *veracious*, *honest* (implied throughout P1 and P2 as a near-synonym cluster) all characterize persons as genuinely meaning what they say or habitually telling the truth
- *sincere* focuses on the alignment between inner state and outward expression (*sincere apology*, *sincerely believe*, *insincerity*); it is used for expressions and intentions as well as persons
- *truthful* focuses on the habit of telling the truth (*truthful person*, *truthful statement*, *untruthful*); it is more commonly applied to persons than *true* is
- *veracious* is the most formal and least common; it characterizes persons and accounts as habitually truthful (*veracious account*, *veraciously reported*)
- *truth-teller* is the agent noun for a person who tells the truth; it is used in moral and political discourse
- *truth-telling* is the practice or virtue of telling the truth
- *good faith* and *bad faith* belong here when used in interpersonal and moral contexts (as opposed to their legal uses): they evaluate whether a person is acting with genuine honest intent
- *sincere* and *genuine* overlap here: *a genuine apology* and *a sincere apology* are near-synonymous in everyday use
- *diffident* and *diffidence* are peripheral to this group: they derive from the *fid-* root and relate to self-trust rather than honesty toward others

**Representative expressions from P1 and P2:**
- *sincere*, *sincerely*, *sincerity*, *insincere*, *insincerity*, *truthful*, *truthfully*, *truthfulness*, *untruthful*, *veracious*, *veracity*
- *truth-teller*, *truth-telling*, *good faith*, *bad faith*, *in good faith*, *yours sincerely*, *yours faithfully*

---

## Group 10: Terms used in political and media discourse about truth and deception

**Label:** Political and media truth discourse terms

**Definition:** These terms have developed specific uses in political, journalistic, and media contexts, often in relation to public communication, propaganda, and the management of information. Several of them are relatively recent coinages or have acquired new meanings in contemporary political discourse.

**Observed usage patterns:**
- *post-truth*, *fake news*, *truthiness*, *half-truth*, *economical with the truth*, *speak truth to power*, *truth commission*, *truth squad*, *credibility gap*, *plausible deniability* all cluster in political and media discourse
- *post-truth* describes a condition in which emotional appeals outweigh factual evidence in public discourse
- *fake news* is used both for fabricated news stories and as a political epithet applied to legitimate journalism
- *truthiness* (Colbert, 2005) describes the quality of seeming true based on feeling rather than evidence
- *economical with the truth* is a fixed ironic phrase for deliberate misleading without technically lying
- *speak truth to power* is a fixed phrase for challenging authority with uncomfortable facts
- *truth commission* and *truth squad* are institutional terms for bodies that investigate or monitor truth claims
- *credibility gap* describes a discrepancy between official claims and public belief
- *plausible deniability* describes the ability to deny knowledge of actions taken by subordinates
- *half-truth* describes a statement that is partially true but used to mislead
- *inconvenient truth* and *uncomfortable truth* are collocations for facts that are true but unwelcome

**Representative expressions from P1 and P2:**
- *post-truth*, *fake news*, *truthiness*, *half-truth*, *economical with the truth*, *speak truth to power*
- *truth commission*, *truth squad*, *credibility gap*, *plausible deniability*, *inconvenient truth*, *uncomfortable truth*, *truth and reconciliation*

---

## Group 11: Terms used in contexts of trust, loyalty, and fidelity to commitment

**Label:** Trust and fidelity terms

**Definition:** These terms are used when the question is whether a person, institution, or relationship can be relied upon over time, or whether a commitment has been honored. They appear in personal, legal, and institutional contexts and focus on reliability of persons and relationships rather than accuracy of statements.

**Observed usage patterns:**
- *faithful*, *trustworthy*, *reliable*, *fidelity*, *good faith*, *bona fide*, *perfidy*, *breach of faith*, *infidelity* all appear in contexts of ongoing commitment and relational trust
- *faithful* in this context means loyal and committed (*faithful servant*, *faithful to one's word*, *unfaithful* in marriage)
- *fidelity* covers both personal loyalty (*fidelity to a spouse*) and accurate reproduction (*high fidelity*); the two uses are distinct but share the same word
- *trustworthy* and *trustworthiness* evaluate persons and institutions as deserving of ongoing trust
- *good faith* and *bad faith* in legal and interpersonal contexts evaluate whether a party is acting with honest intent
- *perfidy* and *perfidious* are formal/literary terms for deliberate betrayal of trust (*perfidious Albion*, *perfidy in international law*)
- *breach of faith* describes a violation of trust or promise
- *infidelity* in its primary contemporary use refers to marital unfaithfulness
- *fiduciary* and *fiduciary duty* are the legal expressions of this cluster: a fiduciary relationship is one in which one party is legally obligated to act in the best interests of another
- *semper fidelis* is an institutional expression of this cluster
- *confidence* in the sense of trust (*confidence in a person*, *in confidence*) belongs here, distinct from its statistical use

**Representative expressions from P1 and P2:**
- *faithful*, *faithfully*, *faithfulness*, *unfaithful*, *fidelity*, *infidelity*, *trustworthy*, *trustworthiness*, *good faith*, *bad faith*, *in good faith*, *breach of faith*
- *fiduciary*, *fiduciary duty*, *perfidy*, *perfidious*, *affidavit*, *semper fidelis*, *confidence*, *confidant*, *confide*

## Ambiguous and Overlapping Items

The following items show significant overlap between groups and cannot be assigned to a single group without distortion:

**legitimate**
- Belongs to Group 2 (genuineness assessors) when used to mean "genuine" or "real" (*legitimate business*, *legitimate grievance*)
- Belongs to Group 3 (legal and institutional validity) when used to mean "conforming to law or recognized authority" (*legitimate government*, *legitimate expectation*)
- The two uses are distinct in context but share the same word form

**credible**
- Belongs to Group 4 (source trustworthiness evaluators) when applied to witnesses and sources (*credible witness*, *credible source*)
- Belongs to Group 3 (legal and institutional validity) when applied to evidence in legal proceedings (*credible evidence*)
- Belongs to Group 7 (surface-plausibility terms) when applied to claims and threats (*credible threat*, *credible accusation*) — here the question is whether the claim is believable, not whether the source is trustworthy

**faithful**
- Belongs to Group 2 (genuineness assessors) and Group 6 (representational accuracy) when applied to reproductions and translations (*faithful reproduction*, *faithful to the original*)
- Belongs to Group 11 (trust and fidelity) when applied to persons and commitments (*faithful servant*, *unfaithful*)

**bona fide**
- Belongs to Group 2 (genuineness assessors) when used to mean "genuine" (*bona fide member*, *the genuine article*)
- Belongs to Group 3 (legal and institutional validity) when used in legal contexts (*bona fide purchaser*, *establish one's bona fides*)
- Belongs to Group 11 (trust and fidelity) when the emphasis is on good faith (*in good faith*, *bona fide offer*)

**spurious**
- Belongs to Group 5 (deception and fraud descriptors) when it implies deliberate false attribution (*spurious claim*, *spurious attribution*)
- Belongs to Group 7 (surface-plausibility terms) when it describes something that lacks genuine basis without implying deliberate deception (*spurious correlation*)

**authentic**
- Belongs to Group 2 (genuineness assessors) when applied to objects and documents (*authentic document*, *prove authentic*)
- Belongs to Group 6 (representational accuracy and realism) when applied to experiences, voices, and cultural representations (*authentic experience*, *authentic voice*, *authentic self*)

**sound**
- Belongs to Group 1 (fact-correspondence evaluators) when applied to judgments and advice (*sound judgment*, *sound advice*)
- Belongs to Group 8 (technical epistemological and logical terms) when used in its strict logical sense (*sound argument* = valid with true premises)

**veracity**
- Belongs to Group 4 (source trustworthiness evaluators) when used to evaluate witnesses and accounts
- Belongs to Group 3 (legal and institutional validity) when used as a technical legal term for witness testimony

**plausible deniability**
- Belongs to Group 7 (surface-plausibility terms) as a construction involving *plausible*
- Belongs to Group 10 (political and media truth discourse) as a term of political and intelligence discourse

## Items That Do Not Fit Cleanly into Any Group

**veritas**
Used primarily in institutional mottoes (*Veritas* — Harvard) and philosophical invocations. It functions as a proper noun or quasi-proper noun in English rather than as a productive common word. It does not participate in the usage patterns of any of the groups above in a way that would make group membership meaningful.

**in vino veritas**
A fixed proverbial phrase. It belongs to the general truth domain but does not cluster with any of the groups above in terms of usage context or contrast patterns. It is used as a standalone proverb rather than as part of a productive construction type.

**verismo / verism / verist**
These are specialized art-historical and musicological terms. They belong to Group 6 (representational accuracy and realism) in principle, but their use is so domain-specific (Italian opera, late 19th-century realist art movements) that they do not participate in the broader usage patterns of that group.

**truism**
*Truism* is derived from *true* but functions differently from all other items in the truth domain: it describes a statement that is *too obviously* true to be worth stating. It does not fit Group 1 (which evaluates whether claims are true) or Group 9 (which evaluates personal honesty). Its primary function is dismissive or rhetorical. It is noted here as an item that inverts the normally positive valence of the *true* root without fitting any of the identified groups.

**truthiness**
Belongs to Group 10 (political and media truth discourse) but is also a novel coinage that occupies a unique position: it describes a subjective sense of truth that is explicitly contrasted with evidence-based truth. It could be placed in Group 7 (surface-plausibility terms) as well, but its specific political and media context makes Group 10 the better fit. It is flagged here because its status as a recent coinage means its group membership may not be stable.

**diffident / diffidence**
These derive from the *fid-* root but their primary use is to describe shyness and lack of self-confidence rather than anything in the truth or trust domain. They do not fit any of the groups above. They are noted as items where the etymological connection to the *fid-* root does not correspond to any semantic connection to the groups identified here.

**Fido**
A proper name deriving from the *fid-* root. It does not participate in any of the usage patterns documented above. It is noted as a marginal item with no group membership.

**of a truth**
An archaic/biblical phrase. It does not participate in contemporary usage patterns and does not fit any group in terms of current use.

# CLCA Protocol v3.4 — P4a CONSTRUCTIONS
**Language: English (en)**

---

## 1. ATTRIBUTION

### Pattern
Copular predication: **Subject + BE + truth-domain adjective**

### Examples
- *The account is accurate.* → N/A → The account is accurate.
- *His testimony was truthful.* → N/A → His testimony was truthful.

### Notes
The primary attributive construction uses the copula *be* with a predicative adjective. All major truth-domain adjectives (*true*, *accurate*, *correct*, *genuine*, *authentic*, *valid*, *credible*, *reliable*, *sound*, *sincere*, *plausible*, *spurious*, *fraudulent*, *legitimate*) enter this frame freely. No morphological change occurs on the adjective.

---

## 2. NEGATIVE ATTRIBUTION

### Pattern
Three competing strategies: **(a) un-/in-/im- prefix + adjective**, **(b) NOT + adjective**, **(c) semantically distinct antonym**

### Examples
- *The document is inauthentic.* → N/A → The document is inauthentic.
- *The claim is not genuine.* / *The claim is fake.* → N/A → The claim is not genuine. / The claim is fake.

### Notes
English shows asymmetry across the domain. Some items have productive prefixed negatives (*inaccurate*, *incorrect*, *invalid*, *insincere*, *implausible*, *unreliable*, *illegitimate*, *inauthentic*, *untruthful*, *unsound*); others lack them and require periphrasis (*not genuine*, *not bona fide*) or a lexically distinct antonym (*fake*, *bogus*, *counterfeit* as negatives of *genuine*/*authentic*). No single strategy is universal.

---

## 3. ADNOMINAL MODIFICATION

### Pattern
**Prenominal attributive adjective + noun** (dominant order); postnominal predicative construction also available

### Examples
- *a credible witness* → N/A → a credible witness
- *fraudulent misrepresentation* → N/A → fraudulent misrepresentation

### Notes
English strongly favors prenominal adjective placement (*accurate account*, *valid argument*, *genuine concern*, *spurious correlation*, *plausible deniability*). Postnominal position is available only in predicative constructions (*the witness was credible*) or in fixed legal phrases (*attorney general*-type patterns, not productive here). Several truth-domain collocations are strongly lexicalized in prenominal position (*fake news*, *counterfeit currency*, *bona fide offer*).

---

## 4. BECOME / INCHOATIVE

### Pattern
**(a) BECOME + adjective** (general inchoative); **(b) PROVE + adjective** (evidential inchoative); **(c) TURN OUT + TO BE + adjective** (discovery inchoative)

### Examples
- *The rumor proved true.* → N/A → The rumor proved true.
- *The document turned out to be fraudulent.* → N/A → The document turned out to be fraudulent.

### Notes
English lacks a dedicated inchoative morpheme for this domain. The construction *become true/valid/authentic* is grammatical but uncommon; *prove* and *turn out to be* are the preferred inchoative-evidential frames, encoding the discovery or confirmation of a truth-domain property. *Come true* is a fixed idiom restricted to wishes, predictions, and fears.

---

## 5. CAUSATIVE

### Pattern
**(a) MAKE + NP + adjective** (periphrastic causative); **(b) derivational verbal causative** (*validate*, *authenticate*, *verify*, *legitimize*, *correct*, *falsify*)

### Examples
- *The expert authenticated the painting.* → N/A → The expert established the painting as authentic.
- *New evidence validated the hypothesis.* → N/A → New evidence made the hypothesis valid / confirmed its validity.

### Notes
English has no productive morphological causative suffix for this domain. The causative is expressed either periphrastically (*make X true/valid*) or through dedicated verbal derivations: *authenticate*, *validate*, *verify*, *legitimize*, *correct*, *falsify*, *invalidate*, *delegitimize*. These verbal forms are not mechanically derived from all adjectives in the domain — *\*genuinize*, *\*accuratize*, *\*plausibilize* are unattested — producing a lexical gap pattern documented in P2a.

---

## 6. ABSTRACT NOMINALIZATION

### Pattern
**Base adjective/verb + nominalizing suffix** (*-ity*, *-ness*, *-cy*, *-tion*, *-ude*, *-th*)

### Examples
- *The authenticity of the document was disputed.* → N/A → The authenticity of the document was disputed.
- *The verification of the claim took three days.* → N/A → The verification of the claim took three days.

### Notes
English uses multiple competing suffixes for abstract nominalization in this domain, with no single suffix dominant: *-ity* (*validity*, *credibility*, *authenticity*, *sincerity*, *plausibility*); *-ness* (*correctness*, *soundness*, *genuineness*, *spuriousness*); *-cy* (*accuracy*, *legitimacy*); *-tion* (*verification*, *authentication*, *validation*, *correction*); *-ude* (*exactitude*, *verisimilitude*); *-th* (*truth*). Suffix selection is lexically determined and not predictable from the base form. Several items have competing nominalizations with distinct register or meaning (*exactness* vs. *exactitude*; *truthfulness* vs. *truth*).

---

## 7. AGENTIVE NOMINALIZATION

### Pattern
**Verbal or nominal base + agentive suffix** (*-er*, *-or*, *-ster*) or **compound** (*truth-teller*, *con man*)

### Examples
- *She is a truth-teller who refuses to flatter the powerful.* → N/A → She is a person who tells the truth.
- *The counterfeiter was convicted on three counts.* → N/A → The person who produces counterfeits was convicted.

### Notes
Agentive nominalization is productive for the deception/fraud cluster (*faker*, *counterfeiter*, *defrauder*, *fraudster*, *con man*) and for the verification cluster (*authenticator*, *validator*, *verifier*). For positive truth-telling, the compound *truth-teller* is preferred over a suffixed form; no *\*truther* (in the neutral sense) or *\*verifier-of-claims* is standard. The negative/deceptive agents have richer morphological expression than the positive/honest agents, an asymmetry preserved from P2a.

---

## 8. EVIDENTIAL AND DISCOURSE-MARKING CONSTRUCTIONS

### Pattern
**Sentence-initial or parenthetical fixed phrase** signaling the speaker's epistemic commitment to what follows

### Examples
- *Truth be told, the plan was never viable.* → N/A → To tell the truth, the plan was never viable.
- *In truth, the situation was far more complex than reported.* → N/A → In reality, the situation was far more complex than reported.

### Notes
English has a productive set of sentence-adverbial and parenthetical constructions that frame the following proposition as honest or corrective: *truth be told*, *in truth*, *to tell the truth*, *if truth be told*, *frankly*, *honestly*. These are not derivations of truth-domain adjectives but fixed phrases functioning as epistemic stance markers. They signal that the speaker is departing from a polite or expected formulation to state what they actually believe. This construction type has no equivalent in the derivational or attributive patterns above and is documented here as an emergent category from the P2b data.

---

## 9. VERBAL IDIOMS WITH TRUTH-DOMAIN PREDICATES

### Pattern
**Intransitive or copular verb + truth-domain element** in a fixed or semi-fixed phrase (*ring true*, *hold true*, *come true*, *stand corrected*)

### Examples
- *His explanation didn't quite ring true.* → N/A → His explanation did not seem genuine or plausible.
- *The same principle holds true across all cases.* → N/A → The same principle remains valid across all cases.

### Notes
English has a set of fixed verbal idioms in which a truth-domain element (*true*, *corrected*) combines with a verb to produce a meaning not fully predictable from the parts: *ring true/false*, *hold true*, *come true*, *stand corrected*, *prove true*. These are distinct from the copular attribution pattern (§1) in that the verb is not *be* and the construction is idiomatic or semi-fixed. They are concentrated around the base form *true* and have no parallel idiom set for most other truth-domain adjectives.

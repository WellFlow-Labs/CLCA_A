## Narrative analysis

Across the languages, several recurring partition patterns are well supported.

A very common pattern is a distinction between:
1. **truth/factuality of statements or reports**,
2. **genuineness/authenticity of things, persons, or identities**, and
3. **correctness/propriety/rightness**.

This three-way tendency is especially clear in Georgian, Finnish, Basque, Indonesian, Turkish, and Korean. For example, Georgian separates **სიმართლე** ‘truth’, **ნამდვილი** ‘genuine/real’, and **სწორი** ‘correct/right’; Finnish separates **totuus/totta**, **aito/autenttinen**, and **oikea**; Indonesian separates **benar**, **asli**, and **tepat / sesuai / cocok**; Korean separates **진실/사실**, **진짜/정품**, and **올바르다/옳다**.

Another recurring pattern is a separate lexical area for **reliability/credibility** rather than truth itself. This is strongly attested in Georgian (**სარწმუნო, უტყუარი, დამაჯერებელი**), Finnish (**luotettava, uskottava**), Basque (**fidagarri, sinesgarri**), Tamil (**நம்பகமான**), Indonesian (**andal**, plus validity and evidential passives), Turkish (**güvenilir, inandırıcı**), and Korean (**신뢰성, 믿을 만하다**). In these languages, “can be trusted” and “is true” are not treated as the same lexical judgment.

A further recurring pattern is a distinct area for **formal validity / legitimacy / institutional acceptability**. This is especially fine-grained in Finnish, Indonesian, Turkish, and Korean. Indonesian is particularly clear here with **sah, absah, valid**; Turkish with **geçerli, meşru, haklı**; Korean with **유효하다, 무효, 정당하다, 타당하다**. Georgian also has a formal evaluative cluster (**მართებული, მართებულობა**) and a legal cluster (**მართლმსაჯულება, მართლზომიერი**), though the boundaries are organized somewhat differently.

Many languages also have a separate cluster for **essence / underlying nature / what something really is**, rather than ordinary truth of propositions. This is visible in Georgian (**არსი**), Finnish (**olemus**), Basque (**funtsean**, plus essence-related uses), Tamil (**மெய்ப்பொருள், தத்துவம், சாரம்**), Indonesian (**hakikat, jati diri**), Turkish (**öz, asıl**), and Korean (**본질, 실체**). This is a recurring cross-language pattern, not just a single-language peculiarity.

A second broad cross-language pattern is that many languages do **not** rely on a simple general verb meaning “become true.” Instead, they often encode truth dynamically through:
- **revelation**,
- **verification**,
- **confirmation**,
- **exposure of falsity**, or
- **institutional recognition**.

This is explicitly supported in Georgian, Yoruba, Basque, Tamil, Turkish, and Korean, and also visible in Indonesian and Finnish. Korean is especially clear: truth is often something that **드러나다** ‘is revealed’, **밝히다** ‘is brought to light’, or **확인하다** ‘is checked’, rather than something that simply “becomes true.” Tamil similarly prefers **உண்மை வெளிவரு** and **உண்மை தெரியவரு**. Georgian prefers “truth became clear/was established” rather than a general inchoative predicate.

Where languages split distinctions more finely, Finnish, Indonesian, Turkish, Georgian, and Korean stand out.

- **Finnish** has one of the finest-grained systems, with clear lexical separation among truth, reality, genuineness, correctness, error, justice, legality, reliability, credibility, proof, identity, and essence.
- **Indonesian** also splits the field finely, especially between **truth/correctness** (*benar*), **precision** (*tepat, akurat*), **conformity** (*sesuai*), **fit** (*cocok*), **authenticity** (*asli*), **validity** (*sah, absah, valid*), and **reliability** (*andal*).
- **Turkish** has many overlapping but still distinct roots: **doğru, gerçek, hakikat, geçerli, meşru, haklı, yerinde, isabetli, sahici, hakiki, özgün**, etc.
- **Georgian** sharply distinguishes everyday truth, elevated truth, authenticity, correctness, precision, credibility, and formal validity.
- **Korean** distinguishes everyday **진짜/가짜**, formal truth/fact terms (**진실, 사실**), formal falsity (**허위**), product authenticity (**정품, 위작**), accuracy (**정확하다**), correctness (**올바르다, 옳다**), and institutional validity (**유효하다, 무효**).

By contrast, some languages merge distinctions more strongly.

The clearest case is **Yoruba**, where the *tọ́ / òtítọ́* family covers **truth, correctness, rightness, and straightness**. The data explicitly shows that factual truth and practical/moral rightness are less sharply separated there than in most of the other languages. Yoruba still has separate areas for evidence, authenticity, and justice, but the central truth/correctness lexicon is more merged.

**Tamil** also shows a kind of internal merger, but of a different type: everyday truth/reality is centered on **உண்மை / நிஜம்**, while a large **மெய்** family covers truth, reality, realization, essence, spiritual knowledge, and several peripheral domains. So Tamil does not simply merge truth and correctness; rather, it has two partly overlapping subsystems with different registers and functions.

**Basque** also shows some broad items, especially **egiazko** and **benetako**, which can cover truth, genuineness, and reality depending on context. But Basque still maintains clear oppositions with **gezur**, **gezurrezko**, **zuzen**, and **oker**.

Several languages show asymmetries or missing contrasts relative to others.

A recurring gap is the lack of a clear, common lexicalized **truth-teller** noun parallel to “liar.” This is explicitly noted for Georgian, Finnish, and Korean, and effectively also for Yoruba and Basque in the supplied material. Korean has **거짓말쟁이** ‘liar’, but the positive side is usually expressed descriptively, not by an equally central lexicalized counterpart. Georgian likewise lacks a clearly established common agentive truth-teller in the supplied data.

Another recurring asymmetry is that some languages have strong lexical means for **fake/false/forged**, but weaker or less symmetrical positive counterparts in the same subdomain, or vice versa. Korean has a very strong everyday **진짜/가짜** contrast and a separate formal authenticity vocabulary, but not all dynamic patterns are equally available on both sides. Indonesian has strong **asli/palsu** and strong validity contrasts, but no equally clear common “become genuine” expression from **asli**. Tamil has strong **பொய்** and strong revelation/concealment patterns, but no equally clear general “make true” verb.

Some distinctions found in many languages are less clearly lexicalized in others. For example, the distinction between **reliability** and **credibility/plausibility** is very clear in Finnish, Georgian, Turkish, and Korean, but less richly elaborated in Yoruba, where evidence and certainty are strong but the lexical split between “reliable” and “merely convincing” is not developed to the same degree in the supplied material.

Likewise, a strong lexical distinction between **formal validity** and **ordinary truth** is very clear in Finnish, Indonesian, Turkish, and Korean, but less central in Yoruba and Basque. Basque has verification and correctness vocabulary, but the supplied material does not show as large a formal validity subsystem as Indonesian or Korean. Yoruba has documentary and evidential vocabulary, but not the same dense lexical field for legal/procedural validity.

Some observations are clearly single-language rather than cross-language patterns.

- The broad merger of truth/correctness/rightness/straightness in **Yoruba** is a language-specific observation.
- The large literary-philosophical **மெய்** subsystem in **Tamil** is also language-specific.
- The especially strong everyday/formal split between **진짜/가짜** and **정품/위작/허위** in **Korean** is a language-specific configuration.
- The especially explicit dynamic/resultative patterns in **Finnish** such as **tulla todeksi** and **osoittautua epätodeksi** are more developed than in most of the other languages in the dataset.
- The Georgian split between **სიმართლე** and **ჭეშმარიტება** is also a language-specific register distinction, though other languages do show elevated vs everyday truth vocabulary in different ways.

By contrast, the following are well-attested cross-language patterns:
- separation of truth, authenticity, and correctness in many languages;
- separate reliability/credibility vocabulary in many languages;
- separate essence/underlying-nature vocabulary in many languages;
- strong fake/forged vs genuine contrasts in many languages;
- preference for revelation/verification constructions rather than a general “become true” verb in many languages.

Overall, the languages do not partition the field identically, but the data repeatedly supports a few broad tendencies: many languages distinguish **what is true**, **what is genuine**, **what is correct**, **what is valid**, and **what is trustworthy**, while differing in how sharply those distinctions are lexicalized and how much they are merged in central roots.

# CLCA Protocol v3.2 — P3 NATURAL SEMANTIC GROUPING
# Language: Swahili (sw)

---

## Preliminary Note on Method

Groupings below are derived from patterns of co-occurrence, contrast, and contextual distribution observed across P1 and P1b/P2a/P2b documentation. No external semantic framework has been imposed. Where items appear in multiple groups, this is noted explicitly. Items that do not fit any observed grouping are listed at the end. Group sizes are uneven and reflect the data, not a desire for symmetry.

---

## GROUP 1: Expressions used to assert or affirm that a statement is true

**Label:** Assertion-of-truth expressions

**Definition:** These expressions are used by a speaker to affirm that a proposition corresponds to fact, or to frame a statement as sincere and accurate. They appear in contexts where the speaker is either making a direct claim of truth or intensifying the credibility of what they are saying. They contrast with expressions in Group 4 (denial or accusation of falsehood) and are distinct from Group 3 expressions, which evaluate correctness of procedures or documents rather than propositional truth.

**Representative expressions:**

- *kweli* — used as a bare assertion ("truth / truly / really") or as a discourse particle inviting confirmation (*Kweli?*)
- *ukweli* — the nominalized form used when truth is the topic of discourse (*ukweli wa mambo*, "the truth of the matter")
- *kweli kweli* — reduplicated intensifier used in spoken Swahili to emphasize that something is genuinely true
- *kwa kweli* — adverbial phrase framing a statement as sincere or factually grounded (*kwa kweli nakuambia*, "I tell you truly")
- *kwa kweli kweli* — further intensified form of the above, more colloquial
- *kweli kabisa* — "absolutely true"; uses *kabisa* as a postposed intensifier
- *kweli ni kweli* — tautological copular construction used rhetorically to assert that a fact cannot be denied
- *Hii ni kweli* — basic copular predication asserting that something is true
- *hakika* — used as a discourse particle or noun affirming certainty (*hakika kabisa*, "absolutely certain")
- *kwa hakika* — adverbial phrase asserting that something is definitely the case
- *bila shaka* — "without doubt"; fixed expression used as a strong affirmation of truth or certainty
- *dhahiri* — "evident, obvious"; used to assert that something is plainly and visibly true
- *Nakuambia kweli* — "I am telling you the truth"; periphrastic assertion construction

**Notes:** *hakika* and *bila shaka* shade into Group 2 (certainty expressions) and are listed in both. *kweli* in its discourse-particle use (*Kweli?*) is more of an interactional device than a truth assertion, but it belongs to the same cluster of usage.

## GROUP 2: Expressions used to evaluate the degree of certainty or reliability of information

**Label:** Certainty and reliability expressions

**Definition:** These expressions are used to evaluate whether a piece of information, a source, or a claim can be relied upon or is known with confidence. They appear in epistemic contexts where the speaker is assessing how sure they or others are, and they contrast with *shaka* (doubt) and *bila uhakika* (without certainty). They differ from Group 1 in that they focus on the epistemic status of the knower or source rather than on the propositional content of a claim.

**Representative expressions:**

- *uhakika* — "certainty, reliability, assurance"; the abstract nominal used when evaluating whether something is known for sure
- *hakika* — overlaps with Group 1 but also used specifically in epistemic evaluation of confidence level
- *bila uhakika* — "without certainty"; used to describe a state of not knowing for sure
- *kwa uhakika* — "with certainty, reliably"; adverbial phrase
- *hakika kabisa* — "absolutely certain"
- *Nina uhakika* — "I am sure / I have certainty"
- *Sina uhakika* — "I am not sure / I have no certainty"; extremely common in everyday epistemic discourse
- *shaka* — "doubt, uncertainty"; the primary expression of the opposite pole
- *bila shaka* — "without doubt"; overlaps with Group 1 but specifically addresses the absence of epistemic uncertainty
- *kuhakikisha* — "to make sure, to verify, to confirm"; the causative verb used when someone acts to establish certainty
- *uhakikisho* — "assurance, guarantee, verification"; the nominal form of the above
- *uaminifu wa chanzo* — "reliability of the source"; used in journalistic and academic contexts
- *uaminifu wa habari* — "reliability of information"

**Notes:** *kuhakikisha* and *uhakikisho* also appear in Group 6 (verification and authentication expressions) because they are used in procedural contexts of confirming facts or documents. *shaka* and *bila shaka* are the clearest markers of the contrast that defines this group.

## GROUP 3: Expressions used to evaluate correctness or accuracy of statements, documents, and procedures

**Label:** Correctness and accuracy expressions

**Definition:** These expressions are used to evaluate whether a statement, calculation, document, answer, or procedure meets a standard of accuracy or correctness. They are particularly common in educational, administrative, and legal contexts. They differ from Group 1 in that they evaluate against a standard or norm (correct/incorrect) rather than asserting correspondence to fact in a general sense.

**Representative expressions:**

- *sahihi* — "correct, accurate, right, exact"; the core evaluative adjective in this group
- *usahihi* — "correctness, accuracy, precision"; the abstract nominal form
- *Ni sahihi* — "It is correct"; copular affirmation of correctness
- *Si sahihi* — "It is incorrect"; negative copular evaluation
- *sahihi kabisa* — "absolutely correct"
- *jibu sahihi* — "correct answer"; extremely common in educational contexts
- *taarifa sahihi* — "accurate report"
- *nakala sahihi* — "certified true copy"
- *sahihi na kamili* — "accurate and complete"; paired expression used in legal declarations
- *tarehe sahihi* — "correct date"
- *kwa usahihi* — "accurately, precisely, with precision"
- *usahihi wa takwimu* — "accuracy of data"
- *usahihi wa lugha* — "linguistic accuracy, grammatical correctness"
- *usahihi wa habari* — "accuracy of information"
- *usahihi wa tafsiri* — "accuracy of translation"
- *kusahihisha* — "to correct, to rectify, to mark (examinations)"
- *usahihishaji* — "correction, the process of marking or editing"
- *msahihishaji / wasahihishaji* — "corrector(s), examiner(s), marker(s)"

**Notes:** *sahihi* also has a distinct use as "signature" (see Group 7), which is a separate usage cluster. The correctness/accuracy cluster is the dominant one in terms of frequency and range of contexts.

---

## GROUP 4: Expressions used to identify or accuse something as false, deceptive, or fraudulent

**Label:** Falsehood and deception expressions

**Definition:** These expressions are used to identify a statement, object, or person as false, deceptive, or not genuine. They appear in contexts of accusation, moral condemnation, legal evaluation, and commercial fraud detection. Several of these expressions contrast directly with authenticity expressions in Group 5.

**Representative expressions:**

- *uongo* — "lie, falsehood, deception"; the primary nominal for false statements; antonym of *ukweli*
- *Hii ni uongo* — "This is a lie / This is false"
- *Piga uongo* — "Tell a lie / Lie"; the most colloquial periphrastic construction for the act of lying
- *Anapiga uongo* — "He/she is lying"
- *uongo mtupu* — "a complete lie, pure falsehood"
- *Uongo una miguu mifupi* — "A lie has short legs"; widely attested proverb
- *Uongo ni dhambi* — "Lying is a sin"; used in religious and moral instruction
- *kudanganya* — "to deceive, to lie, to mislead"
- *udanganyifu* — "deception, deceitfulness, dishonesty"
- *danganyifu* — "deceptive, deceitful"
- *mdanganyifu / wadanganyifu* — "deceiver(s), liar(s)"
- *kudanganywa* — "to be deceived"
- *kudanganyika* — "to be susceptible to deception"
- *ghushi* — "fraud, forgery, adulteration"; carries a stronger connotation of deliberate criminal fraud than *bandia*
- *kughushi* — "to forge, to commit fraud"
- *mghushi / waghushi* — "forger(s), fraudster(s)"
- *Hii ni ghushi* — "This is fraudulent / This is a forgery"
- *Si kweli* — "It is not true / That is not true"
- *nusu ukweli* — "half-truth"; describes a statement that is technically accurate but misleading

**Notes:** *bandia* overlaps with this group but is treated as the core of Group 5 because its primary usage is in identifying non-genuine objects rather than in moral condemnation of deceptive acts. *ghushi* and *kudanganya/udanganyifu* are more strongly associated with deliberate deception and moral/legal condemnation. *nusu ukweli* is a borderline item: it describes something that is not entirely false but is used in contexts of criticism and exposure.

---

## GROUP 5: Expressions used to evaluate whether an object, person, or practice is genuine or counterfeit

**Label:** Genuineness and counterfeit expressions

**Definition:** These expressions are used to evaluate whether an item, person, or practice is the real thing or an imitation. They are particularly common in commercial, legal, and cultural contexts. The contrast between *halisi* (genuine) and *bandia* (fake) is the clearest organizing opposition in this group. These expressions differ from Group 4 in that they focus on the status of objects and things rather than on the acts or character of persons.

**Representative expressions:**

- *halisi* — "genuine, authentic, real, pure"; the primary positive evaluative adjective
- *uhalisi* — "authenticity, genuineness, reality"; the abstract nominal
- *dhahabu halisi* — "genuine gold"
- *bidhaa halisi* — "genuine goods"; contrasts directly with *bidhaa bandia*
- *Hii ni halisi* — "This is genuine"
- *Si halisi* — "It is not genuine / It is fake"
- *mtu halisi* — "a genuine person, a person of integrity"
- *upendo halisi* — "genuine love"
- *bandia* — "fake, counterfeit, artificial, imitation"; the primary negative evaluative adjective
- *pesa bandia* — "counterfeit money"
- *bidhaa bandia* — "fake goods"
- *dawa bandia* — "fake medicine, counterfeit drugs"
- *hati bandia* — "forged document"
- *habari bandia* — "fake news"
- *picha bandia* — "fake image, manipulated photo"
- *akili bandia* — "artificial intelligence"
- *Hii ni bandia* — "This is fake"
- *asili* — "origin, original, authentic, indigenous"; used to evaluate whether something is of the original kind
- *wa asili* — "original, native, natural, authentic"; fixed adjectival expression
- *ghushi* — overlaps here in the sense of fraudulent/adulterated goods (*bidhaa za ghushi*, *pesa za ghushi*)

**Notes:** *asili* and *wa asili* are included here because their primary evaluative use is in distinguishing original or native things from introduced or imitation ones, which aligns with the genuineness/counterfeit contrast. However, *asili* also has uses in cultural and philosophical discourse about origins that do not fit this group. *ghushi* overlaps with both this group and Group 4. *uhalisi* also has a distinct use in literary/aesthetic discourse (see ungrouped items below).

## GROUP 6: Expressions used in contexts of verifying, confirming, or proving something

**Label:** Verification and confirmation expressions

**Definition:** These expressions are used when a speaker or institution acts to establish, confirm, or demonstrate that something is true, authentic, or correct. They appear in legal, administrative, academic, and everyday procedural contexts. They differ from Group 1 (asserting truth) in that they describe a process or act of establishing truth rather than simply claiming it.

**Representative expressions:**

- *thibitisha* — "to confirm, to verify, to prove, to authenticate"
- *kuthibitisha* — full infinitival form of the above
- *uthibitisho* — "confirmation, verification, proof, authentication, attestation"
- *kuthibitishwa* — "to be confirmed, to be verified, to be authenticated"
- *mthibitisho* — "a confirmation, a proof, a certificate"; refers to a specific document or instance of proof
- *kuhakikisha* — "to make sure, to ensure, to verify, to confirm"; overlaps with Group 2
- *uhakikisho* — "assurance, guarantee, verification, confirmation"
- *hakikisha* — imperative: "make sure, ensure, verify"
- *kudhihirisha* — "to reveal, to demonstrate, to make evident, to show clearly"
- *udhihirisho* — "demonstration, manifestation, revelation, evidence, proof"
- *kudhihirika* — "to become evident, to be revealed, to become apparent"
- *dhahiri* — overlaps here in the sense of something being demonstrably evident; also in Group 1

**Notes:** *kuhakikisha* is used in both everyday procedural contexts (making sure a door is closed) and in epistemic verification contexts (confirming a fact). The procedural uses do not involve truth evaluation at all, which means this verb spans this group and contexts entirely outside the truth domain. *thibitisha* and its derivatives are more consistently used in contexts where truth or authenticity is specifically at issue.

---

## GROUP 7: Expressions used in the context of signing or authenticating documents

**Label:** Document signature and authentication expressions

**Definition:** These expressions cluster around the use of *sahihi* in its extended sense of "signature," and are used in administrative, legal, and commercial contexts where a document requires a signature or formal authentication. This usage is distinct from the correctness/accuracy uses of *sahihi* documented in Group 3, though both derive from the same form.

**Representative expressions:**

- *sahihi ya mkono* — "handwritten signature, manual signature"
- *sahihi ya kidole* — "fingerprint signature, thumbprint used as signature"
- *sahihi ya dijiti* — "digital signature, electronic signature"
- *weka sahihi* — "sign here, put your signature, affix a signature"
- *tia sahihi* — "sign, put a signature, affix a signature"; alternative to *weka sahihi*
- *uthibitisho* — overlaps here in the sense of formal authentication of a document

**Notes:** This group is relatively small and tightly defined. It is included because the usage pattern is clearly distinct from other uses of *sahihi* and because the expressions form a coherent cluster in administrative and legal practice. *uthibitisho* overlaps with Group 6.

---

## GROUP 8: Expressions used to assert or evaluate rights and entitlements

**Label:** Rights and entitlement expressions

**Definition:** These expressions are used to assert, claim, or evaluate what someone is legally or morally entitled to. They are organized around *haki* in its rights-and-entitlement sense and are particularly common in legal, political, and advocacy discourse. This usage of *haki* is distinct from its use in asserting factual truth (which overlaps with Group 1) and from its use in evaluating moral rightness (which overlaps with Group 9).

**Representative expressions:**

- *haki za binadamu* — "human rights"
- *haki za kiraia* — "civil rights"
- *haki ya kupiga kura* — "the right to vote"
- *haki ya elimu* — "the right to education"
- *haki ya afya* — "the right to health"
- *haki ya kujieleza* — "the right to self-expression, freedom of expression"
- *haki ya mali* — "property rights"
- *haki ya kisheria* — "legal right, statutory right"
- *haki ya kimila* — "customary right, traditional right"
- *haki za wanawake* — "women's rights"
- *haki za watoto* — "children's rights"
- *haki za ardhi* — "land rights"
- *haki za wafanyakazi* — "workers' rights"
- *haki za watu wenye ulemavu* — "rights of persons with disabilities"
- *haki ya njia* — "right of way"
- *haki ya mwandishi* — "author's right, copyright"
- *haki yako* — "your right, what is rightfully yours"
- *haki yangu* — "my right, what is rightfully mine"
- *Pata haki yako* — "Get your rights, claim what is rightfully yours"
- *Tetea haki* — "Defend rights, advocate for justice"
- *uhaki* — "justice, rightness" as an abstract quality

**Notes:** *haki* is the most semantically wide-ranging item in the entire P1 inventory, and its uses span Groups 1, 8, and 9. The rights-and-entitlement cluster is the most lexically productive of the three, generating the largest number of attested *haki ya/za X* constructions.

---

## GROUP 9: Expressions used in moral evaluation of fairness, justice, and rightness

**Label:** Moral rightness and fairness expressions

**Definition:** These expressions are used to evaluate actions, situations, or outcomes as morally right or wrong, fair or unfair. They appear in everyday moral discourse, in religious instruction, and in legal contexts where moral evaluation is at issue. They differ from Group 8 in that they evaluate the moral quality of a situation rather than asserting a specific entitlement.

**Representative expressions:**

- *Ni haki* — "It is right / It is just / It is fair"
- *Si haki* — "It's not right / It's unjust / It's unfair"; extremely common in everyday moral protest
- *haki na usawa* — "justice and equality, rights and equity"
- *ukweli na haki* — "truth and justice"
- *halali* — "lawful, legitimate, permissible, rightful"
- *uhalali* — "legitimacy, lawfulness, legality"
- *kuhalalisha* — "to legitimize, to legalize, to authorize"
- *uhalalishaji* — "legitimization, legalization"
- *haramu* — "forbidden, unlawful, illicit, wrong"; the antonym of *halali*
- *kuharamisha* — "to prohibit, to make unlawful, to criminalize"
- *usawa* — "equality, balance, fairness, equivalence"
- *sawa* — "correct, equal, right, okay, fair"
- *sawasawa* — "exactly, precisely, equally, just right"
- *kusawazisha* — "to equalize, to balance, to level, to make equal"
- *Uongo ni dhambi* — "Lying is a sin"; moral predication
- *haki ya Mungu* — "God's truth, divine justice"; invokes divine authority in moral evaluation
- *haki itendeke* — "let justice be done, may justice prevail"
- *Alipata haki yake* — "He/she got what he/she deserved"; retributive justice expression

**Notes:** *halali* and *haramu* have both religious-legal and broader secular uses. In their religious-legal uses they belong specifically to Islamic jurisprudential discourse; in their broader uses they function as general moral evaluators. *sawa* and *usawa* are included here because their evaluative uses in discourse about fairness and equity are well-attested, though *sawa* also functions as a simple confirmation marker ("okay, correct") that does not involve moral evaluation. *haki* again spans this group and Group 8.

---

## GROUP 10: Expressions used to evaluate trustworthiness of persons or sources

**Label:** Trustworthiness and credibility expressions

**Definition:** These expressions are used to evaluate whether a person, institution, or source of information can be trusted or relied upon. They appear in moral evaluation of character, in journalistic assessment of sources, and in religious discourse about faith and belief. They differ from Group 2 (certainty expressions) in that they evaluate the trustworthiness of an agent or source rather than the epistemic status of a proposition.

**Representative expressions:**

- *amini* — "trustworthy, faithful, reliable"; also used as a verb (*kuamini*, to believe/trust)
- *kuamini* — "to believe, to trust, to have faith in"
- *aminifu* — "trustworthy, faithful, reliable, honest"
- *uaminifu* — "faithfulness, trustworthiness, loyalty, integrity, reliability"
- *mwaminifu / waaminifu* — "faithful/trustworthy person/people, believer(s)"
- *imani* — "faith, belief, trust, conviction"; shares Arabic root with *amini*
- *mtu wa kuamini* — "a trustworthy person, a person to be trusted"
- *Nakuamini* — "I believe you / I trust you"
- *Siamini* — "I don't believe it / I don't trust it"
- *Niamini* — "Believe me / Trust me"
- *Usiamini kila kitu* — "Don't believe everything"
- *Usiamini uongo* — "Don't believe lies"
- *uaminifu wa habari* — "reliability of information"
- *uaminifu wa chanzo* — "reliability of the source"
- *kwa uaminifu* — "faithfully, trustworthily, with integrity"

**Notes:** *imani* is included here because it shares the Arabic root with *amini* and is consistently treated as related in Swahili usage, but its primary domain is religious faith rather than interpersonal trust. The relationship between *kuamini* (to trust a person) and *kuamini* (to believe a proposition) means this group overlaps with Group 2 at the edges.

## AMBIGUOUS AND OVERLAPPING ITEMS

The following items appear in multiple groups and cannot be cleanly assigned to one:

**haki** — Appears in Groups 1 (truth assertion, as in *haki ya kweli*), 8 (rights and entitlements), and 9 (moral rightness). This is the most semantically distributed item in the inventory. Its three usage clusters are distinct enough that no single group captures it adequately.

**dhahiri** — Appears in Group 1 (asserting that something is plainly true) and Group 6 (making something evident through demonstration). The difference is between *dhahiri* as a predicate ("it is obvious") and *kudhihirisha* as a verb of demonstration ("to show clearly").

**kuhakikisha / uhakikisho** — Appear in both Group 2 (certainty) and Group 6 (verification). The everyday procedural use (*hakikisha mlango umefungwa*, "make sure the door is closed") does not involve truth evaluation at all and falls outside both groups.

**ghushi** — Appears in both Group 4 (deception and fraud) and Group 5 (genuineness and counterfeit). The distinction is between *ghushi* as a characterization of fraudulent conduct (Group 4) and *ghushi* as a characterization of fraudulent objects (Group 5).

**sahihi** — Appears in Group 3 (correctness and accuracy) and Group 7 (document signatures). These are two distinct usage clusters of the same form.

**bila shaka** — Appears in both Group 1 (asserting truth) and Group 2 (certainty). It functions as an affirmation of both propositional truth and epistemic confidence.

**sawa** — Appears in Group 9 (moral fairness) and also functions as a simple confirmation marker ("okay, that's fine") that does not involve truth or moral evaluation at all. This latter use is not well-captured by any group in this inventory.

## ITEMS THAT DO NOT FIT CLEARLY INTO ANY GROUP

**haki ya kweli** — Listed in P1 as [UNCERTAIN]. This expression combines two items from different groups (*haki* and *kweli*) and is used for emphasis. It does not have a stable enough usage pattern to belong to a distinct group, and it overlaps with both Group 1 and Group 8 without fitting either cleanly.

**uhalisi** in literary/aesthetic discourse — The use of *uhalisi* to mean "literary realism" as a movement or style (*uhalisi wa kisanaa*, *uhalisi katika fasihi*) does not fit the genuineness/counterfeit contrast of Group 5 or any other group in this inventory. It is a domain-specific technical use.

**akili bandia** — "Artificial intelligence." This use of *bandia* is noted in P2b as emerging. It does not fit Group 5 (genuineness/counterfeit) in the same way as *pesa bandia* or *bidhaa bandia*, because artificial intelligence is not typically framed as a counterfeit of natural intelligence in Swahili discourse. It is included in Group 5 as an attested form but its fit is imperfect.

**ukweli unaosimama** — "Truth that stands, enduring truth." Listed as [MARGINAL] in P1b. This expression belongs to a rhetorical register (sermon, oratory) that is not well-represented by any group in this inventory.

**uharamishaji** — Listed as [MARGINAL] in P2a. Its usage is too restricted to place it confidently in Group 9 alongside the more common *haramu* and *kuharamisha*.

**uhakika wa chakula / uhakika wa usalama** — "Food security / security assurance." These uses of *uhakika* are noted in P1b as peripheral extensions into the domain of material reliability and stability. They do not fit Group 2 (epistemic certainty) in the same way as *Sina uhakika* or *kwa uhakika*, and they do not fit any other group. They represent a usage of *uhakika* that has moved away from the truth domain entirely.

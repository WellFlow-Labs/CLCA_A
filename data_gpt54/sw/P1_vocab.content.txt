1. **kweli**  
   **Transliteration:** kweli  
   **Literal meaning / morphology:** basic lexical item; ‘truth, true’  
   **Typical English translations:** true, truth, real, indeed  

   Used very commonly to affirm that a statement is accurate or that something is genuinely the case. It also appears in expressions such as *ni kweli* “it is true” and can mark emphatic confirmation in everyday speech.  
   **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

2. **uongo**  
   **Transliteration:** uongo  
   **Literal meaning / morphology:** noun; ‘falsehood, lie’  
   **Typical English translations:** lie, falsehood, untruth  

   This is the ordinary word for a lie or something false. It is used for false statements, deception, and sometimes broader accusations that a claim or report is not true.  
   **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

3. **-kweli**  
   **Transliteration:** -kweli  
   **Literal meaning / morphology:** adjectival stem related to *kweli*; ‘true, genuine’  
   **Typical English translations:** true, genuine, real  

   As an adjectival stem, it occurs with agreement, for example *wa kweli*, *ya kweli*, *cha kweli*. It is used for genuine persons or things, not only for true propositions, as in contrasts between authentic and fake or superficial cases.  
   **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

4. **-ongo**  
   **Transliteration:** -ongo  
   **Literal meaning / morphology:** adjectival stem from *uongo*; ‘false, lying’  
   **Typical English translations:** false, untrue, deceitful  

   This stem is used in agreeing forms such as *si ya/wa kweli bali ya/wa uongo* and in expressions referring to false statements or deceptive behavior. It is less independent than *uongo* but is clearly part of ordinary evaluative usage around falsity.  
   **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

5. **sahihi**  
   **Transliteration:** sahihi  
   **Literal meaning / morphology:** borrowing from Arabic; ‘correct, exact, valid’  
   **Typical English translations:** correct, accurate, right, valid  

   This is widely used for correctness in answers, forms, spelling, calculations, and official details. It also extends to precision and exactness, especially in educational, bureaucratic, and informational contexts.  
   **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

6. **usahihi**  
   **Transliteration:** usahihi  
   **Literal meaning / morphology:** *u-* abstract noun from *sahihi*; ‘correctness, accuracy’  
   **Typical English translations:** correctness, accuracy, precision  

   This noun is used when discussing the quality of being correct or accurate, for example in writing, reporting, or measurement. It is common in formal and semi-formal registers, especially where evaluation of exactness is explicit.  
   **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

7. **-sahihi**  
   **Transliteration:** -sahihi  
   **Literal meaning / morphology:** adjectival use of *sahihi*; ‘correct, proper’  
   **Typical English translations:** correct, proper, accurate  

   In agreement with noun classes, this stem describes answers, procedures, forms, or identifications as correct. It is especially common in school, administrative, and instructional language.  
   **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

8. **halisi**  
   **Transliteration:** halisi  
   **Literal meaning / morphology:** lexical item; ‘real, actual, genuine’  
   **Typical English translations:** genuine, authentic, real, actual  

   This word is used for things considered genuine rather than fake, artificial, or merely apparent. It can describe material objects, identity, value, or the actual nature of a situation.  
   **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

9. **uhalisia**  
   **Transliteration:** uhalisia  
   **Literal meaning / morphology:** *u-* abstract noun from *halisi*; ‘reality, realism, genuineness’  
   **Typical English translations:** reality, realism, authenticity, genuineness  

   This noun appears in philosophical, literary, and general evaluative contexts concerning what is real or realistic. It can refer either to reality itself or to realism/authenticity in representation, depending on context.  
   **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

10. **bandia**  
    **Transliteration:** bandia  
    **Literal meaning / morphology:** lexical item; ‘fake, imitation, counterfeit’  
    **Typical English translations:** fake, counterfeit, imitation, bogus  

    This is used for objects, documents, medicines, money, or credentials judged not to be genuine. It is common in legal, commercial, and everyday contexts where authenticity is in question.  
    **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

11. **feki**  
    **Transliteration:** feki  
    **Literal meaning / morphology:** borrowing from English “fake”  
    **Typical English translations:** fake, bogus, counterfeit  

    This form is common in colloquial and media usage, especially in urban speech, for fake goods, fake accounts, or fake information. It overlaps with *bandia* but often sounds more informal and contemporary.  
    **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

12. **aminifu**  
    **Transliteration:** aminifu  
    **Literal meaning / morphology:** from root related to trust/faith; ‘trustworthy, faithful, honest’  
    **Typical English translations:** trustworthy, faithful, honest, reliable  

    This adjective is used for persons whose conduct or statements can be relied on. It often carries a moral dimension, evaluating character as dependable and sincere rather than merely factually correct.  
    **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

13. **uaminifu**  
    **Transliteration:** uaminifu  
    **Literal meaning / morphology:** *u-* abstract noun from *aminifu*; ‘trustworthiness, honesty, faithfulness’  
    **Typical English translations:** honesty, trustworthiness, integrity, faithfulness  

    This noun is common in moral, religious, and social evaluation of character. It refers to the quality of being reliable and honest in speech, conduct, and obligations.  
    **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

14. **kuaminika**  
    **Transliteration:** kuaminika  
    **Literal meaning / morphology:** verb with stative/passive-like extension from *-amini*; ‘to be believable/trustworthy’  
    **Typical English translations:** be credible, be trustworthy, be reliable  

    This verb is used of people, reports, institutions, and evidence that can be trusted or believed. It is especially useful when evaluating whether a source or claim deserves confidence.  
    **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

15. **aminika**  
    **Transliteration:** aminika  
    **Literal meaning / morphology:** adjective/participle from *kuaminika*; ‘credible, trustworthy’  
    **Typical English translations:** credible, trustworthy, reliable  

    This form commonly describes testimony, sources, witnesses, and institutions as believable or dependable. It is frequent in journalism, public discourse, and formal evaluation of reliability.  
    **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

16. **-fanana**  
    **Transliteration:** -fanana  
    **Literal meaning / morphology:** verbal root; ‘resemble each other, be alike’  
    **Typical English translations:** resemble, be similar, look alike  

    This verb is used when judging whether one thing matches or resembles another in appearance or character. It is relevant in evaluating likeness, comparison, and representational similarity.  
    **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

17. **mfano**  
    **Transliteration:** mfano  
    **Literal meaning / morphology:** noun; ‘example, model, likeness’  
    **Typical English translations:** example, model, likeness, image  

    Besides its very common sense “example,” this word also appears in contexts of likeness, pattern, or representation. It can be used when something is judged as a model or resemblance of something else.  
    **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

18. **asili**  
    **Transliteration:** asili  
    **Literal meaning / morphology:** lexical item; ‘origin, nature, essence, original state’  
    **Typical English translations:** nature, essence, origin, original, authentic  

    This word is used for what something fundamentally is, where it comes from, or its original condition. It is common in discussions of identity, authenticity, and essential character, including philosophical and cultural contexts.  
    **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

19. **ya asili**  
    **Transliteration:** ya asili  
    **Literal meaning / morphology:** ‘of origin/of the original kind’  
    **Typical English translations:** original, natural, authentic, genuine  

    This fixed expression is often used for products, materials, or conditions considered natural or original rather than artificial or adulterated. It can also mark authenticity in contrast with imitation.  
    **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

20. **haki**  
    **Transliteration:** haki  
    **Literal meaning / morphology:** lexical item; ‘right, justice, due claim’  
    **Typical English translations:** right, justice, rightful claim, fairness  

    This is a central term in moral, legal, and social evaluation of what is right or just. It can refer both to abstract justice and to a person’s right or entitlement.  
    **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

21. **-enye haki**  
    **Transliteration:** -enye haki  
    **Literal meaning / morphology:** possessive construction ‘having justice/right’  
    **Typical English translations:** just, fair, rightful, legitimate  

    This expression is used to describe actions, decisions, or persons as fair or just. It is especially natural where Swahili uses a descriptive construction rather than a simple inherited adjective.  
    **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

22. **batili**  
    **Transliteration:** batili  
    **Literal meaning / morphology:** borrowing of Arabic origin; ‘void, null, invalid, false’  
    **Typical English translations:** invalid, null, void, false  

    This term is common in legal, religious, and formal evaluative language for something lacking validity or standing. It can apply to arguments, contracts, claims, or acts judged not to hold as right or valid.  
    **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

23. **halali**  
    **Transliteration:** halali  
    **Literal meaning / morphology:** borrowing from Arabic; ‘lawful, permitted, legitimate’  
    **Typical English translations:** lawful, legitimate, permissible, valid  

    This word is used in legal, religious, and everyday contexts for what is permitted or legitimate. It evaluates conduct, status, or possession in terms of rightness and acceptability under a norm.  
    **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

24. **haramu**  
    **Transliteration:** haramu  
    **Literal meaning / morphology:** borrowing from Arabic; ‘forbidden, illicit, illegitimate’  
    **Typical English translations:** forbidden, illegal, illicit, illegitimate  

    This is the common opposite of *halali* in many contexts. It is used for conduct, goods, earnings, or relations judged wrong, unlawful, or religiously prohibited.  
    **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

25. **dhahiri**  
    **Transliteration:** dhahiri  
    **Literal meaning / morphology:** borrowing from Arabic; ‘apparent, evident, manifest’  
    **Typical English translations:** evident, obvious, apparent, manifest  

    This word is used when the truth or character of something is considered plainly visible or unmistakable. It often appears in formal or elevated style when evaluating whether something is clearly the case.  
    **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

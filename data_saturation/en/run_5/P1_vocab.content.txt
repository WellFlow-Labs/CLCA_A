# CLCA Protocol v3.2 — P1 CORE VOCABULARY COLLECTION
**Language: English (en)**

---

**Note on transliteration:** English uses the Latin script natively; transliteration is not applicable for any item below.

---

**1. true**
- Form: *true*
- Literal meaning: Old English *trēowe* "faithful, trustworthy"; related to Proto-Germanic *trewwaz*
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Applied to statements, propositions, beliefs, and representations to indicate correspondence with fact or reality. Also used of persons and things to indicate faithfulness or genuineness ("a true friend," "true gold"). Participates in productive derivation: *truly*, *truth*, *truthful*, *untruth*.
- **[CORE]**

---

**2. truth**
- Form: *truth*
- Literal meaning: Old English *trēowth*; nominalization of *true*
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Refers to the property of being true, or to a true statement or fact. Used across philosophical, legal, moral, and everyday contexts. Appears in fixed expressions such as *tell the truth*, *the truth of the matter*, *in truth*.
- **[CORE]**

---

**3. accurate**
- Form: *accurate*
- Literal meaning: Latin *accuratus*, past participle of *accurare* "to take care of" (*ad-* + *cura* "care")
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Applied to descriptions, measurements, representations, and reports to indicate close correspondence with fact or a target. Commonly used in scientific, journalistic, and legal registers. Productive derivation: *accuracy*, *accurately*, *inaccurate*.
- **[CORE]**

---

**4. genuine**
- Form: *genuine*
- Literal meaning: Latin *genuinus* "native, natural"; possibly related to *genu* "knee" (placed on the knee to acknowledge paternity) or *genus* "birth, kind"
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Applied to objects, persons, emotions, and documents to indicate that they are what they purport to be, not counterfeit or simulated. Contrasts with *fake*, *spurious*, *counterfeit*. Productive derivation: *genuinely*, *genuineness*.
- **[CORE]**

---

**5. authentic**
- Form: *authentic*
- Literal meaning: Greek *authentikos* "original, genuine, principal" (*authentes* "one acting on one's own authority")
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Applied to objects, documents, artworks, and persons to indicate originality, legitimacy, or fidelity to an original. In philosophical and popular discourse, also applied to persons or behavior to indicate consistency with one's true self. Productive derivation: *authenticity*, *authenticate*, *authentically*, *inauthenticity*.
- **[CORE]**

---

**6. valid**
- Form: *valid*
- Literal meaning: Latin *validus* "strong, effective" (*valere* "to be strong")
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: In logic, applied to arguments whose conclusions follow necessarily from their premises. In legal and administrative contexts, applied to documents, contracts, and claims that meet required conditions. Productive derivation: *validity*, *validate*, *invalid*, *validation*.
- **[CORE]**

---

**7. correct**
- Form: *correct*
- Literal meaning: Latin *correctus*, past participle of *corrigere* "to set right" (*com-* + *regere* "to rule, guide")
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Applied to answers, statements, behavior, and procedures to indicate conformity with a standard, rule, or fact. Used in both epistemic contexts (correct answer) and normative ones (correct conduct). Productive derivation: *correctly*, *correctness*, *incorrect*, *correction*.
- **[CORE]**

---

**8. false**
- Form: *false*
- Literal meaning: Old English *fals*, from Latin *falsus* "deceived, mistaken, feigned," past participle of *fallere* "to deceive"
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Applied to statements and propositions to indicate failure to correspond with fact. Also applied to persons, appearances, and objects to indicate deceptiveness or inauthenticity ("false friend," "false bottom"). Productive derivation: *falsely*, *falseness*, *falsity*, *falsify*, *falsehood*.
- **[CORE]**

---

**9. real**
- Form: *real*
- Literal meaning: Medieval Latin *realis* "actual" (*res* "thing")
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Applied to entities, properties, and states to indicate that they exist or obtain, as opposed to being imagined, simulated, or apparent. Also used to contrast genuine instances with imitations ("real leather," "the real thing"). Productive derivation: *really*, *reality*, *realism*, *unreal*.
- **[CORE]**

---

**10. reliable**
- Form: *reliable*
- Literal meaning: *rely* (from Old French *relier* "to bind") + *-able*
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Applied to persons, sources, instruments, and processes to indicate that they can be depended upon to perform consistently or to provide accurate information. Common in evaluative contexts concerning testimony, evidence, and character. Productive derivation: *reliability*, *reliably*, *unreliable*.
- **[CORE]**

---

**11. credible**
- Form: *credible*
- Literal meaning: Latin *credibilis* "worthy of belief" (*credere* "to believe")
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Applied to persons, testimony, accounts, and threats to indicate that they are worthy of belief or capable of being believed. Frequently used in legal contexts to evaluate witness testimony. Productive derivation: *credibility*, *credibly*, *incredible*, *incredibility*.
- **[CORE]**

---

**12. legitimate**
- Form: *legitimate*
- Literal meaning: Medieval Latin *legitimatus*, past participle of *legitimare* "to make lawful" (*lex/legis* "law")
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Applied to authority, claims, procedures, and children (historically) to indicate conformity with law, rule, or established standards. Extended to evaluations of whether something is a genuine or proper instance of a category. Productive derivation: *legitimacy*, *legitimately*, *illegitimate*, *legitimize*.
- **[CORE]**

---

**13. sound**
- Form: *sound* (adjective)
- Literal meaning: Old English *gesund* "healthy, whole"
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Applied to arguments, reasoning, and judgments to indicate both validity and truth of premises. Also applied to persons, structures, and practices to indicate reliability and freedom from defect. Productive derivation: *soundness*, *soundly*, *unsound*.
- **[CORE]**

---

**14. sincere**
- Form: *sincere*
- Literal meaning: Latin *sincerus* "whole, pure, genuine"; etymology disputed (possibly *semel* "once" + *cera* "wax," or from *sine* "without" + *cera*)
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Applied to persons, statements, and expressions to indicate that they reflect genuine inner states or intentions, without pretense or deception. Common in moral and interpersonal evaluative contexts. Productive derivation: *sincerely*, *sincerity*, *insincerity*, *insincere*.
- **[CORE]**

---

**15. honest**
- Form: *honest*
- Literal meaning: Old French *honeste*, from Latin *honestus* "honorable" (*honor*)
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Applied to persons, statements, and dealings to indicate truthfulness, absence of deception, and integrity. Used in both moral evaluation of character and assessment of specific acts or communications. Productive derivation: *honestly*, *honesty*, *dishonest*, *dishonesty*.
- **[CORE]**

---

**16. spurious**
- Form: *spurious*
- Literal meaning: Latin *spurius* "illegitimate, false"
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Applied to documents, arguments, claims, and attributions to indicate that they are not what they purport to be, or that they rest on false or illegitimate grounds. More formal and domain-specific than *fake* or *false*. Productive derivation: *spuriously*, *spuriousness*.
- **[MARGINAL]**

---

**17. counterfeit**
- Form: *counterfeit*
- Literal meaning: Old French *contrefait*, past participle of *contrefaire* "to imitate" (*contre-* "against, in opposition" + *faire* "to make")
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Applied primarily to currency, documents, and goods made in imitation of genuine articles with intent to deceive. Used as both adjective and noun; also used as a verb. Productive derivation: *counterfeiter*, *counterfeiting*.
- **[CORE]**

---

**18. veridical**
- Form: *veridical*
- Literal meaning: Latin *veridicus* "truth-telling" (*verus* "true" + *dicere* "to say")
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Applied in philosophical and psychological contexts to perceptions, dreams, or experiences that correspond to or accurately represent reality. Contrasts with illusory or hallucinatory experiences. Productive derivation: *veridicality*, *veridically*.
- **[MARGINAL]**

---

**19. faithful**
- Form: *faithful*
- Literal meaning: *faith* (from Old French *feid*, Latin *fides* "trust") + *-ful*
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Applied to reproductions, translations, accounts, and persons to indicate close correspondence to an original or to a commitment. In evaluative contexts, indicates that a representation does not distort or deviate from its source. Productive derivation: *faithfully*, *faithfulness*, *unfaithful*.
- **[CORE]**

---

**20. verifiable**
- Form: *verifiable*
- Literal meaning: *verify* (from Medieval Latin *verificare*, *verus* "true" + *facere* "to make") + *-able*
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Applied to claims, statements, and hypotheses to indicate that they are capable of being checked against evidence or established by investigation. Common in scientific, journalistic, and legal contexts. Productive derivation: *verifiability*, *verify*, *verification*, *unverifiable*.
- **[CORE]**

---

**21. bona fide**
- Form: *bona fide*
- Literal meaning: Latin "in good faith" (*bona* "good" + *fide*, ablative of *fides* "faith")
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Used as an adjective or adverb to indicate that something is genuine, without fraud or deception, or that an action is undertaken sincerely. Common in legal and administrative contexts ("bona fide offer," "bona fide resident"). Functions as a fixed expression; also used attributively.
- **[CORE]**

---

**22. apocryphal**
- Form: *apocryphal*
- Literal meaning: Medieval Latin *apocryphus*, from Greek *apokryphos* "hidden, obscure" (*apo-* "away" + *kryptein* "to hide")
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Applied to stories, sayings, and attributions that are widely circulated but of doubtful authenticity or authorship. Originally referred to texts excluded from the biblical canon. Productive derivation: *apocryphally*; the noun *apocrypha* is also in use.
- **[MARGINAL]**

---

**23. exact**
- Form: *exact*
- Literal meaning: Latin *exactus*, past participle of *exigere* "to drive out, demand, measure" (*ex-* + *agere* "to drive")
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Applied to measurements, reproductions, descriptions, and statements to indicate complete correspondence with a standard or original, without approximation or error. Productive derivation: *exactly*, *exactness*, *exactitude*, *inexact*.
- **[CORE]**

---

**24. plausible**
- Form: *plausible*
- Literal meaning: Latin *plausibilis* "deserving applause" (*plaudere* "to applaud")
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Applied to claims, explanations, and accounts to indicate that they appear reasonable or probable, though not necessarily verified. Occupies a weaker epistemic position than *credible* or *verified*. Productive derivation: *plausibly*, *plausibility*, *implausible*.
- **[CORE]**

---

**25. fraudulent**
- Form: *fraudulent*
- Literal meaning: Latin *fraudulentus* (*fraus/fraudis* "deceit, fraud")
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Applied to acts, documents, claims, and representations made with intent to deceive for gain. Common in legal contexts. Productive derivation: *fraudulently*, *fraud*, *fraudulence*.
- **[CORE]**

---

**26. specious**
- Form: *specious*
- Literal meaning: Latin *speciosus* "good-looking, plausible" (*species* "appearance")
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Applied to arguments, claims, and reasoning that appear sound or valid on the surface but are actually flawed or misleading. Carries a negative evaluative force distinguishing it from *plausible*. Productive derivation: *speciously*, *speciousness*.
- **[MARGINAL]**

---

**27. rightful**
- Form: *rightful*
- Literal meaning: *right* (Old English *riht* "just, proper") + *-ful*
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Applied to claims, ownership, authority, and persons to indicate that they are legitimate or in accordance with what is just or legally proper ("rightful owner," "rightful heir"). Productive derivation: *rightfully*, *rightfulness*.
- **[CORE]**

---

**28. veracity**
- Form: *veracity*
- Literal meaning: Medieval Latin *veracitas* (*verax* "truthful," from *verus* "true")
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Refers to the habitual observance of truth in speech or the accuracy of a statement or account. Used in legal contexts to evaluate the reliability of witnesses and in general discourse to assess the truthfulness of persons or reports. Productive derivation: *veracious*, *veraciously*.
- **[CORE]**

---

**29. fictitious**
- Form: *fictitious*
- Literal meaning: Latin *fictitius* "artificial, feigned" (*fingere* "to shape, feign")
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Applied to names, identities, accounts, and entities to indicate that they are invented or fabricated rather than real. Used in legal contexts (fictitious name) and in general evaluative contexts. Productive derivation: *fictitiously*, *fictitiousness*; related to *fiction*, *fictive*.
- **[CORE]**

---

**30. ostensible**
- Form: *ostensible*
- Literal meaning: Medieval Latin *ostensibilis* (*ostendere* "to show, display")
- Typical English translations: *(source language)*
- Usage: Applied to stated purposes, reasons, and identities to indicate that they are what is presented or claimed on the surface, with an implication that the reality may differ. Frequently used in contexts where the apparent and actual diverge. Productive derivation: *ostensibly*, *ostensibility*.
- **[CORE]**

# CLCA Protocol v3.4 — P4a_CONSTRUCTIONS
# Language: English (en)

## 1. ATTRIBUTION

### Pattern
**Subject + BE + truth-domain adjective**

The basic attributive construction uses the copula "be" to predicate truth-domain properties of subjects. The adjective follows the copula directly without additional marking.

### Examples
- That statement is true.
- That statement is true.
- "The claim corresponds to reality."

- His testimony was false.
- His testimony was false.
- "His account did not correspond to reality."

### Notes
This is the unmarked construction for attributing truth-domain properties. The copula "be" is conjugated for tense, aspect, and agreement. No additional morphology is required on the adjective.

---

## 2. NEGATIVE ATTRIBUTION

### Pattern
**Subject + BE + NEG + truth-domain adjective**
**Subject + BE + negative-prefixed adjective**

Negation is expressed either through the negative particle "not" following the copula, or through negative prefixes (un-, in-, dis-) on the adjective itself.

### Examples
- The document is not authentic.
- The document is not authentic.
- "The document lacks authenticity."

- The claim is untrue.
- The claim is untrue.
- "The claim is false."

### Notes
English allows both analytic negation (not + positive adjective) and synthetic negation (negative prefix + root). The choice often depends on lexical availability and register. Some adjectives strongly prefer one pattern (e.g., "not true" vs. "untrue" are both common, but "not genuine" is more common than "ingenuine").

---

## 3. ADNOMINAL MODIFICATION

### Pattern
**Truth-domain adjective + Noun**

Truth-domain adjectives directly precede and modify nouns without additional marking. The adjective is invariant regardless of the noun's number or other features.

### Examples
- a true statement
- a true statement
- "a statement that corresponds to reality"

- false testimony
- false testimony
- "testimony that does not correspond to reality"

### Notes
English uses strict prenominal position for attributive adjectives. No agreement marking exists. Multiple adjectives can stack ("a completely false statement"). Some truth-domain terms appear primarily or exclusively in this construction (e.g., "spurious reasoning," "fallacious argument").

---

## 4. BECOME / INCHOATIVE

### Pattern
**Subject + become/turn/prove + truth-domain adjective**
**Subject + come true** (specialized)

Change-of-state is expressed through verbs like "become," "turn," "prove," or "turn out to be" followed by the adjective. The specialized construction "come true" expresses realization of predictions or wishes.

### Examples
- The rumor became true.
- The rumor became true.
- "The rumor was realized as fact."

- Her prediction came true.
- Her prediction came true.
- "Her prediction was realized in reality."

### Notes
"Become" is the general inchoative verb. "Come true" is a specialized construction limited to predictions, wishes, and dreams. "Turn out to be" emphasizes discovery of a pre-existing state. "Prove" emphasizes evidential basis for the change in epistemic status.

---

## 5. CAUSATIVE

### Pattern
**Agent + make + Object + truth-domain adjective**
**Agent + render + Object + truth-domain adjective** (formal)

Causative constructions use "make" (common) or "render" (formal) to express causing something to have a truth-domain property. Some verbs incorporate causative meaning (e.g., "falsify," "validate," "authenticate").

### Examples
- The evidence made his claim true.
- The evidence made his claim true.
- "The evidence caused his claim to be true."

- The forgery rendered the document invalid.
- The forgery rendered the document invalid.
- "The forgery caused the document to become invalid."

### Notes
English has both analytic causatives (make/render + adjective) and lexical causatives (falsify, validate, legitimize). The lexical causatives are often derived from the adjectives themselves through -ify, -ize, or -ate suffixation. "Make" is neutral register; "render" is formal.

---

## 6. ABSTRACT NOMINALIZATION

### Pattern
**truth-domain adjective + -ness/-ity/-cy/-th**
**Bare noun forms** (truth, error, lie)

Abstract nominalizations are formed through suffixation (-ness, -ity, -cy, -th) or exist as independent lexical items. These denote the quality or state of having the truth-domain property.

### Examples
- the truthfulness of his statement
- the truthfulness of his statement
- "the quality of his statement being truthful"

- the authenticity of the painting
- the authenticity of the painting
- "the quality of the painting being authentic"

### Notes
Multiple suffixes compete: -ness (rightness, wrongness), -ity (validity, authenticity), -cy (accuracy, legitimacy), -th (truth). Choice is lexically determined. Some roots have multiple nominalizations with different meanings (e.g., "truth" vs. "truthfulness"). Latin-derived adjectives typically take -ity; Germanic roots take -ness or -th.

---

## 7. AGENTIVE NOMINALIZATION

### Pattern
**truth-domain root + -er/-or**
**Compound forms** (truth-teller, wrongdoer)

Agent nouns denoting persons characterized by truth-domain properties are formed through -er/-or suffixation or compounding. These refer to habitual actors rather than single actions.

### Examples
- a liar (from "lie")
- a liar
- "one who habitually tells falsehoods"

- a truth-teller
- a truth-teller
- "one who habitually tells the truth"

### Notes
English has limited productive agentive nominalization in this domain. "Liar" is the most common. Compounds like "truth-teller" and "wrongdoer" are productive but less frequent. Some agents are expressed through -er/-or on verbs rather than adjectives (e.g., "falsifier" from "falsify"). Many concepts lack dedicated agent nouns (no common single word for "one who is always right").

---

## 8. DEGREE MODIFICATION

### Pattern
**Degree adverb + truth-domain adjective**
**Comparative/superlative forms**

Truth-domain adjectives can be modified by degree adverbs (very, completely, partially, somewhat) and can take comparative (-er, more) and superlative (-est, most) forms, though some resist gradation.

### Examples
- completely true
- completely true
- "true to the fullest degree"

- more accurate than the previous version
- more accurate than the previous version
- "having greater accuracy compared to the previous version"

### Notes
Some truth-domain adjectives resist gradation (e.g., "true" and "false" are often treated as absolute, though "truer" and "more true" are attested). Others readily accept degree modification ("very accurate," "quite genuine," "somewhat reliable"). Comparative and superlative forms use analytic constructions (more/most) for polysyllabic adjectives and synthetic forms (-er/-est) for monosyllabic ones.

---

## 9. EVIDENTIAL/EPISTEMIC QUALIFICATION

### Pattern
**Epistemic verb/adverb + truth-domain predication**
**Modal auxiliary + BE + truth-domain adjective**

Truth-domain predications can be qualified by epistemic verbs (seem, appear, prove), adverbs (apparently, evidently, probably), or modal auxiliaries (may, might, must) to indicate the speaker's degree of certainty or evidential basis.

### Examples
- The claim seems true.
- The claim seems true.
- "Based on available evidence, the claim appears to correspond to reality."

- This must be authentic.
- This must be authentic.
- "Based on strong evidence, I conclude this is authentic."

### Notes
English uses multiple strategies for epistemic qualification: raising verbs (seem, appear), evidential adverbs (apparently, evidently), and modal auxiliaries (may, might, must, should). These can combine with any truth-domain adjective. The construction allows speakers to hedge or strengthen their commitment to truth-domain evaluations.

---

## 10. RESULTATIVE CONSTRUCTIONS

### Pattern
**Verb + Object + truth-domain adjective**
**Verb + Object + as + truth-domain adjective**

Resultative constructions express causing or finding something to be in a truth-domain state. The adjective functions as a complement describing the result of the action.

### Examples
- They proved the theory correct.
- They proved the theory correct.
- "They demonstrated that the theory was correct."

- I consider his account reliable.
- I consider his account reliable.
- "I judge his account to be reliable."

### Notes
Verbs like "prove," "find," "consider," "judge," "deem," and "declare" take truth-domain adjectives as complements. Some verbs (prove, find) emphasize discovery; others (consider, judge, deem) emphasize evaluation. The construction "verb + object + as + adjective" is an alternative with some verbs ("regard as authentic," "classify as spurious").

---

## 11. COMPARATIVE CONSTRUCTIONS

### Pattern
**X + BE + more/less + adjective + than + Y**
**X + BE + as + adjective + as + Y**
**X + BE + adjective-er + than + Y**

Comparison between entities with respect to truth-domain properties uses analytic (more/less, as...as) or synthetic (-er) forms depending on adjective length.

### Examples
- This account is more accurate than that one.
- This account is more accurate than that one.
- "This account has greater accuracy compared to that one."

- His testimony is as truthful as hers.
- His testimony is as truthful as hers.
- "His testimony has equal truthfulness to hers."

### Notes
Monosyllabic adjectives typically use synthetic comparison (truer, falser—though these are rare), while polysyllabic adjectives use analytic comparison (more accurate, less reliable). Equality comparisons use "as...as" construction. Some truth-domain adjectives resist comparison due to their absolute nature (e.g., "true" and "false" are often treated as binary).

---

## 12. INTERROGATIVE CONSTRUCTIONS

### Pattern
**BE + Subject + truth-domain adjective?**
**WH-word + BE + Subject + truth-domain adjective?**

Questions about truth-domain properties use subject-auxiliary inversion for yes/no questions and wh-words (how, why) for information questions.

### Examples
- Is this statement true?
- Is this statement true?
- "Does this statement correspond to reality?"

- How accurate is this measurement?
- How accurate is this measurement?
- "To what degree does this measurement correspond to the actual value?"

### Notes
Yes/no questions invert subject and auxiliary ("Is it true?" not *"It is true?"). Information questions use wh-words in initial position with inversion ("How true is it?" "Why is it false?"). Tag questions are also common ("It's true, isn't it?"). Degree questions with "how" are particularly common with gradable truth-domain adjectives.

---

## 13. CLEFT AND FOCUS CONSTRUCTIONS

### Pattern
**It + BE + truth-domain adjective + that-clause**
**What + BE + truth-domain adjective + BE + clause**

Cleft constructions focus on the truth-domain evaluation itself, separating it from the proposition being evaluated.

### Examples
- It is true that he left early.
- It is true that he left early.
- "The proposition 'he left early' corresponds to reality."

- What is true is that we made mistakes.
- What is true is that we made mistakes.
- "The true proposition is that we made mistakes."

### Notes
It-clefts ("It is true/false/certain that...") are extremely common for focusing on truth-domain evaluations of propositions. Wh-clefts ("What is true/false is...") are less common but emphasize the content of the true/false proposition. These constructions allow separation of the evaluation from the evaluated content.

---

## 14. PHRASAL VERB CONSTRUCTIONS

### Pattern
**Verb particle combinations with truth-domain meanings**

English has numerous phrasal verbs that express truth-domain concepts through verb + particle combinations. These are often idiomatic and highly productive.

### Examples
- The story turned out to be false.
- The story turned out to be false.
- "The story was discovered/proved to be false."

- His alibi didn't hold up.
- His alibi didn't hold up.
- "His alibi was not validated/proved false."

### Notes
Phrasal verbs like "turn out," "hold up," "pan out," "check out," "bear out," and "come true" express various truth-domain concepts. These are often more idiomatic and colloquial than single-word equivalents. The particle contributes aspectual or directional meaning that modifies the basic verb sense.

---

## 15. NOMINAL PREDICATION

### Pattern
**Subject + BE + truth-domain noun**
**Subject + BE + a/the + truth-domain noun**

Truth-domain properties can be predicated using nouns rather than adjectives, often with articles or determiners.

### Examples
- That's the truth.
- That's the truth.
- "That corresponds to reality."

- His statement is a lie.
- His statement is a lie.
- "His statement is a falsehood."

### Notes
Some truth-domain concepts are more naturally expressed as nouns than adjectives ("a lie" rather than "lying," "the truth" rather than "true"). The construction requires appropriate articles or determiners. Mass nouns like "truth" can appear without articles in some contexts ("Truth is important"). Count nouns like "lie" require articles ("a lie," "the lie").

---

## 16. ADVERBIAL MODIFICATION OF SPEECH ACTS

### Pattern
**Truth-domain adverb + clause/sentence**
**Subject + truth-domain adverb + verb**

Truth-domain adverbs modify entire clauses or speech acts, indicating the manner or truthfulness of the statement or the speaker's stance.

### Examples
- Honestly, I don't know.
- Honestly, I don't know.
- "Speaking honestly, I don't know."

- She answered truthfully.
- She answered truthfully.
- "She answered in a truthful manner."

### Notes
Sentence-initial adverbs like "honestly," "truly," "frankly" function as discourse markers indicating the speaker's stance. Manner adverbs like "truthfully," "accurately," "correctly" modify the verb directly. Some adverbs have both functions depending on position ("Frankly, I disagree" vs. "She spoke frankly").

## Global Convergence Report

### 1. Scope and Data

This report synthesizes results from eight languages: **Georgian (ka), Yoruba (yo), Finnish (fi), Basque (eu), Tamil (ta), Indonesian (id), Turkish (tr), and Korean (ko)**.

The analysis draws on the full G-phase record, including:
- **F1** vocabulary inventories,
- **F2a** derivational patterns,
- **F2b** constructional patterns,
- **F3** semantic groupings,
- **F4** construction-type comparisons,
- **F5** opposition/contrast structures,
- **F6** naturalness judgments,
- and **G1–G8** cross-language synthesis steps.

Coverage is substantial across all eight languages, but not fully uniform. The lexical and semantic inventories are generally rich throughout, while some domains show thinner constructional evidence in particular languages. Notable unevenness includes:
- weaker explicit F4 constructional coverage for some Yoruba subdomains,
- limited support for dynamic “become/make X” patterns in several languages,
- uneven evidence for agentive person terms such as “truth-teller,”
- and variable depth in formal validity, credibility, and appearance-vs-reality domains.

Accordingly, the report distinguishes carefully between **strongly supported**, **moderately supported**, and **weak or incomplete** findings.

---

### 2. Core Cross-Language Findings

#### 2.1 Organization of the semantic field

Across the sample, truth-related meaning is **not organized as a single undifferentiated domain**. Instead, most languages partition the field into several recurrent subdomains, though the boundaries differ by language. The most recurrent distinctions are:

- **factual truth of statements/claims**
- **falsehood / lying / non-truth**
- **genuineness / authenticity**
- **fake / forged / counterfeit**
- **correctness / rightness / properness**
- **error / wrongness**
- **precision / exactness / accuracy**
- **reliability / trustworthiness**
- **credibility / plausibility** in at least some languages
- **formal validity / legal-procedural standing**
- **justification / legitimacy / fairness**
- **essence / underlying nature**
- **appearance vs reality**
- **resemblance / similarity**, often adjacent to authenticity or correspondence

This partitioning is especially fine-grained in **Finnish, Indonesian, Turkish, Georgian, and Korean**. By contrast, **Yoruba** shows broader merger in the central *tọ́ / òtítọ́* field, where truth, correctness, rightness, and straightness overlap more strongly. **Tamil** shows a different kind of internal complexity, with a split between everyday truth vocabulary (*உண்மை / நிஜம்*) and a large literary-philosophical *மெய்* subsystem.

#### 2.2 Shared lexical patterns

Several lexical contrasts recur strongly across languages:

- **truth vs falsehood**
- **genuine vs fake**
- **correct vs incorrect**
- **valid vs invalid**
- **reliable vs unreliable**
- **essence vs appearance/surface**, though this last contrast is often asymmetrical

The strongest and most recurrent lexical oppositions include:
- Georgian **ნამდვილი / ყალბი**
- Yoruba **òtítọ́ / irọ́**
- Finnish **tosi / epätosi**, **aito / epäaito**
- Basque **egia / gezur**, **zuzen / oker**
- Tamil **உண்மை / பொய்**, **சரி / தவறு**, **அசல் / போலி**
- Indonesian **asli / palsu**, **sah / tidak sah**, **valid / tidak valid**
- Turkish **doğru / yanlış**, **haklı / haksız**, **geçerli / geçersiz**
- Korean **진짜 / 가짜**, **진실 / 거짓**, **유효하다 / 무효**

At the same time, overlap is common. For example:
- Yoruba central truth vocabulary merges truth and correctness.
- Turkish **doğru** spans true/correct/right.
- Georgian **მართალი** spans true/right/just.
- Tamil **சரி** spans correct, proper, acceptable, and conversational “okay.”
- Korean **옳다** can cover both correctness and moral rightness.

#### 2.3 Shared derivational patterns

A strong cross-language result is the recurrence of **abstract quality formation** from truth-related bases. Most languages derive nouns meaning truth, correctness, authenticity, reliability, validity, or related qualities from adjectival or lexical stems.

This is especially clear in:
- Georgian: **სიმართლე, სისწორე, სიზუსტე, ნამდვილობა**
- Finnish: **totuus, aitous, luotettavuus, uskottavuus**
- Basque: **egiazkotasun, zuzentasun**
- Tamil: **உண்மைத்தன்மை, மெய்மை, நம்பகத்தன்மை**
- Indonesian: **kebenaran, keaslian, keabsahan, keakuratan**
- Turkish: **doğruluk, geçerlilik, dürüstlük, özgünlük**
- Korean: **정확성, 정당성, 타당성, 유효성, 진실성**

Also recurrent are:
- **adverb formation** (“truly,” “really,” “accurately,” “genuinely”),
- **negative derivation** in some languages (especially Finnish and Turkish),
- **verb formation** for verifying, correcting, validating, or falsifying,
- and **compound formation** for legal, technical, philosophical, and authenticity-related vocabulary.

A notable asymmetry is that derivational richness is often concentrated in only part of the field. Some roots generate large families, while other equally central meanings remain mostly lexical.

#### 2.4 Shared constructional patterns

The most robust constructional findings are:

1. **simple attribution**
   - e.g. “X is true/correct/valid/real”

2. **negative attribution**
   - e.g. “X is not true,” “X is invalid,” “X is fake”

3. **adnominal modification**
   - e.g. “true story,” “accurate information,” “fake document,” “reliable source”

4. **truth/fact as object of a verb**
   - e.g. “tell the truth,” “hide the truth,” “reveal the truth,” “seek the truth,” “check the facts”

5. **verification/checking/confirmation**
   - e.g. “verify the facts,” “check authenticity,” “confirm information”

6. **revelation/exposure**
   - e.g. “the truth came out,” “the real nature was revealed,” “it turned out to be false”

These are strongly recurrent across the sample.

A particularly important shared result is that many languages prefer to encode truth dynamically through **revelation, verification, confirmation, or exposure**, rather than through a simple general verb meaning “become true.” This pattern is especially clear in **Georgian, Yoruba, Basque, Tamil, Turkish, and Korean**, and also visible in **Finnish** and **Indonesian**.

#### 2.5 Shared oppositional structure

Oppositions are robustly represented in all languages, but not always symmetrically. The strongest recurrent contrast types are:

- **truth vs falsehood**
- **genuine vs fake**
- **correct vs incorrect**
- **valid vs invalid**
- **right/justified vs wrong/unjustified**

In addition, several languages show:
- **scalar contrasts** rather than strict binary oppositions,
- **register contrasts** (e.g. everyday vs elevated truth terms),
- and **domain contrasts** such as reliability vs credibility, or truth vs validity.

#### 2.6 Major asymmetries

Several asymmetries recur across the sample:

- **State attribution is much more broadly available than dynamic truth-causation.**
- **Verification and revelation are much more robust than general “make true” constructions.**
- **Negative-side dynamics** (falsify, forge, invalidate, expose as false) are often better lexicalized than positive-side dynamics (make true, make genuine, make reliable).
- **Authenticity** is often something that is **checked, certified, doubted, or exposed**, not something that straightforwardly “becomes genuine.”
- **Reliability** is often expressed as a state, assessment, or gain/loss of trust, rather than through simple productive “become reliable” or “make reliable” constructions.
- **Compact lexicalized “liar” terms** are more common than equally central compact “truth-teller” terms; the positive side is often descriptive or periphrastic.

### 3. Global Constraint Summary

#### 3.1 Informal constraint surface

The global constraint matrix suggests a relatively clear overall surface:

- **Simple attribution**, **negative attribution**, **adnominal modification**, **verification/checking**, and **abstract nominalization** are broadly available across most major truth-related dimensions.
- **Truth/fact as object of a verb** is also broadly available, especially for factual truth and related domains.
- **Revelation/exposure** is strongly available for factual truth, falsity, authenticity failure, and underlying reality.
- By contrast, **general inchoative and causative patterns are much more restricted**, especially for factual truth, authenticity, reliability, and essence.

#### 3.2 Strongly supported constraints

The strongest supported constraints are:

1. **Broad blocking or strong dispreference of a general “make X true” construction**
   - Languages overwhelmingly prefer verify/confirm/reveal/establish rather than “make true.”

2. **Restricted or variable status of general “become true” constructions**
   - Some languages allow them, but many prefer revelation or confirmation instead.

3. **Broad resistance of authenticity to productive positive inchoative/causative patterns**
   - “Become genuine” and “make genuine” are often weak, absent, or replaced by checking/certification.

4. **Broad resistance of reliability to direct causative treatment**
   - Reliability is often gained, secured, or assessed, not directly “made.”

5. **Strong availability of verification/checking constructions**
   - Especially for truth, authenticity, correctness, conformity, and validity.

6. **Strong availability of revelation/exposure constructions**
   - Especially for truth, falsity, and hidden reality.

7. **Strong availability of abstract quality nouns**
   - Across truth, falsity, authenticity, correctness, validity, reliability, and essence.

#### 3.3 Moderately supported patterns

Moderately supported patterns include:

- **precision/accuracy as a distinct dimension**
  - strong in some languages, less clearly separated in others

- **credibility/plausibility as distinct from reliability**
  - clear in some languages, merged or less sharply lexicalized in others

- **appearance-vs-reality as a major constructional zone**
  - clearly important, but not equally explicit in all languages

- **faithfulness to original/source as a distinct dimension**
  - strong in some languages, merged with authenticity or resemblance in others

- **essence as a distinct semantic domain**
  - strongly lexicalized in many languages, but dynamic behavior remains uneven

#### 3.4 Weak or uncertain areas

The weakest or most uncertain areas are:

- a broad general **“make true”** construction
- a broad general **“become true”** construction across all languages
- productive **“become genuine/authentic”**
- productive **“make genuine/authentic”**
- productive **“make reliable”**
- a symmetrical lexicalized **truth-teller** counterpart to “liar”
- some dynamic patterns for essence and resemblance
- some appearance-vs-reality pairings outside the best-supported languages

#### 3.5 Variability and context dependence

Several constraints are clearly **variable rather than absolute**:
- “become true” is stronger in **Finnish, Indonesian, Turkish**, weaker in **Georgian, Yoruba, Basque, Korean**
- validity change is more natural than truth-state change in several languages
- reliability and credibility are sharply distinguished in some languages but not others
- truth, correctness, and rightness are merged in some languages and separated in others

Accordingly, the matrix should be read as a **constraint surface with graded support**, not as a set of categorical universals.

### 4. Candidate Universals and Outliers

#### 4.1 Strong cross-language tendencies

The strongest candidate tendencies are:

- broad availability of **simple attribution**
- broad availability of **negative attribution**
- strong use of **adnominal modification**
- strong use of **truth/fact as object of a verb**
- strong lexicalization of **verification/checking**
- strong preference for **revelation/exposure** in truth dynamics
- broad lexical distinction between **truth** and **falsehood**
- broad lexical distinction between **genuine** and **fake**
- broad distinction between **truth** and **formal validity**
- broad distinction between **truth** and **reliability**
- broad lexicalization of **essence / underlying nature**
- broad asymmetry in which **negative-side dynamics** are often stronger than positive-side ones

These are the best-supported cross-language tendencies in the current dataset.

#### 4.2 Tentative or weaker candidates

More tentative candidates include:

- a broad tendency against **general “become true”**
- a broad tendency against **productive “become genuine”**
- a broad tendency against **productive “make reliable”**
- a robust cross-language distinction between **credibility** and **reliability**
- a robust cross-language distinction between **precision** and **correctness**
- a broad asymmetry favoring lexicalized **liar** over lexicalized **truth-teller**

These are plausible, but the evidence is less even or less complete.

#### 4.3 Major outliers and structural divergences

Major outliers include:

- **Yoruba**
  - broad merger of truth, correctness, rightness, and straightness in the central lexical family

- **Tamil**
  - strong split between everyday truth vocabulary and a large literary-philosophical *மெய்* subsystem

- **Finnish**
  - unusually explicit lexicalized resultative patterns for truth/falsity

- **Korean**
  - especially strong preference for revelation, disclosure, checking, and institutional status change

- **Indonesian**
  - unusually rich scalar negative system (**tidak / kurang / belum**) across many evaluative domains

- **Georgian**
  - especially clear register split between everyday and elevated truth vocabulary

These divergences appear to be structural rather than merely accidental gaps in the data, though further testing would be useful.

---

### 5. Limitations and Future Work

#### 5.1 Data limitations

The current study is broad but not exhaustive.

- Only **eight languages** were included.
- Coverage is richer for some domains than others.
- Lexical and semantic inventories are generally strong, but some constructional domains are thinner.
- Dynamic constructions are especially unevenly represented.
- Some dimensions, such as **credibility**, **appearance-vs-reality**, and **faithfulness to original**, are better documented in some languages than in others.
- Agentive person terms are unevenly represented.
- Some formal/legal domains are much richer in certain languages than in others.

#### 5.2 Methodological limitations

Several methodological cautions apply:

- The report is based on **elicited and curated lexical-constructional inventories**, not balanced corpus counts.
- Naturalness judgments are available, but not uniformly dense across all subdomains.
- Some cross-language dimensions were reconstructed from partially overlapping lexical fields rather than from identical prompts.
- Some categories in the global matrix necessarily compress distinctions that are lexically separate in some languages and merged in others.
- Absence of evidence in a given language should not automatically be interpreted as categorical impossibility.

#### 5.3 Recommended next steps

Future work should include:

1. **Additional languages**
   - especially from underrepresented families and typological profiles

2. **More targeted prompts for dynamic constructions**
   - especially:
     - “become true”
     - “make true”
     - “become genuine”
     - “make genuine”
     - “become reliable”
     - “make reliable”

3. **More systematic testing of appearance-vs-reality constructions**
   - including seeming, looking, turning out, exposure, and hidden reality

4. **Deeper comparison of reliability vs credibility**
   - to determine whether this is a robust cross-language distinction or a language-specific lexical split

5. **More focused work on precision vs correctness**
   - especially in languages where the distinction is only partly lexicalized

6. **Closer study of formal validity**
   - to determine whether it should be treated as a distinct cross-language semantic domain

7. **Expanded investigation of agentive asymmetries**
   - especially lexicalized “liar” vs descriptive “truth-teller” patterns

8. **Corpus-based follow-up**
   - to test whether the elicited and summarized patterns match actual frequency and register distribution

---

## Overall conclusion

The data support a robust cross-language conclusion that truth-related meaning is typically organized into **multiple recurrent but non-identical subdomains**, rather than a single undifferentiated semantic field. The strongest shared patterns concern:
- **state attribution**,
- **negative attribution**,
- **noun modification**,
- **truth/fact as object of verbs**,
- **verification/checking**,
- **revelation/exposure**,
- and **abstract nominalization**.

The strongest asymmetry is that many languages are much better equipped to express that truth is **revealed, checked, confirmed, denied, concealed, or institutionally recognized** than to express that something simply **becomes true** or is **made true**. This asymmetry appears repeatedly across unrelated languages and is one of the clearest results of the comparison.

# G5 — Cross-Language Construction Comparison

---

## 1. Identification of Main Constructional Functions

Working from the F4 tables across all eight languages, the following constructional functions recur with sufficient frequency to warrant cross-language comparison. These are identified inductively from the data; not every function appears in every language, and the labels are descriptive rather than theoretical.

**Functions identified:**
1. Predicating truth/correctness of a subject (attribution)
2. Negating truth/correctness of a subject (negative attribution)
3. Modifying a noun with a truth-domain adjective (adnominal modification)
4. Expressing change of state toward truth or correctness (inchoative)
5. Expressing change of state away from truth or correctness (inchoative, negative direction)
6. Expressing causation of a truth state (causative)
7. Expressing causation of a false or deceptive state (causative, negative direction)
8. Deriving abstract nouns from truth-domain bases (nominalization)
9. Deriving agent nouns from truth-domain bases (agentive nominalization)
10. Marking epistemic stance toward a proposition (evidential/epistemic marking)
11. Intensifying or emphasizing truth claims (degree/emphasis modification)
12. Expressing explicit contrast or correction (contrastive/corrective)
13. Expressing truth-telling and lying as speech acts (metalinguistic truth predicates)
14. Expressing appearance or seeming without commitment to truth (appearance/seeming)
15. Expressing verification, confirmation, or proof (verification/confirmation)
16. Expressing doubt or questioning of truth (doubt/questioning)
17. Conceding a point while introducing a contrast (concessive)
18. Forming questions about truth or correctness (interrogative)
19. Forming conditional statements involving truth (conditional)
20. Expressing legitimacy, validity, or justification (legitimacy/validity)

## 2. Comparison Table

| Constructional Function | Common Realization Patterns | Languages | Notes on Variation, Restrictions, or Gaps |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Predicating truth/correctness of a subject (attribution)** | Copula + truth-domain predicate nominal or adjective. Zero copula in present tense is common. | ka, yo, fi, eu, ta, id, tr, ko | **Widely attested.** ka: *ეს მართალია* (incorporated copula -ა on adjective) or *ეს არის მართალი* (separate copula); yo: *ọ̀rọ̀ náà tọ́* (subject + stative verb, no copula needed); fi: *se on totta* (copula + partitive); eu: *hau egia da* (copula *da* + predicate nominal); ta: *atu uṇmai* (zero copula, default for 3rd person present) or *atu uṇmai irukku* (explicit copula for emphasis); id: *cerita itu benar* (zero copula, default) or *adalah* in formal writing; tr: *bu doğru* (zero copula, default) or *bu doğrudur* (formal); ko: *이것은 사실이다* (copula *이다*). **Key variation:** zero copula is the unmarked present-tense pattern in yo, ta, id, tr, ko; ka uses an incorporated copula suffix on the adjective (-ა); fi requires an overt copula. Adjectival predicates (yo *tọ́*, fi *tosi*, tr *doğru*) differ from nominal predicates (yo *jẹ́ + òtítọ́*, fi *on totta*, ko *사실이다*) in some languages. |
| **2. Negating truth/correctness of a subject (negative attribution)** | Negative particle + copula + predicate, or negative particle directly on predicate verb. | ka, yo, fi, eu, ta, id, tr, ko | **Widely attested.** ka: *ეს არ არის მართალი* (particle *არ* before copula, obligatory position); yo: *ọ̀rọ̀ náà kò tọ́* (preverbal negator *kò*); fi: *se ei ole totta* (negative auxiliary *ei* + copula); eu: *hau ez da egia* (particle *ez* before copula); ta: *atu uṇmai illai* (negative copula *illai*); id: *itu tidak benar* (preverbal negator *tidak*); tr: *bu doğru değil* (postpredicate negator *değil*); ko: *이것은 사실이 아니다* (copula negation *아니다*) or *정확하지 않다* (-지 않다 for adjectival predicates). **Key variation:** Turkish is the only language where the negator follows the predicate (*değil*); all others place the negator before the copula or predicate verb. Korean distinguishes nominal predicate negation (*아니다*) from adjectival predicate negation (*-지 않다*). Finnish uses a negative auxiliary verb (*ei*) that agrees with the subject. |
| **3. Adnominal modification (truth adjective modifying noun)** | Adjective precedes noun (most languages) or follows noun (yo). No agreement morphology in most languages; Korean requires adnominal suffix. | ka, yo, fi, eu, ta, id, tr, ko | **Widely attested.** ka: *ნამდვილი მეგობარი* (adjective invariant regardless of case/number of head noun); yo: *ọ̀rọ̀ tọ́* (modifier follows head noun, functions as headless relative clause); fi: *tosi juttu* (adjective precedes noun, no agreement); eu: *egiazko historia* (relational suffix -ko on attributive form, no article); ta: *uṇmaiyāṉa katai* (adjectival suffix -āṉa required); id: *berita palsu* (adjective follows noun, no agreement); tr: *doğru bilgi* (adjective precedes noun, no agreement); ko: *진실한 사람* (adnominal suffix -한 required on -하다 adjectives; *옳은 일* for native adjectives). **Key variation:** Yoruba is the only language where the modifier consistently follows the head noun. Korean requires an adnominal suffix (-ㄴ/-는) on the adjective. Basque uses a relational suffix (-zko/-ko) on the attributive form. Tamil requires the -āṉa suffix. Other languages use bare adjectives before the noun. |
| **4. Inchoative: change of state toward truth/correctness** | Inchoative verb or auxiliary + truth-domain predicate. Specialized revelation/discovery verbs are common alternatives. | ka, fi, eu, ta, id, tr, ko; yo (marginal) | **Widely attested, with one notable restriction.** ka: *მართალი გახდა* (auxiliary *გახდა* "became"); fi: *toteutua* (dedicated inchoative verb "come true"), *totuus paljastuu* ("truth is revealed"); eu: *albistea egiaztatu da* (intransitive perfect "has been verified"), *istorioa egia bihurtu da* (generic inchoative *bihurtu* "became," marked [MARGINAL]); ta: *uṇmai āku* ("truth becomes"), *poy ampalamāku* ("lie becomes exposed"); id: *menjadi benar* ("become true"), *ternyata benar* (grammaticalized evidential "it turns out to be true"); tr: *gerçekleşmek* (dedicated inchoative "come true/be realized"), *doğru çıkmak* ("turn out to be correct"); ko: *진실이 드러나다* ("truth comes to light"), *사실로 밝혀지다* ("be brought to light as fact"); yo: *ọ̀rọ̀ náà di òtítọ́* (marked [MARGINAL], reinterpreted as discovery). **Key restriction:** Yoruba treats truth as a static property; inchoative constructions are marginal and reinterpreted as discovery or revelation. Korean similarly prefers discovery verbs (*드러나다*, *밝혀지다*) over generic inchoatives for propositional truth. Finnish lacks a common expression for "becoming untrue" (gap in the negative direction). |
| **5. Inchoative: change of state away from truth/correctness** | Negative inchoative or discovery-of-falsity construction. Less productive than the positive direction across languages. | ka, fi, eu, ta, id, tr, ko; yo (absent) | **Moderately attested; consistently less productive than Function 4.** ka: *მცდარი გახდა* ("became erroneous"), *ყალბი გახდა* (marked [MARGINAL]); fi: *osoittautua vääräksi* ("turn out to be false"), *paljastua valheeksi* ("be revealed as a lie") — no simple inchoative for "become untrue"; eu: *haren esana gezurra zela frogatu zen* ("it was proven that his statement was a lie"); ta: *poy ampalamāku* ("lie becomes exposed"), *poy piṭipaṭu* ("lie is caught"); id: *ternyata palsu* ("it turns out to be fake"), *terbukti salah* ("proven wrong"); tr: *yanlış çıkmak* ("turn out to be wrong"), *yalan çıkmak* ("turn out to be a lie"); ko: *거짓말이 들통나다* ("a lie is exposed"), *거짓으로 판명되다* ("determined to be false"); yo: no attested construction. **Key pattern:** Across languages, "becoming false" is expressed primarily through discovery/revelation constructions rather than true inchoatives. Finnish explicitly lacks a simple inchoative for this direction. Yoruba has no attested construction. The asymmetry between Functions 4 and 5 is cross-linguistically consistent. |
| **6. Causative: causing a truth state** | Causative morphology or periphrastic causative + truth-domain predicate. Verification and confirmation verbs are common alternatives. | ka, fi, eu, ta, id, tr; ko (periphrastic only); yo (marginal/reinterpreted) | **Widely attested with two notable restrictions.** ka: *დაამტკიცა სიმართლე* ("proved the truth"), *გადაამოწმა* ("verified") — lexical verbs preferred over morphological causatives; fi: *todistaa oikeaksi* ("prove correct"), *todentaa* ("verify/authenticate"), *toteuttaa* ("realize/implement"); eu: *ikertzaileak datuak egiaztatu ditu* ("the researcher verified the data"), *kazetariak egia agerian jarri du* ("the journalist exposed the truth"); ta: *uṇmaiyai veḷippaṭuttu* ("reveal the truth"), *uṟutipaṭuttu* ("confirm"); id: *membenarkan* ("confirm/justify"), *mengesahkan* ("validate/legalize"); tr: *doğrulamak* ("verify/confirm"), *gerçekleştirmek* ("realize/implement"); ko: *진실을 밝히다* ("reveal the truth"), *사실을 확인하다* ("verify the facts") — periphrastic *-게 하다* for causing someone to tell truth; yo: *ó mú kí ọ̀rọ̀ náà tọ́* (marked [MARGINAL], reinterpreted as verification). **Key restrictions:** Yoruba treats causing truth states as pragmatically odd; the construction is reinterpreted as verification or enabling. Korean lacks productive morphological causatives for truth predicates; periphrastic constructions are used instead. In most languages, lexical verification verbs (prove, confirm, verify) are preferred over morphological causatives of truth adjectives. |
| **7. Causative: causing a false or deceptive state** | Causative morphology or periphrastic causative + deception-domain predicate. More productive than Function 6 across languages. | ka, yo, fi, eu, ta, id, tr, ko | **Widely attested; more productive than Function 6.** ka: *გააყალბა* ("falsified"), *მოატყუა* ("deceived/fooled"); yo: *wọ́n mú kí ó pa irọ́* ("they made him tell a lie"), *ó mú kí wọn gbàgbọ́ irọ́* ("he made them believe a lie"); fi: *vääristää totuutta* ("distort the truth"), *väärentää* ("forge/counterfeit"); eu: *norbaitek dokumentua faltsutu du* ("someone falsified the document"), *gobernuak egia ezkutatu du* ("the government hid the truth"); ta: *poy kaṭṭu* ("fabricate lies"), *ēmāṟṟu* ("deceive/cheat"); id: *memalsu* ("forge/counterfeit"), *memalsukan* ("falsify"), *membohongi* ("lie to someone"); tr: *yalanlamak* ("deny/refute"), *yanıltmak* ("mislead/deceive"), *vääristää* — not tr; *sahte çıkarmak* (implied); ko: *거짓말을 하게 하다* ("make someone lie"), *조작하다* ("fabricate"), *왜곡하다* ("distort"); yo: *wọ́n mú kí ó pa irọ́* ("they made him lie") — natural. **Key pattern:** Causing deception is consistently more morphologically productive than causing truth across languages. This parallels the agentive asymmetry (liar lexicalized; truth-teller not). |
| **8. Abstract nominalization** | Suffix or circumfix derives abstract noun from truth-domain base. Used in formal, philosophical, and analytical discourse. | ka, yo, fi, eu, ta, id, tr, ko | **Widely attested.** ka: *სიმართლე* (si-...-e), *სანდოობა* (-oba); yo: *ìgbàgbọ́* (ì- prefix), *ìbámu* (ì- prefix); fi: *totuus* (-uus), *rehellisyys* (-yys); eu: *zuzentasuna* (-tasun), *egiazkotasuna* (-tasun); ta: *uṇmaittaṉmai* (-taṉmai), *nērmai* (-mai); id: *kebenaran* (ke-...-an), *kejujuran* (ke-...-an); tr: *doğruluk* (-luk), *dürüstlük* (-lük); ko: *정확성* (-성), *신빙성* (-성). **Key variation:** The specific suffix differs typologically but the functional pattern is consistent across all eight languages. In ka, two competing patterns exist (si-...-e vs. -oba). In yo, the ì- prefix is the primary nominalizer. In eu, -tasun is highly productive and applies to both positive and negative bases. In eu, the pejorative -keria suffix creates a distinct class of nominalizations for habitual deception (gezurkeria, faltsukeria) not found in other languages. |
| **9. Agentive nominalization** | Suffix or prefix derives noun denoting a person characterized by lying or truth-telling. Asymmetric: "liar" is universally lexicalized; "truth-teller" is absent or periphrastic. | ka, yo, fi, eu, ta, id, tr, ko | **Widely attested (liar); absent or marginal (truth-teller).** ka: *მატყუარა* (ma-...-ara, "liar"); yo: *apurọ́* (a- prefix, "liar"), *ọmọ òtítọ́* (compound, "truthful person"); fi: *valehtelija* (-ja suffix, "liar"); eu: *gezurlari* (-lari suffix, "liar"); ta: *poyyan/poyyāḷ/poyyar* (person-marking suffixes, "liar"); id: *pembohong* (peN- prefix, "liar"); tr: *yalancı* (-cı suffix, "liar"); ko: *거짓말쟁이* (-쟁이 suffix, "liar"). "Truth-teller" requires periphrastic expression in all languages: yo *ọmọ òtítọ́* (child of truth), ka *მართლმოსაუბრე* ([MARGINAL]), ta *uṇmaiyai colpavar* (person who speaks truth), ko *진실을 말하는 사람* (person who speaks truth). **Key asymmetry:** This is the most consistent cross-language asymmetry in the dataset. The morphological pathway from deception root to agent noun is productive in all eight languages; the parallel pathway from truth root to agent noun is blocked or marginal in all eight. |
| **10. Evidential/epistemic marking** | Particles, auxiliaries, or constructions marking the speaker's epistemic stance (certainty, hearsay, inference, surprise at learning). | ka, yo, fi, eu, ta, id, tr, ko | **Widely attested; formally diverse.** ka: *მართალი თურმე იყო* (particle *თურმე* for reportative/mirative), *მართალი ხომ არის* (particle *ხომ* for shared knowledge/confirmation-seeking), *მართალი მეთქი იყო* (particle *მეთქი* for hearsay); yo: *mo gbàgbọ́ pé ó tọ́* (belief verb + complementizer), *ṣé ó tọ́?* (question particle *ṣé*); fi: *on todennäköistä, että* (impersonal probability construction), *todistettavasti väärä* (adverb "demonstrably"); eu: *gezurra dirudi* (evidential verb *dirudi* "seems"), *egiazko itxura du* (appearance construction); ta: *uṇmai eṉṟu terikiṟatu* (evidential verb *terikiṟatu* "appears"), *uṇmaiyāka irukkalām* (modal possibility); id: *ternyata benar* (grammaticalized evidential "it turns out"), *sebenarnya* (discourse marker "actually"); tr: *gerçekten mi?* (emphatic question), *kesinlikle* (certainty adverb); ko: *사실인 것 같다* (inferential *것 같다*), *틀림없이* (certainty adverb "without fail"). **Key variation:** Georgian has the most elaborated system of dedicated epistemic particles (*თურმე*, *ხომ*, *მეთქი*). Yoruba uses complementizer constructions with epistemic verbs. Finnish uses probability adjectives and adverbs. Korean uses the inferential construction *것 같다* extensively. Indonesian has a grammaticalized evidential (*ternyata*). |
| **11. Degree/emphasis modification** | Intensifiers, emphatic particles, or reduplication strengthen truth claims or indicate degree of correctness/genuineness. | ka, yo, fi, eu, ta, id, tr, ko | **Widely attested; formally diverse.** ka: *ძალიან მართალი* (adverb *ძალიან* "very"), *სრულიად მართალი* ("completely right"), *ნამდვილად* ("genuinely/really"); yo: *ó tọ́ gan-an* (emphatic particle *gan-an* "indeed/really"), *kò tọ́ rárá* (emphatic negation "not at all"); fi: *se on ihan tosi* (intensifier *ihan* "completely"), *totta tosiaan* (emphatic fixed expression); eu: *gezur-gezurra* (full reduplication "complete lie"), *zuzen-zuzen* (reduplication "very straight"), *ziur-ziur* (reduplication "absolutely certain"); ta: *uṇmaiyilaiyē* (particle stacking -il-ē-y-ē), *nijamāka-v-ē* (emphatic -ē); id: *benar-benar* (reduplication "really/truly"), *betul-betul* (reduplication), *benar sekali* ("exactly right"); tr: *doğru dürüst* (alliterative compound "proper/decent"), *kesinlikle* ("certainly/definitely"), *gerçekten de* ("really and truly"); ko: *틀림없이* ("without fail/certainly"), *명백히* ("obviously/clearly"). **Key variation:** Basque uses full reduplication with hyphen as the primary emphatic strategy. Indonesian uses reduplication of the base word. Tamil uses particle stacking. Turkish uses alliterative compounds and lexicalized adverbs. Korean uses certainty adverbs rather than reduplication. Yoruba uses post-predicate emphatic particles. Core truth predicates resist scalar degree modification in yo, ko, fi, eu, ta (truth is binary); intensification is used for emphasis rather than scalar grading. |
| **12. Contrastive/corrective construction** | Fixed frame explicitly rejecting one alternative and asserting another. Used for corrections and clarifications. | ka, yo, fi, eu, ta, id, tr, ko | **Widely attested; formally diverse.** ka: *არა მცდარია, არამედ მართალია* (fixed frame *არა...არამედ* "not X but Y"); yo: (no dedicated fixed frame; focus construction *X ni* used for emphasis: *òtítọ́ ni mo sọ* "it is the truth that I spoke"); fi: *ei ole väärä vaan oikea* ("not wrong but correct"); eu: *egia da, ez gezurra* ("it is truth, not a lie"); ta: *uṇmaiyā poyyā* (contrastive disjunction "true or false?"), *atu uṇmai, tappu illai* ("that is true, not wrong"); id: *bukan salah, melainkan benar* ("not wrong, but correct"); tr: *yanlış değil, doğru* ("not wrong, correct"); ko: *거짓이 아니라 진실이다* ("not a lie but truth"). **Key variation:** Georgian has the most grammaticalized contrastive frame (*არა...არამედ*). Yoruba achieves contrast primarily through the focus construction (*ni*) rather than a dedicated corrective frame. Most languages use the general negation + assertion pattern rather than a fixed corrective frame. |
| **13. Metalinguistic truth predicates (truth-telling and lying as speech acts)** | Fixed expressions describing the act of telling the truth or telling a lie, typically verb + object constructions. | ka, yo, fi, eu, ta, id, tr, ko | **Widely attested.** ka: *სიმართლეს ამბობს* (truth-DAT + says, "tells the truth"), *ტყუილს ამბობს* (lie-DAT + says, "tells lies"), *სიმართლე რომ ვთქვათ* (fixed discourse marker); yo: *sọ òtítọ́* (speak + truth, "tell the truth"), *pa irọ́* (kill/strike + lie, "tell a lie") — asymmetric verbs; fi: *puhua totuutta* (speak + truth-PART, "speak the truth"), *valehdella* (dedicated verb "to lie"); eu: *egia esan* (truth + say, "tell the truth"), *gezurra esan* (lie + say, "tell a lie"); ta: *uṇmaiyai col* (truth-ACC + say, "tell the truth"), *poy col* (lie + say, "tell lies") — asymmetric case marking; id: *berkata jujur* (speak + honestly, "speak honestly"), *berbohong* (dedicated verb "to lie"); tr: *doğru söylemek* (correct + say, "tell the truth"), *yalan söylemek* (lie + say, "tell a lie"); ko: *진실을 말하다* (truth-ACC + say, "tell the truth"), *거짓말하다* (lie + do, "to lie"). **Key asymmetries:** (a) Yoruba uses different verbs for truth-telling (*sọ* "speak") and lying (*pa* "kill/strike"), with *pa irọ́* conventionalized. (b) Tamil marks truth with accusative (*uṇmaiyai*) but lie without case marking (*poy col*). (c) Finnish has a dedicated verb for lying (*valehdella*) but requires verb + object for truth-telling. (d) Korean has a compound verb for lying (*거짓말하다*) but requires verb + object for truth-telling. These asymmetries parallel the agentive asymmetry (Function 9). |
| **14. Appearance/seeming construction** | Construction expressing that something appears or seems true/false without commitment to actual truth value. | ka, yo, fi, eu, ta, id, tr, ko | **Widely attested.** ka: *მართალი ჩანს* (verb *ჩანს* "seems/appears"), *მე მართალი მეჩვენება* (dative experiencer construction); yo: *ó ṣe é mọ̀* ("it is clear/evident"), *ó hàn bí òtítọ́* ("it appears as truth"); fi: *se kuulostaa uskottavalta* ("it sounds credible"), *todennäköisesti* (probability adverb); eu: *gezurra dirudi* (evidential verb *dirudi* "seems"), *egiazko itxura du* ("it has the appearance of being true"); ta: *uṇmai eṉṟu terikiṟatu* ("it appears to be true"), *teḷivāka teriyavillai* ("it doesn't appear clearly"); id: *kelihatannya benar* ("it appears to be true"), *sepertinya* ("seemingly"); tr: *doğru gibi görünmek* ("to seem correct"), *şüpheli görünmek* ("to look suspicious"); ko: *사실인 것 같다* (inferential *것 같다* "seems to be a fact"), *그럴듯하다* ("plausible/seems likely"). **Key variation:** Georgian has a dedicated appearance verb (*ჩანს*) and a dative experiencer variant. Yoruba uses evidential constructions with *hàn* ("show/appear"). Korean uses the inferential *것 같다* construction extensively. Basque uses the dedicated evidential verb *dirudi*. Finnish uses probability adverbs and credibility adjectives rather than a dedicated appearance verb. |
| **15. Verification/confirmation construction** | Construction expressing the act of proving, confirming, or verifying truth through evidence or examination. | ka, yo, fi, eu, ta, id, tr, ko | **Widely attested.** ka: *დაამტკიცა სიმართლე* ("proved the truth"), *გადაამოწმა ინფორმაცია* ("verified the information"), *დაადასტურა* ("confirmed"); yo: *mo ṣe ìdánilójú* ("I confirmed/verified"), *a wádìí òtítọ́ ọ̀rọ̀ náà* ("we investigated the truth of that matter"), *mo fi òtítọ́ hàn* ("I revealed the truth"); fi: *todistaa oikeaksi* ("prove correct"), *todentaa henkilöllisyys* ("verify identity"), *tarkistaa* ("check/verify"); eu: *ikertzaileak datuak egiaztatu ditu* ("the researcher verified the data"), *frogek teoria ziurtatu dute* ("the evidence confirmed the theory"); ta: *uṇmaiyai uṟutipaṭuttu* ("confirm the truth"), *cāṭci* ("witness/evidence"); id: *membenarkan laporan itu* ("confirmed that report"), *mengesahkan dokumen* ("validate documents"), *memverifikasi* ("verify"); tr: *doğrulamak* ("verify/confirm"), *teyit etmek* ("confirm"); ko: *진실을 밝히다* ("reveal the truth"), *사실을 확인하다* ("verify the facts"), *증명하다* ("prove"). **Key variation:** Finnish has the most elaborated verification vocabulary (todistaa, todentaa, tarkistaa, vahvistaa). Yoruba splits verification into investigation (*wádìí*), examination (*yẹ̀wò*), and confirmation (*ìdánilójú*) sub-constructions. Basque has a productive verification stem (*egiazta-*). |
| **16. Doubt/questioning construction** | Construction expressing doubt about or questioning the truth of a claim. | ka, yo, fi, eu, ta, id, tr, ko | **Widely attested.** ka: *ეჭვი მაქვს* (possessive construction "I have doubt"), *საეჭვო ინფორმაციაა* (predicative "it is doubtful information"), *ეჭვი შემეპარა* ([MARGINAL] "doubt seized me"); yo: *ṣé o dájú?* ("are you certain?"), *kò dájú* ("it is not certain"); fi: *epäilen sitä* ("I doubt it"), *se on kyseenalainen* ("it is questionable"); eu: *haren zintzotasuna zalantzan jarri dut* ("I called his honesty into question"), *zalantzan egon* ("to be in doubt"); ta: *cantēkam illai* ("no doubt"), *cantēkamāṉa* ("doubtful"); id: *saya meragukan itu* ("I doubt that"), *itu meragukan* ("that is doubtful"); tr: *şüphelenmek* ("to suspect/doubt"), *şüpheli* ("doubtful/suspicious"), *kuşkulanmak* ("to doubt"); ko: *신빙성이 의심되다* ("credibility is doubted"), *의심스럽다* ("suspicious/doubtful"). **Key variation:** Georgian uses a possessive construction for doubt (*ეჭვი მაქვს* "I have doubt"). ## 3. Narrative Analysis

### Recurring Cross-Language Constructional Patterns

**Attribution and negative attribution are universal but formally diverse.** Every language predicates truth or correctness of a subject and negates such predications, but the formal strategies differ substantially. The most notable variation is in negation placement: Turkish places the negator after the predicate (*değil*), while all other languages place it before the copula or predicate verb. Korean distinguishes nominal predicate negation (*아니다*) from adjectival predicate negation (*-지 않다*). Finnish requires an overt copula where several other languages use zero copula. Georgian incorporates the copula into the adjective as a suffix (-ა). These differences are not trivial: they affect the morphosyntactic integration of truth predicates into the clause and constrain what modifications are possible.

**Adnominal modification shows a consistent word-order split.** In seven of the eight languages, truth-domain adjectives precede the noun they modify. Yoruba is the sole exception, where the modifier follows the head noun and functions as a headless relative clause (*ọ̀rọ̀ tọ́* "true word," literally "word that is correct"). This is not a minor variation: it reflects a fundamental difference in how Yoruba encodes modification, with the truth predicate retaining its verbal character even in attributive position. Korean and Tamil require dedicated adnominal suffixes on the adjective (-ㄴ/-는 in Korean, -āṉa in Tamil), which is a morphological requirement absent in the other languages.

**The inchoative asymmetry is cross-linguistically consistent.** Across all eight languages, constructions expressing "becoming true" or "coming true" are more productive and more naturally expressed than constructions expressing "becoming false" or "turning out to be a lie." The positive direction (Function 4) is realized through dedicated inchoative verbs in several languages (fi *toteutua*, tr *gerçekleşmek*, eu *egiaztatu da*) and through discovery/revelation verbs in others (ko *드러나다*, fi *paljastuu*). The negative direction (Function 5) is consistently expressed through discovery-of-falsity constructions rather than true inchoatives: something is "revealed as false," "proven wrong," or "exposed as a lie" rather than simply "becoming false." Finnish explicitly lacks a common expression for the negative direction. Yoruba lacks attested constructions for both directions, treating truth as a static property. This asymmetry is not a gap in the data but a consistent cross-language pattern: languages conceptualize truth as something that is uncovered or revealed rather than something that changes, and falsity as something that is exposed rather than something that emerges.

**The causative asymmetry parallels the inchoative asymmetry.** Causing deception (Function 7) is consistently more morphologically productive than causing truth (Function 6) across languages. In Yoruba and Korean, causing propositional truth states is pragmatically constrained or absent as a productive construction, while causing deception is natural. In other languages, causing truth is expressed through verification and confirmation verbs (prove, confirm, verify) rather than through direct causatives of truth adjectives. This suggests that across languages, truth is conceptualized as something discovered or established through evidence rather than something directly caused by an agent.

**Metalinguistic truth predicates show a consistent asymmetry between truth-telling and lying.** In at least four languages (yo, fi, ta, ko), truth-telling is expressed through a verb + object construction while lying has a dedicated verb. In Yoruba, the verb used with "lie" (*pa* "kill/strike") is different from the verb used with "truth" (*sọ* "speak"), and the pairing of *pa* with *irọ́* is conventionalized rather than compositionally predictable. In Tamil, truth takes the accusative case marker (*uṇmaiyai col*) while lie appears without case marking (*poy col*). In Korean, lying is expressed as a compound verb (*거짓말하다*) while truth-telling requires a separate object (*진실을 말하다*). These asymmetries are not random: they consistently show that the deception side of the truth domain is more morphologically condensed and lexically integrated than the truth side.

**Discourse marker grammaticalization is universal.** In all eight languages, at least one truth-domain form has grammaticalized into a discourse marker that introduces corrections, frank statements, or factual clarifications. The degree of grammaticalization varies from fully opaque (fi *totta kai* "of course," id *ternyata* "it turns out") to semi-transparent (ka *მართლა?* "really?," ko *사실은* "actually"). This pattern appears across unrelated language families and is not explained by contact. It appears to reflect a general tendency: truth-evaluation is a fundamental discourse management function, and expressions that evaluate truth are recruited for discourse-organizational purposes.

**Verification constructions are universally present but internally organized differently.** All eight languages have constructions for proving, confirming, and verifying truth, but the internal organization of this domain varies. Finnish has the most elaborated verification vocabulary, with distinct verbs for proving (*todistaa*), verifying (*todentaa*), confirming (*vahvistaa*), and checking (*tarkistaa*). Yoruba splits verification into investigation (*wádìí*), examination (*yẹ̀wò*), observation (*àkíyèsí*), and confirmation (*ìdánilójú*) sub-constructions, all built from the productive *ṣe* + noun pattern. Basque has a productive verification stem (*egiazta-*) that generates multiple forms. Other languages use a smaller set of verification verbs. This variation in internal organization does not affect the presence of the function but does affect how finely it is lexically differentiated.

### Important Asymmetries, Substitutions, and Restrictions

**The agentive asymmetry (Function 9) is the most consistent cross-language finding.** Every language lexicalizes "liar" through a morphological derivation; no language productively lexicalizes "truth-teller" through the same strategy. Where a "truth-teller" form exists, it is either marginal (ka *მართლმოსაუბრე*) or requires a compound or periphrastic construction (yo *ọmọ òtítọ́*, ko *진실을 말하는 사람*). This asymmetry is not explained by morphological constraints: the same suffixes that create "liar" forms could in principle create "truth-teller" forms. It appears to reflect a cross-linguistic tendency to lexicalize the negative agent more readily than the positive agent in the deception domain.

**Degree modification is restricted for core truth predicates in multiple languages.** Yoruba, Korean, Finnish, Basque, and Tamil all show documented resistance to scalar degree modification of core truth predicates. In Yoruba, *tọ́* is explicitly treated as binary and non-gradable; the comparative construction (*tọ́ jù ú lọ* "more correct than it") is marked [MARGINAL] in F4. In Korean, truth predicates resist degree modification because truth is conceptualized as categorical rather than scalar. In Finnish, truth-becoming constructions are conceptualized as discrete events rather than gradual processes. This restriction does not apply to related terms like "credibility" or "similarity," which are freely gradable in all languages. The restriction appears to reflect a conceptual framing of truth as binary rather than scalar, though this framing is not universal: Georgian, Indonesian, and Turkish show more gradable uses of truth predicates.

**Yoruba and Korean show the strongest restrictions on inchoative and causative constructions for propositional truth.** Both languages treat truth as a static property that is discovered or revealed rather than something that changes or is caused. In Yoruba, inchoative constructions for propositional truth are marked [MARGINAL] and reinterpreted as discovery; causative constructions for truth states are pragmatically odd. In Korean, morphological causatives for truth predicates are absent; the language uses periphrastic constructions. These restrictions are not found in the other six languages, which show productive inchoative and causative patterns. The restriction appears to reflect a language-specific conceptual framing rather than a universal constraint.

**The appearance/seeming construction (Function 14) substitutes for direct truth assertion in epistemic hedging contexts.** Across all eight languages, speakers use appearance constructions when they want to indicate that something seems true without committing to its actual truth value. This function is realized through dedicated evidential verbs (ka *ჩანს*, eu *dirudi*), inferential constructions (ko *것 같다*), probability adverbs (fi *todennäköisesti*), or grammaticalized evidentials (id *ternyata*). The substitution of appearance constructions for direct truth assertion is a cross-linguistically consistent strategy for managing epistemic commitment.

**The concessive construction (Function 17) uses general adversative conjunctions rather than truth-specific frames.** No language in the dataset has a dedicated concessive construction specific to the truth domain. All languages use general adversative conjunctions (*but*, *however*, *nevertheless*) applied to truth predicates. This contrasts with the contrastive/corrective construction (Function 12), where Georgian has a grammaticalized frame (*არა...არამედ*). The absence of truth-specific concessive frames suggests that concession is a general discourse function that is not specialized for the truth domain.

### Cases Where One Strategy Replaces Another

**Discovery/revelation verbs replace inchoative constructions for truth emergence.** In Korean and Yoruba, and to a lesser extent in Finnish, the inchoative construction ("become true") is replaced by discovery/revelation verbs ("come to light," "be revealed," "be exposed"). This substitution reflects a conceptual difference: rather than truth changing its value, truth is treated as a pre-existing state that becomes known. The substitution is most complete in Yoruba (where inchoatives are absent) and Korean (where they are marginal), and partial in Finnish (where inchoatives exist for some truth-domain terms but not for "becoming untrue").

**Verification verbs replace morphological causatives for causing truth states.** Across all eight languages, the preferred way to express causing something to be true is through lexical verification verbs (prove, confirm, verify, reveal) rather than through morphological causatives of truth adjectives. Even in languages with productive causative morphology (fi, eu, tr), the causative of a truth adjective (*make-true*) is less natural than a verification verb (*prove*, *confirm*). This substitution is most complete in Yoruba and Korean, where causing truth states through morphological causatives is absent or marginal.

**Periphrastic constructions replace morphological agentives for "truth-teller."** As noted above, the morphological agentive that creates "liar" forms does not productively create "truth-teller" forms. Instead, all languages use periphrastic constructions (compounds, relative clauses, or noun phrases) to express the concept of a truth-teller. This substitution is consistent across all eight languages.

**Epistemic particles replace degree modification for emphasizing truth.** In languages where core truth predicates resist scalar degree modification (yo, ko, fi, eu, ta), emphasis is achieved through epistemic particles, emphatic adverbs, or reduplication rather than through degree adverbs. In Yoruba, *gan-an* ("indeed/really") emphasizes truth without implying a scale. In Korean, *틀림없이* ("without fail/certainly") expresses certainty without grading truth. In Basque, reduplication (*gezur-gezurra* "complete lie") emphasizes totality rather than degree. These strategies achieve emphasis while respecting the binary conceptualization of truth.

### Cases Where a Function Is Weakly Expressed, Constructionally Indirect, or Absent

**Function 5 (inchoative, negative direction) is weakly expressed across languages.** No language has a simple, productive inchoative verb meaning "become false/untrue." The function is expressed indirectly through discovery-of-falsity constructions (be revealed as false, turn out to be wrong, be exposed as a lie). Finnish explicitly lacks a common expression for this direction. Yoruba lacks any attested construction. This is the most consistent gap in the constructional inventory across languages.

**Function 6 (causative, causing truth) is constructionally indirect in all languages.** No language has a simple morphological causative of a truth adjective meaning "make true" that is used productively in everyday speech. The function is expressed indirectly through verification verbs (prove, confirm, verify) or through enabling constructions (allow truth to emerge, reveal truth). The directness of causing truth is conceptually constrained across languages.

**Function 9 (agentive nominalization, truth-teller) is absent as a productive morphological pattern in all languages.** As documented above, no language productively derives an agentive noun for "truth-teller" through the same morphological strategy used for "liar." The function is expressed through periphrastic constructions in all eight languages.

**Function 19 (conditional) is not elaborated as a truth-specific construction in any language.** All languages form conditional statements involving truth predicates, but none has a dedicated conditional frame specific to the truth domain. The function is expressed through general conditional constructions applied to truth predicates. This contrasts with the evidential/epistemic marking function (Function 10), which shows more language-specific elaboration.

**Function 20 (legitimacy/validity) shows the greatest cross-language variation in elaboration.** While all languages have some vocabulary for legitimacy and validity, the degree of constructional elaboration varies enormously. Indonesian has the most elaborated legal validity cluster, with dedicated verbs, nominalizations, and fixed legal phrases. Turkish has the most elaborated legitimacy vocabulary. Yoruba expresses this function primarily through the appropriateness verb *yẹ* rather than dedicated validity terms. Korean has formal legitimacy vocabulary (*정당성*, *타당성*) but less constructional elaboration than Indonesian or Turkish. This variation suggests that the legitimacy/validity function is more culturally and institutionally variable than the core truth-evaluation functions.

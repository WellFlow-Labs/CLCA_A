# CLCA Protocol v3.2 — P3 NATURAL SEMANTIC GROUPING
# Language: English (en)

---

## Preliminary Note on Method

Groupings below are derived from observed patterns in P1 and P2: which items appear in the same collocational environments, which are contrasted with each other in usage, which are restricted to particular registers or domains, and which share characteristic syntactic behaviors. No theoretical framework has been imposed. The number of groups, their sizes, and their boundaries reflect the data as documented.

---

## Group 1 — Terms used to evaluate statements and propositions against facts

**Definition:** These terms are used to predicate that a statement, claim, description, or representation does or does not correspond to an external state of affairs. They appear most frequently in contexts where a specific proposition is being assessed for accuracy — in journalism, science, legal testimony, and everyday correction. The primary question they answer is: *does this statement match the facts?*

**Representative expressions from P1 and P2:**

- *true* (#1) — "That statement is true."
- *false* (#2) — "The claim was found to be false."
- *accurate* (#5) — "The report is not an accurate picture of the situation."
- *correct* (#6) — "The answer given was correct."
- *inaccurate* (P2a, Item 5.3) — "The map was found to be inaccurate in several respects."
- *incorrect* (P2a, Item 6.3) — "The form was incorrectly completed."
- *untrue* (P2a, Item 1.4) — "That statement is simply untrue."
- *falsehood* (P2a, Item 2.8) — "The report was riddled with falsehoods."
- *falsity* (P2a, Item 2.1) — "The falsity of the claim was established by documentary evidence."
- *it is true that* (P2b, Item 1.9) — concessive or assertive frame
- *it is false that* (P2b, Item 2.8) — assertive frame for falsity
- *prove true* (P2b, Item 1.4) — "His fears proved true."
- *prove false* (P2b, Item 2.6) — "The prediction proved false within a year."
- *prove correct* (P2b, Item 6.6) — "His prediction proved correct."
- *hold true* (P2b, Item 1.3) — "This observation holds true across all cases."
- *the same is true of* (P2b, Item 1.12) — discourse connective
- *verifiable* (#21) — "The statement is verifiably false."
- *unverifiable* (P2a, Item 21.3) — "The claim is unverifiable given current technology."
- *falsifiable* (P2a, Item 2.6) — "A scientific hypothesis must be falsifiable."
- *unfalsifiable* (P2a, Item 2.7) — "The claim is unfalsifiable."
- *half-truth* (P1b, Item 2.14) — "The advertisement was full of half-truths."
- *false premise* (P1b, Item 3.22) — "The argument fails because it rests on a false premise."

**Notes on internal distinctions within this group:**

*True* and *false* are the most general and syntactically flexible members. *Accurate* and *correct* are more specific: *accurate* tends to appear where the question is one of degree of correspondence (measurements, descriptions, representations), while *correct* tends to appear where a definite right answer or norm exists (answers, procedures, forms). *Untrue* functions as a softer alternative to *false* in many contexts. *Verifiable* and *falsifiable* are used specifically in contexts where the *capacity* to be checked is at issue, rather than the actual truth value.

## Group 2 — Terms used to evaluate the logical structure of arguments

**Definition:** These terms are used specifically to assess whether an argument is well-constructed — whether its conclusion follows from its premises, and whether the premises themselves are acceptable. They appear primarily in philosophical, academic, and formal analytical contexts. The primary question they answer is: *does this reasoning hold together?*

**Representative expressions from P1 and P2:**

- *valid* (#7) — "The argument is valid but the premises are disputed."
- *invalid* (P2a, Item 7.4) — "The contract was declared invalid." / "The argument is invalid."
- *sound* (#8) — "The conclusion rests on sound reasoning."
- *unsound* (P2a, Item 8.3) — "The reasoning was found to be unsound."
- *validity* (P2a, Item 7.1) — "The validity of the argument was disputed."
- *soundness* (P2a, Item 8.1) — "The soundness of the argument was not questioned."
- *a valid argument* (P2b, Item 7.2)
- *a valid point* (P2b, Item 7.1) — extended to general discussion
- *sound reasoning* (P2b, Item 8.1)
- *specious* (#24) — "The court rejected the specious argument."
- *specious reasoning* (P2b, Item 24.3)
- *spurious* (#13) — used of arguments that appear valid but are not
- *false dichotomy* (P1b, Item 3.13) — "The argument presents a false dichotomy."
- *false equivalence* (P1b, Item 3.14) — "Comparing the two incidents is a false equivalence."

**Notes on internal distinctions within this group:**

*Valid* and *sound* have precise technical meanings in formal logic (valid = conclusion follows from premises; sound = valid + true premises), but both are also used in general registers with looser meanings. *Specious* and *spurious* are used to describe arguments that *appear* valid but are not; they carry a stronger implication of deceptive surface plausibility than simply *invalid* or *unsound*. *False dichotomy* and *false equivalence* name specific argument failures rather than evaluating arguments globally.

**Overlap with Group 1:** *Sound* and *valid* can apply to propositions as well as arguments in some uses. *Specious* and *spurious* can apply to claims as well as arguments.

## Group 3 — Terms used to evaluate whether something is what it claims or appears to be

**Definition:** These terms are used to assess whether an object, document, person, or entity is genuinely what it purports to be, as opposed to being an imitation, forgery, or misrepresentation. They appear in contexts involving authentication, fraud detection, and the assessment of origins. The primary question they answer is: *is this the real thing?*

**Representative expressions from P1 and P2:**

- *genuine* (#3) — "The document proved genuine upon examination."
- *authentic* (#4) — "The painting proved authentic after extensive testing."
- *fake* (#11) — "He was exposed as a faker."
- *counterfeit* (#12) — "The bank was flooded with counterfeit currency."
- *spurious* (#13) — "The document was spurious."
- *bona fide* (#22) — "A bona fide offer."
- *ersatz* (#27) — "An ersatz version of the excitement of the original."
- *the genuine article* (P2b, Item 3.1) — "This is the genuine article, not a reproduction."
- *prove genuine* (P2b, Item 3.4)
- *prove authentic* (P2b, Item 4.3)
- *spot a fake* (P2b, Item 11.6)
- *pass off as genuine* (P2b, Item 12.3)
- *counterfeit currency* (P2b, Item 12.1)
- *counterfeit goods* (P2b, Item 12.2)
- *a fake ID* (P2b, Item 11.1)
- *apocryphal* (#23) — applied to stories and sayings of doubtful authenticity
- *pseudo-* (#28) — *pseudoscience*, *pseudo-intellectual*
- *legitimate* (#14) — in the sense of genuine or rightful, as opposed to fraudulent
- *illegitimate* (P2a, Item 14.3) — in the sense of not properly constituted
- *bona fide purchaser* (P2b, Item 22.4) — legal fixed phrase
- *establish one's bona fides* (P2b, Item 26.1)

**Notes on internal distinctions within this group:**

*Genuine* and *authentic* overlap substantially but *authentic* is more commonly used for documents, artworks, and cultural objects, while *genuine* has a broader range including persons and emotions. *Counterfeit* is more specifically associated with deliberate fraud involving currency and branded goods. *Ersatz* carries a connotation of inferiority as well as inauthenticity. *Apocryphal* is used specifically for stories and sayings that are probably not genuine but are difficult to disprove. *Pseudo-* is used productively to mark false claims to a category (*pseudoscience*) rather than fraudulent physical objects.

**Overlap with Group 1:** *Spurious* appears in both Group 2 (arguments) and Group 3 (objects, documents). *Legitimate* and *illegitimate* appear in Group 3 and also in Group 5 below.

## Group 4 — Terms used to evaluate persons as sources of truthful or honest communication

**Definition:** These terms are used to characterize persons (or their statements as products of persons) with respect to their disposition toward honesty, their tendency to tell the truth, or the match between what they express and what they actually believe or feel. They appear in contexts of moral evaluation, legal testimony, and social assessment. The primary question they answer is: *can this person be trusted to communicate honestly?*

**Representative expressions from P1 and P2:**

- *truthful* (#16) — "The witness was instructed to give a truthful answer."
- *sincere* (#17) — "She doubted the sincerity of his apology."
- *honest* (#18) — "He had acted dishonestly in his dealings with clients."
- *veracious* (#25) — "She reported the events veraciously." (formal/literary)
- *dishonest* (P2a, Item 18.3) — "The broker was found to have engaged in dishonest practices."
- *insincere* (P2a, Item 17.3) — "His praise seemed insincere and formulaic."
- *untruthful* (P2a, Item 16.3) — "The report was found to be untruthful."
- *truthfulness* (P2a, Item 16.2) — "The truthfulness of the account was never seriously questioned."
- *sincerity* (P2a, Item 17.1) — "She doubted the sincerity of his apology."
- *honesty* (P2a, Item 18.1) — "Honesty is the best policy."
- *veracity* (P2a, Item 25.1 / P1b, Item 1.3) — "The court questioned the veracity of the witness."
- *to be honest* (P2b, Item 18.1) — discourse marker
- *let's be honest* (P2b, Item 18.7) — discourse marker
- *an honest answer* (P2b, Item 18.4)
- *honest opinion* (P2b, Item 18.5)
- *an honest mistake* (P2b, Item 18.3) — genuine error without deceptive intent
- *be truthful with* (P2b, Item 16.2)
- *truth-teller* (P1b, Item 2.9) — "She was known as a truth-teller."
- *truth-telling* (P1b, Item 2.8)
- *bear false witness* (P2b, Item 2.4) — negative pole
- *false witness* (P1b, Item 3.21) — negative pole

**Notes on internal distinctions within this group:**

*Truthful* focuses on the match between a person's statements and the facts. *Sincere* focuses on the match between a person's expressed feelings or intentions and their actual inner states — a person can be sincere but mistaken. *Honest* is broader, encompassing fairness and integrity in dealings as well as verbal accuracy. *Veracious* and *veracity* are formal/literary variants of *truthful*/*truthfulness* and appear primarily in legal and elevated registers. The discourse markers *to be honest* and *let's be honest* signal a shift to candor rather than predicating a property of a person.

**Overlap with Group 1:** *Truthful* and *untruthful* can apply to statements as well as persons. The boundary between evaluating a person's disposition (Group 4) and evaluating a specific statement (Group 1) is not always sharp in usage.

## Group 5 — Terms used to evaluate conformity with rules, standards, or authority

**Definition:** These terms are used to assess whether something conforms to an established rule, legal norm, recognized standard, or authoritative source. They appear in legal, institutional, and formal contexts. The primary question they answer is: *does this conform to the applicable standard or rule?*

**Representative expressions from P1 and P2:**

- *valid* (#7) — in legal use: "The contract remains valid until the end of the year."
- *legitimate* (#14) — "The legitimacy of the government was disputed."
- *canonical* (#30) — "The text is canonically attributed to the apostle."
- *correct* (#6) — in the sense of conforming to a norm or rule: "The procedure was not correctly followed."
- *legitimate means* (P2b, Item 14.5) — "The dispute should be resolved through legitimate means."
- *a legitimate government* (P2b, Item 14.3)
- *a legitimate claim* (P2b, Item 14.4)
- *illegitimate* (P2a, Item 14.3) — "The use of force was declared illegitimate."
- *legitimacy* (P2a, Item 14.1) — "The legitimacy of the government was disputed."
- *legitimize* (P2a, Item 14.6) — "The election was intended to legitimize the new government."
- *delegitimize* (P2a, Item 14.9) — "The campaign sought to delegitimize the opposition."
- *invalidate* (P2a, Item 7.5) — "New evidence invalidated the earlier findings."
- *validate* (P2a, Item 7.2) — "The results were validated by an independent team."
- *canonically* (P2a, Item 30.1)
- *canonize* (P2a, Item 30.3) — "The work has been canonized as a masterpiece."
- *bona fide* (#22) — in legal use: "bona fide purchaser"
- *self-correcting* (P2a, Item 6.9) — "Science is often described as a self-correcting enterprise."

**Notes on internal distinctions within this group:**

*Legitimate* is primarily used in legal and political contexts where the question is one of rightful authority or conformity with recognized norms. *Valid* in this group refers to legal force or proper constitution rather than logical validity (Group 2). *Canonical* is used in literary, religious, and academic contexts to designate what is recognized as authoritative or standard. *Correct* in this group refers to conformity with a rule or procedure rather than correspondence to fact (Group 1). These uses overlap with other groups but share the feature that the standard being applied is external and institutionally recognized.

**Overlap with Groups 2 and 3:** *Valid* appears in Groups 2 and 5. *Legitimate* appears in Groups 3 and 5.

## Group 6 — Terms used to evaluate sources, evidence, and testimony for dependability

**Definition:** These terms are used to assess whether a source of information, a piece of evidence, or a method of inquiry can be depended upon to produce consistent and accurate results. They appear in journalistic, scientific, legal, and investigative contexts. The primary question they answer is: *can this source or method be relied upon?*

**Representative expressions from P1 and P2:**

- *reliable* (#9) — "According to a reliable source, the decision has already been made."
- *credible* (#10) — "The jury found her to be a credible witness."
- *sound* (#8) — in the sense of reliable judgment or advice
- *a reliable source* (P2b, Item 9.1)
- *a reliable witness* (P2b, Item 9.2)
- *a reliable indicator* (P2b, Item 9.3)
- *a reliable account* (P2b, Item 9.4)
- *a credible witness* (P2b, Item 10.2)
- *a credible account* (P2b, Item 10.3)
- *a credible threat* (P2b, Item 10.1)
- *find credible* (P2b, Item 10.5)
- *credibility* (P2a, Item 10.1) — "The scandal damaged his credibility."
- *reliability* (P2a, Item 9.1) — "The reliability of the witness was questioned."
- *unreliable* (P2a, Item 9.3) — "The narrator is deliberately portrayed as unreliable."
- *incredible* (P2a, Item 10.3) — in the sense of not credible (the weakened intensifier sense is noted but not primary here)
- *sound advice* (P2b, Item 8.2)
- *sound judgment* (P2b, Item 8.3)
- *verifiable facts* (P2b, Item 21.3)
- *independently verifiable* (P2b, Item 21.1)

**Notes on internal distinctions within this group:**

*Reliable* focuses on consistent performance over time — a reliable source is one that has repeatedly proven accurate. *Credible* focuses on believability in a given instance — a credible witness is one whose account seems worthy of belief. The two terms are often used in overlapping contexts but *credible* is more commonly used in legal and evaluative contexts where a judgment is being made about a specific claim or person, while *reliable* is more commonly used in contexts of ongoing dependability. *Sound* in this group refers to the quality of judgment or advice rather than to a source per se.

## Group 7 — Terms used in contexts of precision and exactness of measurement or representation

**Definition:** These terms are used to assess the degree of precision with which something corresponds to a standard, original, or true value. They appear in scientific, technical, editorial, and craft contexts where the question is not merely whether something is right or wrong but how closely it matches a target. The primary question they answer is: *how precisely does this correspond?*

**Representative expressions from P1 and P2:**

- *exact* (#20) — "Those were his exact words."
- *accurate* (#5) — in technical contexts: "accurate to within 0.5 millimeters"
- *faithful* (#19) — "A faithful translation must balance accuracy with readability."
- *exactly* (P2a, Item 20.1) — "That is exactly what I meant."
- *exactness* (P2a, Item 20.2)
- *exactitude* (P2a, Item 20.3) — "He described the events with remarkable exactitude."
- *inexact* (P2a, Item 20.4) — "The term is inexact but widely understood."
- *inexactitude* (P2a, Item 20.7) — including *terminological inexactitude*
- *to be exact* (P2b, Item 20.1) — "There were forty-seven, to be exact."
- *exact words* (P2b, Item 20.4)
- *the exact opposite* (P2b, Item 20.2)
- *accurate to within* (P2b, Item 5.3)
- *broadly accurate* (P2b, Item 5.4)
- *reasonably accurate* (P2b, Item 5.5)
- *a faithful reproduction* (P2b, Item 19.1)
- *a faithful translation* (P2b, Item 19.2)
- *faithful to the original* (P2b, Item 19.4)
- *an exact science* (P2b, Item 20.6)
- *out of true* (P1b, Item 2.26) — craft/technical use: "The door frame is slightly out of true."
- *verisimilitude* (P1b, Item 1.9) — the appearance of precise correspondence in narrative

**Notes on internal distinctions within this group:**

*Exact* implies no margin for deviation. *Accurate* allows for a specified margin (*accurate to within*) and is used with degree modifiers (*broadly accurate*, *reasonably accurate*) in a way that *exact* is not. *Faithful* is used specifically for reproductions, translations, and accounts where the question is adherence to a source rather than to a measured value. *Verisimilitude* is used in aesthetic contexts where the question is the *appearance* of precise correspondence rather than actual correspondence.

**Overlap with Group 1:** *Accurate* and *correct* appear in both Group 1 and Group 7. The distinction is that in Group 1 the question is binary (does it match the facts?), while in Group 7 the question is one of degree (how closely does it match?). In practice the two uses shade into each other.

## Group 8 — Terms used to describe things that appear one way but are actually another

**Definition:** These terms are used to characterize items — arguments, claims, objects, or appearances — that present a misleading surface. The emphasis is on the gap between appearance and reality, particularly where the appearance is plausible or attractive. They appear in critical, academic, and evaluative contexts. The primary question they answer is: *does this look better or more legitimate than it actually is?*

**Representative expressions from P1 and P2:**

- *specious* (#24) — "The argument was speciously constructed to appear more rigorous than it was."
- *spurious* (#13) — "The spuriousness of the correlation was demonstrated."
- *plausible* (#29) — used in contexts where something *seems* true without being established as true
- *verisimilitude* (P1b, Item 1.9) — the appearance of truth in narrative
- *apocryphal* (#23) — stories that circulate as if true but are probably not
- *false modesty* (P1b, Item 3.17) — a performance of modesty that is not genuine
- *a false sense of security* (P2b, Item 2.2) — an unwarranted feeling of safety
- *specious reasoning* (P2b, Item 24.3)
- *a specious argument* (P2b, Item 24.1)
- *a specious claim* (P2b, Item 24.2)
- *a spurious correlation* (P2b, Item 13.1)
- *a spurious argument* (P2b, Item 13.2)
- *plausible deniability* (P2b, Item 29.4) — the ability to appear uninvolved
- *ring false* (P2b, Item 2.7) — "The official explanation rang false to many observers."
- *strike a false note* (P2b, Item 2.3)
- *in a false light* (P2b, Item 2.5)

**Notes on internal distinctions within this group:**

*Specious* and *spurious* both describe things that appear valid or genuine but are not; *specious* emphasizes attractive or persuasive surface appearance, while *spurious* is more neutral about the mechanism of deception. *Plausible* is not inherently negative — it describes something that *seems* reasonable — but it appears in this group because it is frequently used in contexts where the question is whether an appearance of truth is warranted. *Apocryphal* describes stories that have acquired the appearance of authenticity through circulation. *False modesty* and *a false sense of security* describe states or performances that have the surface features of the genuine thing without the substance.

## Group 9 — Terms used in philosophical and technical discourse about truth and meaning

**Definition:** These terms appear primarily in academic philosophical, logical, and scientific registers to discuss the properties of propositions, theories, and perceptions in technical terms. They are largely absent from general speech and are used by specialists to make distinctions that ordinary vocabulary does not mark. They are grouped here not because they share a single meaning but because they share a register and a type of use.

**Representative expressions from P1 and P2:**

- *veridical* (#15) — "A veridical perception accurately corresponds to the external world."
- *veridicality* (P2a, Item 15.1)
- *truth value* (P1b, Item 2.10) — "In classical logic, every proposition has exactly one truth value."
- *truth table* (P1b, Item 2.11)
- *truth condition* (P1b, Item 2.12)
- *truth-conditional* (P1b, Item 2.13)
- *falsifiability* (P2a, Item 2.6) — Popperian criterion
- *falsifiable* (P2a, Item 2.5)
- *unfalsifiable* (P2a, Item 2.7)
- *verificationism* (P1b, Item 1.16) — "Verificationism was the central thesis of the Vienna Circle."
- *verificationist* (P1b, Item 1.17)
- *verisimilar* (P1b, Item 1.10) — used in philosophy and literary theory
- *verity* (P1b, Item 1.4) — in formal and philosophical use
- *a veridical hallucination* (P2b, Item 15.2)
- *veridical experience* (P2b, Item 15.3)
- *empirically verifiable* (P2b, Item 21.2)
- *sound* (#8) — in the technical logical sense (valid + true premises)
- *valid* (#7) — in the technical logical sense

**Notes on this group:**

This group is defined primarily by register and domain of use rather than by a shared semantic property. The items here are used by philosophers, logicians, and scientists to make distinctions that are not routinely made in general speech. Several items in this group (*valid*, *sound*, *falsifiable*) also appear in other groups where they are used in non-technical registers with related but looser meanings.

## Group 10 — Fixed expressions and borrowed forms used to assert genuineness or good faith

**Definition:** These are fixed or semi-fixed expressions, many of Latin or foreign origin, that are used to assert or question the genuineness, sincerity, or legitimacy of a person's credentials, intentions, or actions. They function as units rather than as compositionally transparent phrases and appear in both legal and general registers.

**Representative expressions from P1 and P2:**

- *bona fide* (#22) — "A bona fide offer."
- *bona fides* (#26) — "She quickly established her bona fides."
- *establish one's bona fides* (P2b, Item 26.1)
- *question someone's bona fides* (P2b, Item 26.2)
- *demonstrate one's bona fides* (P2b, Item 26.3)
- *prove one's bona fides* (P2b, Item 26.4)
- *bona fide purchaser* (P2b, Item 22.4) — legal fixed phrase
- *ersatz* (#27) — "An ersatz version."
- *pseudo-* (#28) — *pseudoscience*, *pseudo-intellectual*

**Notes on this group:**

*Bona fide* and *bona fides* are grouped together because they share a Latin source and a common function of asserting or questioning genuine credentials or good faith. The verbal constructions with *bona fides* (*establish*, *question*, *demonstrate*, *prove*) form a recognizable pattern. *Ersatz* and *pseudo-* are included here because they are borrowed or bound forms that function as markers of inauthenticity without the compositional transparency of native English items. *Ersatz* is a loanword with limited morphological integration; *pseudo-* is a bound prefix. Neither generates the kind of idiomatic multi-word constructions that native items do.

## Ambiguous and Overlapping Items

The following items from P1 and P2 do not fit cleanly into a single group or present notable ambiguity:

---

**spurious** (#13)
Appears in Group 2 (arguments), Group 3 (objects and documents), and Group 8 (things that appear one way but are another). In statistical usage (*spurious correlation*), it belongs to Group 8. In usage applied to documents or attributions, it belongs to Group 3. In usage applied to arguments, it belongs to Group 2.

---

**valid** (#7)
Appears in Group 2 (logical arguments), Group 5 (legal and institutional conformity), and Group 6 (in the phrase *a valid concern*, which is closer to Group 6 or a general evaluative use). The technical logical sense (Group 2) and the legal sense (Group 5) are distinct in usage, but the word is used across both without consistent disambiguation.

---

**plausible** (#29)
Appears in Group 8 (things that appear one way but are another) and also functions as a weaker epistemic positive — something that *seems* true without being established. In many uses it is not negative at all (*a plausible explanation*) and belongs to Group 6 (evaluating sources and accounts). *Plausible deniability* belongs to Group 8. The word occupies an intermediate epistemic position that does not map cleanly onto any single group.

---

**accurate** (#5)
Appears in Group 1 (statements evaluated against facts) and Group 7 (precision of measurement or representation). The distinction is one of context: in journalistic and everyday use it belongs to Group 1; in scientific and technical use it belongs to Group 7.

---

**correct** (#6)
Appears in Group 1 (statements evaluated against facts) and Group 5 (conformity with rules and standards). "The answer is correct" belongs to Group 1; "The procedure was not correctly followed" belongs to Group 5.

---

**legitimate** (#14)
Appears in Group 3 (is this the real thing?) and Group 5 (does this conform to rules and authority?). In the sense of *genuine* or *rightful* it belongs to Group 3; in the sense of *lawful* or *properly constituted* it belongs to Group 5. The two senses are related but distinct in usage.

---

**faithful** (#19)
Appears in Group 7 (precision and exactness) in the context of translations and reproductions. In the context of persons and relationships (*faithful to one's word*, *the faithful*), it belongs to a different domain not well captured by any group above — one concerned with loyalty and commitment rather than representational accuracy. The representational sense (Group 7) and the loyalty sense are both active and frequent.

---

**honest** (#18)
Appears in Group 4 (persons as sources of truthful communication) as its primary home. However, *an honest mistake* (P2b, Item 18.3) is used to distinguish genuine error from deliberate deception, which is closer to Group 3 (is this what it appears to be?). *Honest broker* (P2b, Item 18.6) is used in diplomatic and political contexts that are closer to Group 5 or Group 6.

---

**verisimilitude** (P1b, Item 1.9)
Appears in Group 7 (precision and exactness, in the aesthetic sense) and Group 8 (things that appear one way but are another). It describes the *appearance* of truth in narrative, which places it in Group 8, but it is used as a positive aesthetic quality rather than a criticism, which distinguishes it from the other Group 8 items.

---

## Items That Do Not Fit Clearly into Any Group

**post-truth** (P1b, Item 2.27)
This term predicates a social or political condition (*a post-truth era*) rather than a property of any individual statement, person, or object. It does not fit into any of the groups above, which are all concerned with evaluating specific items. It is noted here as ungrouped.

---

**truth commission** (P1b, Item 2.30)
This is an institutional term referring to a specific type of official body. It belongs to political and legal discourse but does not participate in the evaluative patterns that define the groups above. Ungrouped.

---

**truth serum** (P1b, Item 2.28)
This term refers to a pharmacological mechanism and belongs to popular-culture and journalistic discourse. It does not participate in the evaluative patterns above. Ungrouped.

---

**moment of truth** (P1b, Item 2.29)
This term predicates a temporal and dramatic quality (a decisive or revealing moment) rather than a property of a statement, person, or object. It does not fit into any of the groups above. Ungrouped.

---

**ground truth** (P1b, Item 2.16)
In its technical machine-learning and remote-sensing use, this term refers to empirically verified calibration data. It belongs to a specialized technical domain and does not participate in the evaluative patterns above in a way that fits any group cleanly. In its more general use (*the actual state of affairs*), it is close to Group 1 but is not a standard evaluative predicate. Noted as marginal.

---

**true north** (P1b, Item 2.23)
In its figurative use (*the organization's values serve as its true north*), this term predicates a guiding principle rather than a truth-value or authenticity. It does not fit into any group above.

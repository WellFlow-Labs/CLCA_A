# CLCA Protocol v3.4 — P4a CONSTRUCTIONS
**Language: English (en)**

---

## 1. ATTRIBUTION

### Pattern
Copula *be* + truth-domain adjective, predicated of a proposition, object, or person: *[Subject] is true / false / correct / genuine / authentic / valid / real / fake / bogus*

### Examples
- *That is true.* → N/A → That statement corresponds to the facts.
- *The document is genuine.* → N/A → The document is what it purports to be; it is not a forgery.

### Notes
The canonical frame for truth-domain predication in English. The copula *be* is the sole linking verb; no inflection on the adjective. Applies across all sub-domains: propositional (*true/false*), authenticity (*genuine/fake*), logical (*valid/invalid*), and reliability (*credible/reliable*).

---

## 2. NEGATIVE ATTRIBUTION

### Pattern
Negated copula *be not* + adjective, or prefixed negative adjective (*un-*, *in-*, *il-*, *dis-*) in predicative position: *[Subject] is not true / is untrue / is inaccurate / is invalid / is dishonest*

### Examples
- *That is not true.* → N/A → That statement does not correspond to the facts.
- *The contract is invalid.* → N/A → The contract lacks legal force.

### Notes
English uses both analytic negation (*is not true*) and morphological negation (*untrue*, *inaccurate*, *invalid*, *dishonest*, *insincere*, *inauthentic*) with no consistent semantic difference between the two strategies; choice is largely lexical and register-driven. Morphological negation is more common in formal and written registers.

---

## 3. ADNOMINAL MODIFICATION

### Pattern
Truth-domain adjective in prenominal attributive position: *a [adj] [noun]*

### Examples
- *A genuine concern was raised.* → N/A → A sincere, real concern was raised.
- *A spurious correlation appeared in the data.* → N/A → A statistically coincidental, non-meaningful correlation appeared in the data.

### Notes
English truth-domain adjectives freely occupy prenominal attributive position without any morphological change. The same adjective form serves both attributive (*a true statement*) and predicative (*the statement is true*) functions. Postnominal attributive position is not used for this class.

---

## 4. BECOME / INCHOATIVE

### Pattern
*Come* + predicative *true* for the inchoative of realization; *prove* + predicative adjective for the inchoative of confirmed truth; *turn out* + *to be* + adjective for discovered truth-status

### Examples
- *Her prediction came true.* → N/A → Her prediction was realized; what she anticipated actually happened.
- *The report turned out to be false.* → N/A → The report was subsequently discovered to be inaccurate.

### Notes
English lacks a dedicated inchoative morpheme for truth-domain adjectives. The inchoative is expressed periphrastically. *Come true* is restricted to wishes, predictions, and hopes becoming actual. *Turn out to be* + adjective is the general-purpose frame for discovered truth-status and applies across the full range of truth-domain adjectives (*turned out to be genuine / fake / correct / valid*). *Prove* + adjective (*proved false*, *proved correct*) is a more formal variant.

---

## 5. CAUSATIVE

### Pattern
Causative expressed by dedicated verbs derived from or associated with truth-domain adjectives: *verify* (cause to be confirmed), *validate* (cause to be valid), *authenticate* (cause to be authenticated), *falsify* (cause to be false/shown false), *legitimize* (cause to be legitimate), *invalidate* (cause to become invalid)

### Examples
- *The laboratory verified the results.* → N/A → The laboratory confirmed that the results were accurate.
- *New evidence falsified the hypothesis.* → N/A → New evidence showed the hypothesis to be false.

### Notes
English has no productive morphological causative. The causative function is expressed by dedicated verbs, most of which are derived from the relevant adjective via *-ate* (*validate*, *authenticate*, *invalidate*) or *-ify* (*falsify*, *verify*). There is no single causative construction; each truth-domain adjective has its own associated causative verb, and several adjectives lack one entirely (no *\*truify*, *\*genuinize*, *\*correctify*). Where a dedicated causative verb is absent, periphrastic constructions are used (*make correct* → *correct* as a verb; *make real* → *realize*).

---

## 6. ABSTRACT NOMINALIZATION

### Pattern
Suffixation of truth-domain adjective to form an abstract noun: *-ity* (*truth*, *validity*, *authenticity*, *falsity*, *sincerity*, *credibility*, *legitimacy*), *-ness* (*correctness*, *genuineness*, *soundness*, *truthfulness*), *-y* (*honesty*, *accuracy*), *-hood* (*falsehood*), *-th* (*truth* from *true*)

### Examples
- *The authenticity of the painting was disputed.* → N/A → Whether the painting was genuine was disputed.
- *The validity of the argument depends on its premises.* → N/A → Whether the argument's conclusion follows from its premises depends on those premises.

### Notes
Abstract nominalization is highly productive across the truth-domain vocabulary. The suffix *-ity* is the most frequent for Latinate bases (*validity*, *authenticity*, *falsity*, *sincerity*, *credibility*, *veracity*, *legitimacy*); *-ness* is more common for Germanic and hybrid bases (*truthfulness*, *correctness*, *genuineness*, *soundness*). The suffix *-th* that produced *truth* from *true* is no longer productive. Multiple nominalizations may coexist for a single base with partially distinct distributions (*falseness* vs. *falsity*; *genuineness* vs. *authenticity*).

---

## 7. AGENTIVE NOMINALIZATION

### Pattern
Suffixation *-er* or *-or* applied to a verbal base in the truth domain: *truth-teller*, *liar* (from *lie*), *verifier*, *authenticator*, *validator*, *faker*, *counterfeiter*, *corrector*

### Examples
- *She was known as a truth-teller who never softened difficult facts.* → N/A → She was known as a person who consistently told the truth regardless of consequences.
- *The counterfeiter was arrested with printing equipment.* → N/A → The person who made fraudulent imitation currency was arrested.

### Notes
Agentive nominalization in the truth domain is formed by *-er* on verbal bases (*truth-teller*, *faker*, *verifier*) and *-or* on Latinate bases (*authenticator*, *validator*, *corrector*). The compound *truth-teller* is notable in that it nominalizes a practice rather than a single verb. The agentive for the negative pole (*liar*, *deceiver*, *fraud*) is drawn from a separate lexical set not derived from the positive truth-domain adjectives. No agentive is derived directly from *true*, *honest*, *sincere*, or *genuine* (*\*truer*, *\*honester*, *\*sincerer* do not exist as agentives).

---

## 8. DEGREE AND HEDGING MODIFICATION

### Pattern
Degree adverb (*strictly*, *broadly*, *reasonably*, *perfectly*, *completely*) + truth-domain adjective, or partitive construction (*there is a grain of truth in*, *some truth in*): expressing scalar or partial correspondence

### Examples
- *The account is broadly accurate but omits several key facts.* → N/A → The account is correct in its main outlines but not in every detail.
- *There is a grain of truth in what he says.* → N/A → What he says contains a small element of accuracy.

### Notes
Truth-domain adjectives in English participate in scalar modification, reflecting the treatment of truth-domain properties as admitting of degrees in ordinary usage. The adverbs *strictly*, *broadly*, *reasonably*, and *perfectly* form a productive set with *accurate*, *correct*, and *true*. The partitive construction (*a grain of truth*, *some truth in*) expresses partial correspondence and is specific to the nominal *truth*; it has no parallel with *accuracy* or *correctness*. This pattern is absent from the logical sub-domain: *\*broadly valid* and *\*a grain of validity* are not attested in the technical sense.

---

## 9. DISCOURSE-MARKER CONSTRUCTIONS (CANDOR FRAMING)

### Pattern
Fixed infinitival or clause-initial phrase functioning as a sentence adverbial to signal frank disclosure: *to be honest*, *to tell the truth*, *truth be told*, *in truth*, *let's be honest*, *if I am correct*

### Examples
- *To be honest, I was not convinced by the argument.* → N/A → Speaking candidly, I was not convinced by the argument.
- *Truth be told, the project was already failing before she arrived.* → N/A → If I am being frank, the project was already failing before she arrived.

### Notes
This construction type is not derivational or purely attributive but performs a distinct pragmatic function: it frames the following proposition as a candid disclosure, often one the speaker might otherwise have withheld or softened. The subjunctive *be* in *truth be told* marks it as a lexicalized fixed expression. These constructions are concentrated in spoken and informal written registers and are absent from formal logical or legal discourse.

---

## 10. LEGAL AND PROCEDURAL FIXED PHRASES

### Pattern
Fixed multi-word expressions with specialized legal or procedural meaning, not fully compositional: *bona fide*, *under false pretenses*, *the whole truth and nothing but the truth*, *genuine issue of material fact*, *self-authenticating*, *authentic instrument*

### Examples
- *She obtained the funds under false pretenses.* → N/A → She obtained the funds by misrepresenting her identity or intentions.
- *The court found no genuine issue of material fact and granted summary judgment.* → N/A → The court found no real factual dispute requiring a trial and ruled in the moving party's favor.

### Notes
This construction type is distinguished by register-specificity and reduced compositionality: the meaning of the whole cannot be fully predicted from the parts in their ordinary senses. *Bona fide* functions as a fixed Latin borrowing with no productive English morphology. *Genuine issue of material fact* is a term of art in U.S. civil procedure. These expressions are not productive outside their specialized registers.

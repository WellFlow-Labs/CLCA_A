# CLCA Protocol v3.4 — P4b CONSTRUCTION USAGE & CONSTRAINTS
**Language: English (en)**

---

## Construction Usage Table

| Construction Type | Example | Transliteration | Translation | Naturalness Rating | Usage Notes | Attested Alternates |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. ATTRIBUTION** | *That is true.* | N/A | That statement corresponds to the facts. | [CORE] | Ubiquitous across all registers, spoken and written. No observable restriction on subject type (proposition, person, object) or register. | *That holds true.* (slightly more formal); *That is correct.* (preferred in formal/professional contexts) |
| **1. ATTRIBUTION** | *The document is genuine.* | N/A | The document is what it purports to be; it is not a forgery. | [CORE] | Common in legal, archival, and journalistic contexts. The adjective choice (*genuine* vs. *authentic*) is largely register- and domain-driven. | *The document is authentic.* (more formal/technical); *The document checks out.* (informal) |
| **2. NEGATIVE ATTRIBUTION** | *That is not true.* | N/A | That statement does not correspond to the facts. | [CORE] | The analytic negation form is the default in spoken and informal written registers. No restriction on subject type. | *That is false.* (more assertive, formal); *That is untrue.* (slightly more formal) |
| **2. NEGATIVE ATTRIBUTION** | *The contract is invalid.* | N/A | The contract lacks legal force. | [CORE] | Morphological negation (*invalid*, *inaccurate*, *dishonest*) is strongly preferred in formal and written registers, particularly legal and academic. | *The contract is not valid.* (analytic, more neutral); *The contract is void.* (legal term of art) |
| **3. ADNOMINAL MODIFICATION** | *A genuine concern was raised.* | N/A | A sincere, real concern was raised. | [CORE] | Freely used across all registers in prenominal position. No morphological change from predicative form. | *A real concern was raised.* (more informal); *A legitimate concern was raised.* (formal/professional) |
| **3. ADNOMINAL MODIFICATION** | *A spurious correlation appeared in the data.* | N/A | A statistically coincidental, non-meaningful correlation appeared in the data. | [CORE] | Restricted in practice to academic, scientific, and formal analytical registers; *spurious* is a learned Latinate adjective not common in everyday speech. | *A false correlation appeared.* (less precise, informal); *A misleading correlation appeared.* (journalistic) |
| **4. BECOME / INCHOATIVE** | *Her prediction came true.* | N/A | Her prediction was realized; what she anticipated actually happened. | [CORE] | Highly idiomatic and frequent; restricted to subjects that are wishes, predictions, dreams, or hopes. Not extensible to other truth-domain adjectives (*\*came genuine*, *\*came valid*). | *Her prediction was fulfilled.* (more formal); *Her prediction proved correct.* (formal variant) |
| **4. BECOME / INCHOATIVE** | *The report turned out to be false.* | N/A | The report was subsequently discovered to be inaccurate. | [CORE] | General-purpose inchoative frame for discovered truth-status; applies across the full range of truth-domain adjectives. Common in spoken and written narrative registers. | *The report proved false.* (more formal); *The report was found to be false.* (neutral, written) |
| **5. CAUSATIVE** | *The laboratory verified the results.* | N/A | The laboratory confirmed that the results were accurate. | [CORE] | Standard in scientific, academic, and professional registers. *Verify* is the most broadly distributed causative verb in this domain. | *The laboratory confirmed the results.* (slightly less specific); *The laboratory validated the results.* (emphasizes procedural approval) |
| **5. CAUSATIVE** | *New evidence falsified the hypothesis.* | N/A | New evidence showed the hypothesis to be false. | [CORE] | Strongly associated with scientific and philosophical discourse (Popperian falsificationism); less common in everyday speech. | *New evidence disproved the hypothesis.* (more general, less technical); *New evidence refuted the hypothesis.* (common in academic writing, though contested in strict logical usage) |
| **6. ABSTRACT NOMINALIZATION** | *The authenticity of the painting was disputed.* | N/A | Whether the painting was genuine was disputed. | [CORE] | Common in formal, written, and academic registers. Nominalization allows the truth-domain property to function as a subject or object of discourse. | *Whether the painting was genuine was disputed.* (clausal paraphrase, more informal) |
| **6. ABSTRACT NOMINALIZATION** | *The validity of the argument depends on its premises.* | N/A | Whether the argument's conclusion follows from its premises depends on those premises. | [CORE] | Strongly associated with logical, philosophical, and academic registers. *Validity* in the strict logical sense is a term of art; in looser usage it extends to general soundness or acceptability. | *The soundness of the argument…* (overlapping but distinct technical term); *Whether the argument is valid…* (clausal paraphrase) |
| **7. AGENTIVE NOMINALIZATION** | *She was known as a truth-teller who never softened difficult facts.* | N/A | She was known as a person who consistently told the truth regardless of consequences. | [MARGINAL] | *Truth-teller* is attested but less frequent than synonyms; it carries a marked, often evaluative or literary tone. More common in political commentary and journalism than in everyday speech. | *She was known as an honest person.* (more common, less marked); *She was known for her candor.* (abstract nominalization preferred in formal registers) |
| **7. AGENTIVE NOMINALIZATION** | *The counterfeiter was arrested with printing equipment.* | N/A | The person who made fraudulent imitation currency was arrested. | [CORE] | *Counterfeiter* is the standard, unmarked term in legal and journalistic registers for this referent. No notable restriction. | *The forger was arrested.* (overlapping, but *forger* more commonly applies to documents); *The fraudster was arrested.* (broader, less specific) |
| **8. DEGREE AND HEDGING MODIFICATION** | *The account is broadly accurate but omits several key facts.* | N/A | The account is correct in its main outlines but not in every detail. | [CORE] | Common in journalistic, academic, and analytical registers where nuanced assessment is expected. The adverb *broadly* is particularly frequent in this hedging function. | *The account is largely accurate…* (near-synonym, equally common); *The account is mostly correct…* (more informal) |
| **8. DEGREE AND HEDGING MODIFICATION** | *There is a grain of truth in what he says.* | N/A | What he says contains a small element of accuracy. | [CORE] | Idiomatic and frequent in spoken and informal written registers. Restricted to the nominal *truth*; the construction does not extend to *accuracy* or *correctness*. | *There is some truth in what he says.* (less idiomatic, more transparent); *What he says is partially true.* (more analytic paraphrase) |
| **9. DISCOURSE-MARKER CONSTRUCTIONS** | *To be honest, I was not convinced by the argument.* | N/A | Speaking candidly, I was not convinced by the argument. | [CORE] | Very frequent in spoken and informal written registers as a candor-framing device. Functions as a sentence adverbial with no grammatical restriction on the following clause type. | *Honestly, I was not convinced.* (more informal, clipped); *Frankly, I was not convinced.* (slightly more formal) |
| **9. DISCOURSE-MARKER CONSTRUCTIONS** | *Truth be told, the project was already failing before she arrived.* | N/A | If I am being frank, the project was already failing before she arrived. | [MARGINAL] | Less frequent than *to be honest* or *to tell the truth*; carries a slightly literary or emphatic tone. The subjunctive *be* marks it as a fixed expression. More common in written than spoken registers. | *To tell the truth, the project…* (more common spoken variant); *In truth, the project…* (more formal, written) |
| **10. LEGAL AND PROCEDURAL FIXED PHRASES** | *She obtained the funds under false pretenses.* | N/A | She obtained the funds by misrepresenting her identity or intentions. | [CORE] | Standard legal and journalistic fixed phrase; fully lexicalized. Appears in criminal law contexts and in journalistic reporting on fraud. Not productive outside this register. | *She obtained the funds by fraud.* (more general legal term); *She obtained the funds by deception.* (broader, less fixed) |
| **10. LEGAL AND PROCEDURAL FIXED PHRASES** | *The court found no genuine issue of material fact and granted summary judgment.* | N/A | The court found no real factual dispute requiring a trial and ruled in the moving party's favor. | [MARGINAL] | Highly register-restricted: this is a term of art in U.S. civil procedure (Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56) and is not used outside legal contexts. Meaning is not fully compositional from ordinary senses of *genuine*, *issue*, *material*, and *fact*. | *The court found no triable issue of fact.* (alternate legal formulation used in some jurisdictions); no non-legal alternate exists |

## Narrative Analysis

### Recurring Partition Patterns

**The factual/moral split is present in all eight languages but realized differently.** Every language in the dataset maintains some distinction between terms for propositional truth (whether a statement corresponds to facts) and terms for moral honesty (whether a person is characterized by truthfulness as a virtue). However, the degree of lexical separation varies considerably. Finnish and Indonesian show the clearest separation, with distinct root families for each domain (tosi/totuus vs. rehellinen/vilpitön in Finnish; benar vs. jujur in Indonesian). Yoruba and Georgian show partial merger: Yoruba's tọ́ covers both factual correctness and moral rightness, while Georgian's mart'ali spans both epistemic and moral evaluation. Turkish doğru similarly covers both domains. This suggests that while the factual/moral distinction is universally present, it is not universally lexicalized as a sharp boundary.

**The propositional truth / ontological reality distinction is present but variably lexicalized.** All languages distinguish in some way between "this statement is true" and "this thing is real/genuine," but the lexical resources differ. Finnish maintains the clearest separation (tosi for propositional truth, todellinen for ontological reality, aito for material authenticity). Georgian similarly separates č'ešmarit'i (propositional truth) from namdvili (ontological reality). Indonesian separates benar (propositional correctness) from nyata (ontological reality) and asli (material authenticity). By contrast, Turkish gerçek covers both propositional truth and ontological reality without a sharp lexical boundary, and Korean jinsil similarly spans both. Yoruba gidi covers material genuineness but the propositional/ontological distinction is less sharply marked.

**Correctness and error form a distinct grouping in all languages, but the boundary with propositional truth varies.** All languages have terms for correctness/error that are used in educational, procedural, and evaluative contexts (Georgian sc'ori/mcdari, Yoruba tọ́/àṣìṣe, Finnish oikea/virhe, Basque zuzena/okerra, Tamil cari/tappu, Indonesian benar/salah, Turkish doğru/yanlış, Korean matda/teullida). In most languages, these terms overlap substantially with propositional truth terms—the same word that means "correct" also means "true." The clearest separation is in Tamil, where cari/tappu (colloquial correctness/error) is distinct from uṇmai/poy (truth/falsehood), and in Yoruba, where àṣìṣe (error/mistake) is distinct from irọ́ (lie/falsehood). In other languages (Georgian, Finnish, Indonesian, Turkish, Korean), the correctness and propositional truth groupings share vocabulary or overlap substantially.

**Authenticity/genuineness of objects is universally present but variably integrated with propositional truth.** All eight languages have vocabulary for distinguishing genuine from fake objects, documents, or goods. In Finnish, Basque, Indonesian, and Korean, this grouping is relatively distinct from propositional truth vocabulary (Finnish aito/väärennös, Basque benetako/faltsua, Indonesian asli/palsu, Korean jinjja/gajja). In Georgian, Tamil, and Turkish, the same terms used for propositional truth also cover object authenticity (Georgian namdvili, Tamil uṇmaiyāna, Turkish gerçek/asıl). This suggests that the authenticity grouping is sometimes integrated with propositional truth and sometimes separated from it.

**Reliability/trustworthiness is present as a distinct grouping in most languages.** Georgian (sando), Yoruba (fidagarria via borrowing), Finnish (luotettava, uskottava), Basque (fidagarria, sinesgarria), Tamil (nampakam), Indonesian (dapat dipercaya), Turkish (güvenilir), and Korean (sinbingseong) all maintain vocabulary for evaluating whether sources, persons, or information can be trusted. This grouping is consistently distinct from propositional truth evaluation: reliability concerns whether something will consistently provide accurate information, not whether a specific statement is true. The grouping is present in all eight languages but is more elaborated in some (Finnish, Basque) than others (Yoruba, Tamil).

**Legal/formal validity is present in most languages as a distinct grouping.** Georgian (samart'ali, damoc'meba), Finnish (sah via mengesahkan), Basque (zuzenbide), Tamil (sattiyam in oath contexts), Indonesian (sah, valid), Turkish (geçerli, meşru), and Korean (jinsul, jinwi) all maintain vocabulary for legal or formal validity that is distinct from everyday propositional truth. Yoruba shows this grouping less clearly, with ìdájọ́ (judgment/verdict) and ẹ̀rí (testimony/evidence) covering related functions. The legal/formal validity grouping consistently involves conformity to procedural or institutional requirements rather than correspondence to facts.

### Where Languages Split More Finely

**Finnish shows the most fine-grained partition.** Finnish maintains distinct groupings for propositional truth (tosi/totuus), ontological reality (todellinen/todellisuus), material authenticity (aito/väärennös), deliberate falsehood (valhe/valehdella), moral honesty (rehellinen/vilpitön), reliability (luotettava/uskottava), legal validity (sah via mengesahkan), precision (tepat via ketepatan), and probability (todennäköinen). No other language in the dataset maintains this many distinct groupings with dedicated vocabulary for each. Finnish also uniquely separates sincerity (vilpitön "without deceit") from honesty (rehellinen "honest"), a distinction that is merged in most other languages.

**Korean shows the most elaborate partition of actual vs. nominal status.** The sil- (實) compound family in Korean creates a systematic distinction between actual and nominal status across multiple domains (sillyeok "actual ability" vs. claimed ability, siljeok "actual results" vs. planned results, silhyo "actual effect" vs. intended effect, silje "actual reality" vs. theoretical status). No other language shows this degree of systematic lexical elaboration for the actual/nominal distinction. This grouping is distinct from propositional truth evaluation and from ontological reality, focusing specifically on whether something has real-world impact or is merely formal.

**Basque shows the most elaborate partition of certainty and doubt.** Basque maintains distinct vocabulary for certainty (ziurra, ziurtasuna), doubt (zalantza, zalantzagarria), reliability (fidagarria), and plausibility (sinesgarria) as separate groupings. The -garri suffix productively creates evaluative adjectives for each epistemic stance. Most other languages merge some of these distinctions: Turkish kesin covers both certainty and definiteness; Korean hwaksilhada covers both certainty and reliability; Indonesian tidak yakin covers uncertainty without distinguishing doubt from unreliability.

**Tamil shows the most elaborate partition by register.** Tamil uniquely maintains two parallel truth roots (uṇmai and mey) with systematic register stratification, effectively creating two versions of the propositional truth grouping differentiated by formality and philosophical depth. Additionally, Tamil maintains a distinct solemn/religious truth grouping (sattiyam) that is separate from both everyday and literary truth. No other language shows this three-way register partition within the truth domain.

### Where Languages Merge Distinctions

**Turkish hak merges truth, rights, deservingness, and divine names.** The Turkish hak root (from Arabic ḥaqq) uniquely merges what other languages treat as distinct groupings: propositional truth (hak "truth"), moral rightness (haklı "right/justified"), legal rights (hak sahibi "rights holder"), deservingness (hak etmek "to deserve"), and divine names (Hak Teala "God Almighty"). In all other languages, these functions are distributed across separate roots. The merger in Turkish appears to reflect the Arabic source word's semantic breadth rather than a Turkish-specific innovation.

**Yoruba tọ́ merges factual correctness and moral rightness more completely than other languages.** While most languages show some overlap between correctness and moral rightness, Yoruba tọ́ covers both domains without a clear lexical boundary. The same verb that means "to be correct" (ó tọ́ "it is correct") also means "to be morally right" (kì í tọ́ láti ṣe bẹ́ẹ̀ "it is not right to do that"). Other languages that show this merger (Georgian mart'ali, Turkish doğru) maintain additional terms that more clearly separate the domains, but Yoruba shows the most complete merger.

**Indonesian salah merges factual error, moral wrong, and quantification.** Indonesian salah covers factual incorrectness (salah jawaban "wrong answer"), moral transgression (salah besar "grave wrong"), and grammaticalizes as a quantifier (salah satu "one of"). The quantifier use is entirely outside the truth domain and represents a complete semantic shift. No other language in the dataset shows a truth-domain term grammaticalizing into a quantifier.

**Georgian mart'l- merges correctness, moral rightness, management, and religious identity.** The Georgian mart'l- root covers factual correctness (mart'ali "correct"), moral rightness (mart'ali "righteous"), management/governance (mart'va "management," mart'avs "governs"), and religious identity (mart'lmadidebeli "Orthodox Christian"). The management and religious extensions are not found in other languages and represent a distinctive merger of the correctness domain with administrative and confessional domains.

### Cases Where a Language Lacks a Clear Contrast Found Elsewhere

**No language lacks the truth/falsehood distinction, but the lexical realization varies.** All eight languages maintain some lexical contrast between truth and falsehood. However, the degree to which this contrast is lexically elaborated differs substantially. Finnish and Korean show the most elaborate falsehood vocabulary (Finnish: valhe, valehdella, vale- prefix, harhaanjohtava, petollinen; Korean: geojit, geojinmal, geojitjeungeon, geojitjeongbo, geojittuseongi). Yoruba and Basque show somewhat less elaborate falsehood vocabulary, though both maintain productive systems.

**Plausibility/seeming-true is present in some languages but absent or marginal in others.** Finnish (uskottava "believable," todennäköinen "probable"), Turkish (makul "reasonable," akla yakın "plausible"), and Korean (geureoldeuthada "plausible") maintain dedicated vocabulary for the epistemic category of seeming-true or being plausible. Yoruba, Basque, and Tamil show this category less clearly in the data. Georgian and Indonesian have some relevant vocabulary (Georgian: sinesgarria via borrowing; Indonesian: masuk akal "makes sense") but it is less elaborated. This suggests that plausibility as a distinct epistemic category is present in some languages but not universally lexicalized.

**Moral righteousness as a distinct grouping is present in Yoruba and Georgian but less clear in others.** Yoruba òdodo (righteousness/justice) and Georgian samart'liani (just/fair) maintain a distinct grouping for moral righteousness that goes beyond simple honesty to cover justice, fairness, and conformity to moral law. Finnish, Basque, and Indonesian show this category less clearly, with honesty terms (rehellinen, zintzo, jujur) covering the moral domain without a separate righteousness grouping. Tamil niyāyam (justice/fairness) and Korean jeongdanghada (legitimate/justified) cover related territory but are less central to the truth domain.

**Testimony/evidence as a distinct grouping is present in some languages but absent in others.** Yoruba (ẹ̀rí "witness/testimony," jẹ́rìí "to testify"), Finnish (todistaja "witness," todistus "testimony/certificate," todistustaakka "burden of proof"), and Korean (jinsul "statement/testimony," jinwi "truth or falsehood") maintain dedicated vocabulary for testimony and evidence as distinct from general truth-evaluation. Georgian (damoc'meba "verification/attestation"), Basque (lekukotza "testimony"), and Tamil (sāṭci "witness/testimony") show related vocabulary. Indonesian and Turkish show this category less clearly in the data, with verification vocabulary (membenarkan, doğrulamak) covering related functions without a dedicated testimony grouping.

**Consistency/coherence as a distinct grouping is present in Turkish and Finnish but absent or marginal in others.** Turkish tutarlı/tutarsız (consistent/inconsistent) and Finnish johdonmukainen (consistent) maintain a distinct grouping for logical consistency or coherence of arguments and behavior. This grouping is not clearly present in Yoruba, Basque, Tamil, or Korean data. Georgian and Indonesian show some related vocabulary but it is not elaborated as a distinct grouping. This suggests that consistency/coherence is a language-specific elaboration rather than a universal partition.

### Well-Attested Cross-Language Patterns vs. Single-Language Observations

**Well-attested across all or most languages:**
- The truth/falsehood lexical opposition (all eight languages)
- The factual/moral honesty distinction (all eight languages, variably realized)
- Light verb constructions for truth-telling and lying (all eight languages)
- Revelation/concealment as a productive constructional pair (all eight languages)
- Asymmetric agentive nominalization (liars marked, truth-tellers unmarked) (all eight languages)
- Absence of productive causative for "making X true" (all eight languages)
- Discourse markers grammaticalized from truth roots (all eight languages)
- Authenticity/genuineness as a distinct grouping (all eight languages)
- Reliability/trustworthiness as a distinct grouping (all eight languages)
- Categorical rather than scalar treatment of core truth terms (all eight languages)

**Attested in several but not all languages:**
- Legal/formal validity as a distinct grouping (six of eight languages clearly; Yoruba and Tamil less clearly)
- Plausibility/seeming-true as a distinct grouping (Finnish, Turkish, Korean clearly; others less so)
- Precision/exactness as a distinct grouping (Finnish, Georgian, Tamil, Indonesian clearly; others less so)
- Testimony/evidence as a distinct grouping (Yoruba, Finnish, Korean clearly; others less so)
- Certainty/doubt as a distinct grouping (Basque, Turkish, Korean clearly; others less so)

**Single-language or near-unique observations:**
- hak merging truth, rights, deservingness, and divine names (Turkish only)
- Systematic register stratification between two parallel truth roots (Tamil only)
- sil- compound family for actual vs. nominal status (Korean most elaborated; no parallel elsewhere)
- mart'l- extension into management/governance and religious identity (Georgian only)
- salah grammaticalization as a quantifier (Indonesian only)
- tosi- and vale- as productive compound-forming prefixes (Finnish only)
- Moral righteousness (òdodo) as a highly elaborated distinct grouping (Yoruba most clearly)
- zuzen extension into "live broadcast" and direct democracy (Basque only)

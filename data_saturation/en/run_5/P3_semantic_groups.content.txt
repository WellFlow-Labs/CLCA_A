# CLCA Protocol v3.2 — P3 NATURAL SEMANTIC GROUPING
**Language: English (en)**

---

**Note on transliteration:** English uses the Latin script natively; transliteration is not applicable for any item below.

**Scope note:** Groupings below are derived from observed patterns in P1 and P2 data — specifically, which items co-occur in the same contexts, which items are contrasted with each other, which items share typical collocates and registers, and which items are used to do similar evaluative work. No theoretical framework has been imposed. The number of groups, their sizes, and their boundaries reflect the data as documented.

---

## Group 1: Terms used to evaluate statements and propositions against fact

**Label:** Proposition-evaluation terms

**Definition:** These terms are applied primarily to statements, claims, assertions, and propositions to indicate whether they correspond to or deviate from fact. They are the terms most commonly used in contexts where the question is whether something said or written is the case. They contrast directly with each other (e.g., *true* vs. *false*, *accurate* vs. *inaccurate*, *correct* vs. *incorrect*) and appear in logical, journalistic, legal, and everyday contexts where the content of a claim is being assessed.

**Representative expressions:**
- *true* / *truth* / *truly* / *truthful* / *truthfully*
- *false* / *falsely* / *falsehood* / *falsity*
- *accurate* / *accurately* / *accuracy* / *inaccurate* / *inaccuracy*
- *correct* / *correctly* / *correctness* / *incorrect* / *incorrectly*
- *exact* / *exactly* / *exactness* / *to be exact*
- *factually accurate*
- *strictly accurate* / *broadly accurate*
- *untruth* / *untruthful*

**Notes:** *True* and *false* are the broadest members of this group and appear across all registers. *Accurate* and *correct* are more specific: *accurate* tends to appear where closeness to a target or standard is at issue (measurements, descriptions), while *correct* tends to appear where conformity to a rule or expected answer is at issue (answers, procedures). *Exact* occupies the most precise end of the spectrum within this group. *Falsehood* and *untruth* are used in contexts where a more formal or euphemistic alternative to *lie* is preferred.

---

## Group 2: Terms used to assess whether something is what it claims to be

**Label:** Genuineness-assessment terms

**Definition:** These terms are applied to objects, documents, persons, emotions, and representations to evaluate whether they are what they purport to be, as opposed to being imitations, forgeries, or performances. The central question in this group is not whether a proposition is true but whether a thing or person is the real instance of a category. These terms appear frequently in contexts involving art authentication, legal document verification, emotional sincerity, and consumer goods.

**Representative expressions:**
- *genuine* / *genuinely* / *genuineness*
- *authentic* / *authenticity* / *authenticate* / *authentication*
- *real* / *the real thing* / *for real*
- *bona fide* / *bona fides*
- *faithful* (as in *faithful reproduction*, *faithful translation*)
- *counterfeit* (as the negative pole: not what it claims to be)
- *spurious* (negative pole)
- *the genuine article*
- *veridical* (in perception contexts: the perception is what it appears to be)

**Notes:** *Genuine* and *authentic* overlap substantially but show register differences: *authentic* is more common in philosophical discourse (existentialist authenticity, authentic self) and in cultural/aesthetic evaluation, while *genuine* is more common in everyday and commercial contexts (*genuine leather*, *genuine concern*). *Real* overlaps with both but is the most colloquially productive. *Faithful* belongs here specifically in its reproduction/translation sense, where the question is whether a copy corresponds to an original. *Counterfeit* and *spurious* are the primary negative-pole members of this group.

---

## Group 3: Terms used in legal and administrative contexts to assess standing, force, or entitlement

**Label:** Legal-validity and entitlement terms

**Definition:** These terms are used in legal, administrative, and institutional contexts to evaluate whether a document, claim, authority, procedure, or person has the required standing, force, or entitlement under applicable rules or law. The question is not primarily whether something is factually true but whether it meets formal conditions. These terms appear in court proceedings, contract law, administrative decisions, and official documentation.

**Representative expressions:**
- *valid* / *validity* / *validate* / *validation* / *invalid* / *invalidate* / *invalidity*
- *legitimate* / *legitimacy* / *legitimately* / *illegitimate* / *illegitimacy* / *legitimize* / *delegitimize*
- *rightful* / *rightfully* (as in *rightful owner*, *rightful heir*)
- *bona fide* / *bona fides* (in legal and administrative usage)
- *fraudulent* / *fraudulently* / *fraudulent misrepresentation* / *fraudulent claim*
- *false pretenses* / *under false pretenses*
- *false imprisonment*
- *fictitious name* / *fictitious address*
- *verdict* / *open verdict* / *split verdict*
- *legitimate expectation* (technical administrative law term)
- *ostensible authority* (agency law)
- *legitimate authority*

**Notes:** *Valid* and *legitimate* are the core members of this group. *Valid* is used more narrowly for documents, contracts, and logical arguments; *legitimate* is used more broadly for authority, claims, and procedures. *Rightful* overlaps with *legitimate* but tends to appear in contexts of ownership and succession rather than procedural compliance. *Fraudulent* and *false pretenses* are the primary negative-pole members. *Verdict* belongs here because its primary function is institutional and procedural, even though its etymology connects it to the *ver-* (true) root. *Bona fide* appears in both this group and Group 2, depending on context.

---

## Group 4: Terms used to evaluate persons as sources of truthful communication

**Label:** Speaker-trustworthiness terms

**Definition:** These terms are applied to persons — and by extension to their statements, testimony, and accounts — to evaluate whether they can be trusted to communicate truthfully and without deception. They appear in contexts where the character or disposition of a communicator is being assessed, including legal testimony evaluation, moral character assessment, and interpersonal trust. The focus is on the person as a habitual or dispositional truth-teller rather than on any single proposition.

**Representative expressions:**
- *honest* / *honestly* / *honesty* / *dishonest* / *dishonesty*
- *sincere* / *sincerely* / *sincerity* / *insincere* / *insincerity*
- *truthful* / *truthfully* / *truthfulness* / *untruthful*
- *reliable* / *reliably* / *reliability* / *unreliable* / *unreliability*
- *credible* / *credibly* / *credibility* / *incredible* (in its epistemic sense)
- *veracity* / *veracious* / *veraciously*
- *credulous* / *credulity* (the negative counterpart: a person too ready to believe)
- *incredulous* / *incredulity* (the state of not believing)
- *discredit*
- *reliable source* / *reliable witness* / *credible witness*
- *honest broker*
- *truth-teller* / *truth-telling*
- *disingenuous* / *disingenuously* / *disingenuousness*

**Notes:** *Honest* and *sincere* are closely related but show a distinction in usage: *honest* tends to appear in contexts of truthfulness and absence of deception in communication and dealings, while *sincere* tends to appear in contexts where the question is whether an expressed feeling or intention genuinely reflects an inner state. *Reliable* and *credible* both appear in legal testimony contexts but differ: *reliable* emphasizes consistency and dependability, while *credible* emphasizes believability. *Veracity* and *veracious* are more formal and appear primarily in legal and literary registers. *Disingenuous* belongs here as the term most commonly used to describe a person or statement that appears candid but is not.

---

## Group 5: Terms used in scientific and methodological contexts to assess claims against evidence

**Label:** Evidence-and-testability terms

**Definition:** These terms appear in scientific, philosophical, and methodological contexts where the question is whether a claim can be checked against evidence, has been so checked, or meets the conditions for being a properly scientific or empirically grounded assertion. They are used in discussions of research methodology, philosophy of science, and journalistic fact-checking. The central concern is the relationship between a claim and the evidence or procedures that could confirm or disconfirm it.

**Representative expressions:**
- *verifiable* / *verifiability* / *unverifiable* / *verifiable fact* / *independently verifiable*
- *verify* / *verification*
- *falsifiable* / *falsifiability* / *unfalsifiable*
- *falsify* / *falsification* (in the Popperian methodological sense)
- *sound* / *soundness* (in the logical sense: valid argument with true premises)
- *valid* / *validity* (in the logical sense: conclusion follows from premises)
- *truth value* / *truth condition* / *truth-conditional* / *truth table* / *truth-apt* / *truth-aptness*
- *veridical* / *veridicality* (in philosophy of perception)
- *plausible* / *plausibility* (as a weaker epistemic status than verified)

**Notes:** *Verifiable* and *falsifiable* are the most distinctively scientific members of this group; they appear in contexts where the demarcation between scientific and non-scientific claims is at issue. *Sound* and *valid* belong here in their technical logical senses, though both also appear in Group 1 (*valid argument* in everyday use) and Group 3 (*valid document*). *Plausible* occupies a weaker epistemic position within this group: it marks a claim as appearing reasonable without asserting that it has been verified. *Truth value*, *truth condition*, and related terms are primarily philosophical and logical technical vocabulary. *Veridical* belongs here in its philosophy-of-perception use.

---

## Group 6: Terms used to describe deliberate deception or misrepresentation

**Label:** Deliberate-deception terms

**Definition:** These terms are used in contexts where the falsity of a representation is attributed to intentional deception rather than mere error. They appear in legal, moral, and journalistic contexts where the question is not only whether something is false but whether it was made false on purpose, with intent to mislead. They contrast with terms in Group 1 (which are neutral about intent) and with terms like *inaccurate* or *incorrect* (which typically imply error rather than deception).

**Representative expressions:**
- *fraudulent* / *fraudulently* / *fraud* / *fraudulent misrepresentation*
- *counterfeit* / *counterfeiter* / *counterfeiting*
- *falsify* / *falsification* / *falsifier* (in the legal sense)
- *false pretenses* / *under false pretenses*
- *false witness* / *bearing false witness*
- *false flag*
- *fictitious* / *fictitious name* / *fictitious address*
- *spurious* (in the sense of deliberately misattributed or fabricated)
- *disingenuous* / *disingenuously*
- *specious* (in the sense of deliberately misleading rather than merely mistaken)
- *ostensible* (in contexts where the stated purpose conceals the actual one)
- *false consciousness* (in the Marxist theoretical sense: a systematically induced distortion)

**Notes:** *Fraudulent* is the most legally specific member of this group, requiring intent to deceive for gain. *Counterfeit* is more narrowly applied to physical objects (currency, goods, documents). *Falsify* in its legal sense belongs here; in its Popperian methodological sense it belongs to Group 5. *Specious* and *spurious* overlap with Group 2 (genuineness-assessment) but belong here when the emphasis is on deliberate misleading rather than mere inauthenticity. *Ostensible* belongs here when used in contexts where the gap between stated and actual purpose implies deliberate concealment. *Disingenuous* is the most common everyday member of this group.

---

## Group 7: Terms used as discourse markers to signal candor or introduce honest statements

**Label:** Candor-signaling discourse markers

**Definition:** These are fixed expressions and phrases used by speakers to signal that they are about to say something honest, candid, or frank — often something that qualifies, contradicts, or complicates what has just been said or what might be expected. They function as pragmatic markers rather than as descriptors of propositions or persons. They appear across registers from formal to colloquial.

**Representative expressions:**
- *truth be told*
- *to tell the truth*
- *in truth*
- *the truth of the matter*
- *to be honest*
- *honest to God* / *honest to goodness*
- *real talk*
- *true enough*
- *frankly* (not a P1/P2 item but noted as a close functional parallel)
- *if I'm honest* (compositional but functionally parallel)
- *speak truth to power* (partially: signals commitment to candor in adversarial contexts)

**Notes:** These expressions share the function of framing what follows as more candid than what might otherwise be said. They do not describe a proposition as true; they signal the speaker's communicative stance. *Truth be told* and *to tell the truth* are the most common formal members; *real talk* is the most colloquial. *True enough* is slightly different in that it signals qualified agreement rather than introducing a candid statement, but it shares the function of marking an honest assessment.

---

## Group 8: Terms used to describe appearance versus reality

**Label:** Appearance-reality contrast terms

**Definition:** These terms are used in contexts where what something appears to be, is claimed to be, or seems to be is explicitly contrasted with what it actually is. They mark the gap between surface presentation and underlying reality. They appear in journalistic, legal, political, and philosophical contexts where this gap is the central concern.

**Representative expressions:**
- *ostensible* / *ostensibly* (the stated purpose vs. the actual purpose)
- *specious* (appears sound but is not)
- *plausible* (appears reasonable but not necessarily true)
- *apocryphal* (widely circulated but of doubtful authenticity)
- *false modesty* (performed modesty vs. genuine feeling)
- *false dawn* (appears to signal improvement but does not)
- *false note* (appears appropriate but is not)
- *ring false* / *ring true* (the impression of authenticity vs. its absence)
- *too good to be true* (appearance of advantage vs. likely reality)
- *half-truth* (appears to be a full account but is not)
- *economical with the truth* (appears to be honest but withholds)
- *plausible deniability* (the appearance of non-involvement)
- *false flag* (appears to be one party's action but is another's)
- *surreal* / *surrealism* (reality that appears dreamlike or impossible)

**Notes:** This group is defined by the structural contrast between appearance and reality rather than by any single semantic feature. *Ostensible* is the most directly appearance-reality-contrast term in the P1 set. *Specious* and *plausible* both belong here because they describe the appearance of validity or reasonableness rather than its presence. *Apocryphal* belongs here because it marks a story as having the appearance of authenticity without confirmed basis. Several *false* + noun compounds (*false dawn*, *false modesty*, *false note*) belong here because they describe things that appear to be one thing but are another.

---

## Group 9: Terms used in aesthetic, literary, and representational evaluation

**Label:** Representational-fidelity terms

**Definition:** These terms are used in contexts where the question is how closely a representation — a translation, a reproduction, a narrative, a performance, a work of art — corresponds to what it represents or claims to represent. They appear in literary criticism, art history, translation studies, and cultural commentary. The standard of comparison is an original, a reality, or a type rather than a factual proposition.

**Representative expressions:**
- *faithful* / *faithfully* / *faithfulness* (faithful translation, faithful reproduction, faithful account)
- *true to life*
- *accurate* / *accuracy* (in the sense of representational accuracy)
- *authentic* / *authenticity* (in the sense of fidelity to an original or tradition)
- *veridical* (in philosophy of perception: the perception accurately represents reality)
- *verism* / *verismo* / *verist* (artistic movements committed to faithful representation)
- *realistic* / *realistically* / *realism* / *realist*
- *surrealism* / *surrealist* / *surrealistic* (as the contrasting aesthetic)
- *apocryphal* (in the sense of doubtful attribution or authenticity of a text)
- *fictitious* (in the sense of invented rather than real, applied to characters or events)
- *true to type* (in biological and agricultural contexts: conforming to the expected characteristics)

**Notes:** *Faithful* is the most distinctively representational-fidelity term in the P1 set; it appears almost exclusively in contexts where a copy, translation, or account is being evaluated against an original. *Authentic* overlaps with Group 2 (genuineness-assessment) but belongs here when the context is aesthetic or cultural rather than commercial or legal. *Verism* and *verismo* are the most specialized members of this group, confined to art criticism and musicology. *Realistic* and *realism* are the most broadly used members, appearing in both aesthetic and everyday practical contexts.

## Ambiguous and Overlapping Items

The following items from P1 and P2 show patterns of use that place them in more than one group, or that do not fit cleanly into any of the groups above.

---

**sound** (adjective)

In its logical sense (*sound argument* = valid with true premises), *sound* belongs to Group 5 (evidence-and-testability terms). In its general evaluative sense (*sound judgment*, *sound advice*, *of sound mind*), it belongs to Group 4 (speaker-trustworthiness terms) or to a broader evaluative register that is not specifically about truth. The word is used across enough different contexts that it does not belong primarily to any single group.

---

**plausible** / **plausibility**

*Plausible* appears in Group 5 (as a weaker epistemic status than verified) and in Group 8 (appearance-reality contrast: appears reasonable but not confirmed). It also appears in the compound *plausible deniability* (Group 6: deliberate deception). The word's usage spans these contexts without settling into one.

---

**real** / **reality** / **really**

*Real* appears in Group 2 (genuineness-assessment: *the real thing*, *for real*) and in Group 8 (appearance-reality contrast: *real world*, *reality check*, *virtual reality*). The intensifier use of *really* and *real* (as in *really interesting*, *real talk*) is not clearly part of any truth-evaluation group; it represents a use where the epistemic content has largely bleached out.

---

**credible** / **credibility**

*Credible* appears in Group 4 (speaker-trustworthiness: *credible witness*) and in Group 3 (legal-validity: *credible threat* in legal contexts). *Credibility gap* belongs to a political-discourse context that overlaps Groups 3 and 4.

---

**bona fide** / **bona fides**

*Bona fide* appears in Group 2 (genuineness-assessment: *bona fide offer* meaning genuine) and in Group 3 (legal-validity: *bona fide resident* in administrative law). The term's usage is distributed across both contexts.

---

**falsify** / **falsification**

In its legal sense (to alter records with intent to deceive), *falsify* belongs to Group 6 (deliberate-deception terms). In its Popperian methodological sense (to test a hypothesis by attempting to show it false), it belongs to Group 5 (evidence-and-testability terms). The two senses are distinct in usage and context.

---

**valid** / **validity**

In its logical sense, *valid* belongs to Group 5 (evidence-and-testability). In its legal and administrative sense, it belongs to Group 3 (legal-validity). In everyday use (*valid point*, *valid concern*), it functions more like a general evaluative term not specific to any group.

---

**authentic** / **authenticity**

*Authentic* appears in Group 2 (genuineness-assessment: *authentic document*, *authenticate*), Group 9 (representational-fidelity: *authentic performance*, *authentic cuisine*), and in a philosophical-personal-identity context (*authentic self*, *authentic experience*) that does not fit cleanly into any of the groups above. The philosophical-personal-identity use is well-attested in the data but does not share enough usage patterns with other items to constitute a group on its own.

---

**post-truth** / **truthiness**

These two items do not fit into any of the groups above. Both are used to describe conditions or qualities where the normal relationship between truth and public discourse has been disrupted or subverted, but they do so in different ways: *post-truth* describes a political and epistemic condition, while *truthiness* describes a quality of seeming true based on feeling. Neither is used to evaluate a specific proposition, person, or representation in the way the other groups require. They are noted here as ungrouped.

---

**veracity**

*Veracity* appears primarily in Group 4 (speaker-trustworthiness: *question the veracity of the witness*) but is also used to describe the accuracy of accounts and reports in a way that overlaps with Group 1 (proposition-evaluation). Its usage is concentrated in formal and legal registers.

---

**apocryphal**

*Apocryphal* appears in Group 8 (appearance-reality contrast: widely circulated but of doubtful authenticity) and in Group 9 (representational-fidelity: doubtful attribution of texts). It does not fit cleanly into either group and could be considered a marginal member of both.

---

**verdict** / **open verdict** / **split verdict**

These terms belong to Group 3 (legal-validity and entitlement terms) in their primary institutional function. Their etymological connection to the *ver-* (true) root is noted in P1b but is not semantically active in current usage; speakers do not use *verdict* in contexts where the truth-evaluation sense of *ver-* is relevant.

---

## Items That Do Not Fit Clearly Into Any Group

The following items from P1 and P2 show usage patterns that are too specialized, too peripheral, or too context-specific to be assigned to any of the groups above with confidence.

- **veridical** / **veridicality** / **veridically**: Used almost exclusively in philosophy of mind and psychology. The usage base is too narrow to place this item in a group with confidence, though it has affinities with Group 5 and Group 9.
- **verism** / **verismo** / **verist**: Specialized art-criticism and musicology terms. Belong loosely to Group 9 but are too domain-specific to be representative members.
- **eternal verities**: A fixed literary and philosophical collocation. Its usage is too narrow and register-specific to assign to a group.
- **true north** (figurative): Used in motivational and philosophical discourse to mean a guiding principle. The truth-evaluation content is largely absent in this use.
- **true-blue**: Marks loyalty and political allegiance. No clear truth-evaluation content in current usage.
- **falsetto**: Documented in P1b as a peripheral extension of the *fals-* root. No truth-evaluation content in current usage.
- **false consciousness**: Documented in P1b and P2b. Its usage is confined to Marxist theory and critical theory; it does not share enough usage patterns with other items to be placed in a group.
- **surreal** / **surrealism** / **surrealist**: Documented under the *real* derivational family. Belongs loosely to Group 9 (as the contrasting aesthetic to realism) but its primary usage is in art history and everyday description of bizarre experiences, not in truth evaluation.

*End of P3 Natural Semantic Grouping for English (en).*

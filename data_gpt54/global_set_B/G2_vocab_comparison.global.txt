## Narrative analysis

A recurring pattern across the languages is that the lexical field is not organized as a single simple true/false opposition. Most languages in the dataset show at least a partial split between:
1. truth/falsity of statements,
2. correctness or rightness of answers/actions,
3. genuineness/authenticity versus fake or counterfeit status.

This pattern is especially clear in Chinese, Hebrew, Greek, English, Tagalog, and Swahili. Sanskrit also shows it, though in a more scholastic form, and Quechua shows a smaller version of it.

Another recurring pattern is a distinction between broad correctness and narrower precision. Chinese separates 正确 from 准确; Hebrew separates נכון from מדויק; Greek separates σωστός/ορθός from ακρίβεια/ακριβής; English separates correct/right from accurate/exact/precise; Tagalog separates tama/wasto from tumpak/eksakto; Swahili separates broader evaluative uses from sahihi/usahihi. This is a well-attested cross-language pattern in the dataset, not a single-language accident.

A further recurring partition is between truth itself and credibility or trustworthiness. Chinese has 可信 and 可靠; Hebrew has אמין and מהימן; Greek has έγκυρος, credible-like and authority-related terms; English has reliable, trustworthy, credible; Tagalog has mapagkakatiwalaan and maaasahan; Swahili has kuaminika, aminika, aminifu. In these languages, something can be believable or dependable without being presented as already verified true. This distinction is strongly supported across multiple languages.

Many languages also distinguish statement-truth from authenticity of objects, identity, or origin. Chinese has 真品, 假货, 冒牌, 伪造; Hebrew has אותנטי, מקורי, מזויף, זיוף; Greek has αυθεντικός, γνήσιος, πλαστός, παραποιημένος; English has authentic, genuine, fake, counterfeit, forgery; Tagalog has tunay, peke, huwad; Swahili has halisi, ya asili, bandia, feki. This is another robust cross-language pattern.

Several languages additionally separate truth from resemblance or appearance-of-truth. Chinese distinguishes 仿真/逼真 from 真/真实. Hebrew distinguishes דומה, זהה, תואם. Greek has αληθοφανής and αληθοφάνεια. English has ring true, verisimilitude, semblance, true to life. Swahili has -fanana and some realism-related uses of uhalisia. Quechua has rikch’akuy versus more reality-oriented forms like chiqaq kay. This pattern is present in multiple languages, but it is more elaborated in some than others.

Where languages split more finely:
- Chinese is the most finely partitioned in the dataset. It separates everyday truth, official verification, deception/fraud, correctness, precision, credibility, commercial authenticity, realism, original-style authenticity, essence/substance, and legitimacy.
- Sanskrit is especially fine-grained in epistemological and philosophical domains, with separate lexical families for true cognition, proof, validity, and factual correspondence.
- Greek also shows a dense partition, especially among truth, correctness, precision, validity, authenticity, reality, substance, fidelity, and plausibility.
- Hebrew has a strong split among truth, precision, credibility, authenticity, correspondence, justice, and propriety.

Where languages merge more:
- Quechua has fewer distinct lexical zones in the supplied data. It clearly distinguishes truth, lying, mistake, rightness, and guilt, but has less lexical elaboration for authenticity, verification, and credibility than Chinese, Greek, English, or Hebrew.
- Tagalog has clear distinctions, but several broad forms cover multiple areas: totoo overlaps truth and genuineness; tama overlaps factual and moral rightness; tapat overlaps honesty and fidelity.
- Swahili also has broad items such as kweli/-kweli, which can cover both truth and genuineness.

Some languages show gaps or weaker contrasts relative to others:
- Hebrew explicitly lacks a common general inchoative pattern for “become true/correct/authentic” in the supplied data.
- Sanskrit also lacks a single general “make true” or broad inchoative truth verb; dynamicity is concentrated in proof and cognition expressions.
- English has many dynamic expressions, but not one unified verb across the whole field; different subdomains use different verbs such as prove true, authenticate, validate, correct.
- Tagalog has a strong dynamic pattern in the totoo family (magkatotoo, nagkatotoo), but many other nearby domains remain mainly stative.
- Swahili and Quechua likewise show many stative or classificatory expressions, but little evidence for broad “become true/false” verbs in the supplied material.
- Quechua in particular appears to lack clear lexical contrasts for some distinctions that are strong elsewhere, such as a well-developed authenticity-vs-counterfeit zone or a separate credibility-vs-truth zone.

There are also recurring asymmetries. In several languages, falsehood or liar terms are more lexically compact or more central than positive “truth-teller” terms. English has liar versus the weaker truth-teller. Swahili has mwongo and mkweli, but the lying side is especially salient in ordinary accusation. Quechua has llullaq and llulla runa, while positive truth-person expressions are less central in the supplied material. Chinese and Sanskrit do have positive truth-speaker formations, but these are often more moralized or formal.

Another recurring asymmetry is that some languages have specialized official verification language while others do not. Chinese is strongest here with 属实, 经查属实, 查证属实. Tagalog has beripikado / beripikahin as procedural verification terms. Greek has result/discovery patterns like αποδεικνύεται ψευδής and αποδείχθηκε πλαστή. English has prove true, authenticate, validate. Hebrew has revelation/testing expressions rather than a single verification adjective comparable to Chinese 属实.

Single-language observations that should not be overgeneralized include:
- Chinese original-style authenticity terms such as 原汁原味 and 道地.
- Sanskrit’s unusually elaborate प्रमाण / प्रमा system.
- Tagalog’s seriousness shift in totohanin / totohanan.
- Greek’s especially strong substance-vs-form discourse with ουσία, κατ’ ουσίαν, επί της ουσίας.
- Quechua’s repeated lack of attested inchoative truth-state expressions in the supplied material.

Overall, the strongest cross-language patterns supported by the data are:
- truth/falsity is often distinct from correctness,
- correctness is often distinct from precision,
- truth is often distinct from credibility,
- statement-truth is often distinct from authenticity of objects/identity,
- many languages lexicalize lying and fake/counterfeit separately from simple error,
- many languages rely on domain-specific constructions rather than one general “become true” verb.

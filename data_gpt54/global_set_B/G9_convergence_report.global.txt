## Global Convergence Report

### 1. Scope and Data

This report synthesizes results from eight languages: **Chinese (zh), Hebrew (he), Sanskrit (sa), Greek (el), English (en), Tagalog (tl), Swahili (sw), and Quechua (qu)**.

The comparison draws on the full G-phase record, including:
- **F1** vocabulary inventories,
- **F2a** derivational patterns,
- **F2b** recurrent constructions,
- **F3** within-language semantic groupings,
- **F4** constructional comparison,
- **F5** oppositional structures,
- **F6** naturalness judgments for dynamic, causative, and stability-related expressions,
- and the global syntheses in **G1–G8**.

The dataset is comparatively rich for **zh, he, el, en**, and also substantial for **tl** and **sw**. **sa** is especially rich in philosophical and scholastic vocabulary. **qu** is clearly structured but less complete in several subdomains, especially authenticity, verification, and dynamic truth-state expressions. Some construction types are unevenly documented across languages, particularly interrogatives, inchoatives, and causatives.

---

### 2. Core Cross-Language Findings

#### 2.1 Overall semantic organization

Across the sample, truth-related meaning is **not organized as a single binary true/false field**. Instead, most languages show a recurrent partition into several partially overlapping domains:

1. **statement truth vs falsehood**
2. **correctness / right answer / proper result**
3. **precision / exactness**
4. **credibility / trustworthiness**
5. **honesty / sincerity**
6. **genuineness / authenticity**
7. **fake / counterfeit / forged status**
8. **verification / confirmed status**
9. **basis / support / evidential grounding**
10. in some languages, additional neighboring domains such as **validity**, **lawfulness/legitimacy**, **justice/fairness**, **reality**, **essence/substance**, and **resemblance/fidelity**

These distinctions are especially fine-grained in **Chinese**, **Greek**, **Hebrew**, and **English**. **Sanskrit** shows a particularly elaborate split around **truth, correct cognition, proof, and validity**. **Tagalog** and **Swahili** show clear but somewhat broader systems. **Quechua** distinguishes truth, lying, mistake, rightness, and identity-related notions clearly, but with less lexical elaboration in some modern subdomains.

#### 2.2 Shared vocabulary patterns

Several lexical patterns recur strongly across languages:

- A basic **truth vs falsehood** contrast is robustly attested in all languages.
- A separate **correct / incorrect** zone is also widely attested and often distinct from truth proper.
- Many languages distinguish **precision** from broader correctness.
- Many languages distinguish **credibility** from verified truth.
- Many languages distinguish **authenticity/genuineness** from **fake/counterfeit/forged** status.
- Many languages distinguish **mistake/error** from **deception/lying**.

This yields a recurrent cross-language structure in which:
- something may be **true** without being especially **precise**,
- **credible** without being verified,
- **correct** without being **authentic**,
- or **fake** without being merely **mistaken**.

#### 2.3 Shared derivational patterns

The strongest derivational convergence is **abstract nominalization**:
- truth, correctness, accuracy, validity, authenticity, reliability, credibility, and related properties are commonly encoded as nouns.
- This is strong in **zh, he, sa, el, en, sw**, and also present in **tl**.

A second strong pattern is **negative derivation or analytic negation**:
- many languages form or regularly use opposites such as “untrue,” “incorrect,” “invalid,” “inauthentic,” or their analytic equivalents.
- This is especially clear in **zh, he, sa, en**, and also constructionally in **el, tl, sw, qu**.

A third recurrent pattern is **person-label derivation**, especially on the negative side:
- “liar,” “forger,” and similar labels are often compact and lexicalized.
- positive counterparts such as “truth-teller” are often weaker, more formal, or paraphrastic.

Derivational asymmetry is important: many languages readily derive **negative states** and **abstract properties**, but do **not** show equally productive derivation for broad dynamic meanings such as “become true” or “make authentic.”

#### 2.4 Shared constructional patterns

The most stable constructional findings are:

- **simple predicative evaluation** is broadly available in all languages;
- **negative predicative evaluation** is also broadly available;
- **noun modification/classification** is highly recurrent;
- **speech-action constructions** are a major strategy for truth and falsehood.

The speech-action pattern is especially important. Many languages prefer constructions equivalent to:
- “tell the truth,”
- “lie,”
- “answer truthfully,”
rather than relying only on static truth predicates.

Another strong shared pattern is the use of **verification/result constructions** instead of broad inchoatives:
- “verified as true,”
- “proved true,”
- “shown false,”
- “authenticated,”
- “validated.”

This pattern is especially strong in **zh, sa, el, en, tl**, and in different ways in **he**.

#### 2.5 Shared oppositional patterns

The opposition data show strong recurrent contrasts of several types:

- **lexical oppositions**: true/false, genuine/fake, lawful/forbidden, same/different
- **morphological oppositions**: positive vs negated forms
- **constructional oppositions**: affirmation vs denial, truthful speech vs lying, supported vs baseless, verified vs unverified

A notable recurrent pattern is that many languages oppose:
- **truth** to **falsehood**,
- **mistake** to **deception**,
- **authenticity** to **counterfeit/fake**,
- **credibility** to **non-credibility**,
- **support** to **lack of basis**.

#### 2.6 Major asymmetries

Several asymmetries recur across the sample.

**What is widely natural**
- static predication
- negative predication
- noun classification
- abstract nominalization
- speech-action constructions
- lexical verbs for falsification, correction, authentication, validation, or misleading

**What is often restricted or absent**
- a single general **“become true”** system across the whole domain
- a single general **“make X true/correct/authentic”** system
- ordinary inchoatives for **authenticity/genuineness**
- broad dynamic expressions for **verification adjectives**
- strong positive person-labels parallel to “liar” or “forger”

A particularly strong tendency is that **dynamic truth-related meanings are often rerouted through specialized constructions**:
- verification,
- proof,
- revelation,
- correction,
- falsification,
- authentication,
rather than through a general “become” or “make” pattern.

Another recurrent asymmetry is that the **negative side is often more lexically compact**:
- liar > truth-teller
- fake/forged > become genuine
- falsify/forge > make true

### 3. Global Constraint Summary

#### 3.1 Informal constraint surface

The G7a matrix supports a constraint surface in which **truth-related meanings are most robustly realized as stable evaluative states**, especially through:
- predication,
- negated predication,
- noun modification,
- and abstract nominalization.

By contrast, **dynamic change** and **causation** are much less uniform. They are often:
- lexically restricted,
- subdomain-specific,
- or replaced by verification, proof, correction, or falsification constructions.

#### 3.2 Strongly supported constraints

The strongest cross-language constraints are:

1. **Static evaluation is broadly licensed.**
   Most major dimensions support simple predication.

2. **Negative evaluation is broadly licensed.**
   If a dimension supports positive predication, it usually also supports some form of negative predication.

3. **Noun classification is broadly licensed.**
   Many dimensions support modifier constructions such as “true story,” “fake document,” “reliable source.”

4. **Abstract nominalization is broadly licensed.**
   Truth, accuracy, validity, authenticity, credibility, and related notions are commonly nominalized.

5. **Broad inchoative systems are restricted.**
   General “become true/false/correct/authentic” patterns are weak, uneven, or absent.

6. **Broad causative systems are restricted.**
   General “make true/correct/authentic” patterns are weak; specialized lexical verbs are preferred.

7. **Authenticity especially resists ordinary inchoatives.**
   “Become authentic/genuine” is often weak or absent, while falsification/forgery verbs are strong.

8. **Verification is often constructionally specialized.**
   Verified/confirmed status is frequently expressed by dedicated procedural or evidential constructions rather than ordinary adjective syntax.

#### 3.3 Moderately supported patterns

The following are supported, but less uniformly:

- **basis/support/evidential grounding** as a distinct dimension
- **validity/formal standing** as distinct from truth
- **lawfulness/legitimacy** as distinct from truth and correctness
- **justice/fairness** as a neighboring evaluative domain
- **correspondence/fidelity** as distinct from truth proper
- **appearance-of-truth/plausibility** as a separate zone
- **person-labeling** beyond a few entrenched forms
- **interrogative checking** as a dedicated constructional type

These patterns are real in the data, but their distribution is uneven and often language-specific.

#### 3.4 Weak or uncertain areas

Evidence is weakest for:

- broad inchoatives for **honesty/sincerity**
- broad inchoatives for **authenticity**
- broad causatives for **truth/correctness/authenticity**
- dynamic constructions in **essence/substance**
- dynamic constructions in **resemblance/plausibility**
- positive person-label systems parallel to negative ones
- some subdomains in **Quechua**
- some dynamic and causative patterns in **Swahili** and **Hebrew**
- some uncertain lexical items in **Sanskrit** and **Quechua**

#### 3.5 Variability and context dependence

The matrix should not be read as implying identical systems across languages. Several constraints are clearly **partial or context-dependent**:

- **truth vs authenticity** is strongly split in some languages, less so in others
- **correctness vs precision** is strong in some languages, weak in others
- **credibility vs truth** is strongly split in some languages, less elaborated in others
- **reality vs essence** is strongly distinct only in part of the sample
- dynamic patterns often depend on **specific lexical families**, not whole semantic domains

### 4. Candidate Universals and Outliers

#### 4.1 Strong cross-language tendencies

The strongest candidate tendencies from G8 are:

- broad availability of **simple predicative evaluation**
- broad availability of **negative predicative evaluation**
- broad availability of **noun modification/classification**
- recurrent distinction between **truth/falsity** and **correctness**
- recurrent distinction between **correctness** and **precision**
- recurrent distinction between **truth** and **credibility**
- recurrent distinction between **authenticity** and **fake/counterfeit**
- recurrent distinction between **mistake** and **deception**
- strong use of **speech-action constructions**
- recurrent use of **verification/proof/result constructions** instead of broad inchoatives
- broad weakness of a single general **become true / make true** system
- recurrent **discourse-framing expressions** built from truth/reality terms

These are best treated as **strong tendencies**, not absolute universals.

#### 4.2 Tentative or weaker candidates

More tentative candidates include:

- a distinct **basis/support** dimension
- a distinct **appearance-of-truth/plausibility** dimension
- a distinct **essence/substance** dimension within the truth-related field
- a broad asymmetry favoring **negative-side lexicalization**
- a broad asymmetry in which **authenticity resists inchoatives more than falsification does**
- a broad distinction between **correspondence/fidelity** and **truth proper**

These are supported, but less evenly across the sample.

#### 4.3 Major outliers and structural divergences

**Chinese**
- strongest lexical partitioning in the sample
- especially rich in verification, fraud, authenticity, realism, and essence/substance

**Sanskrit**
- unusually elaborate proof/validity/cognition system
- strong scholastic differentiation not matched elsewhere

**Hebrew**
- especially clear absence of a common general inchoative truth-domain pattern
- dynamic meanings routed through revelation, vindication, testing, and refinement

**Tagalog**
- dynamic truth concentrated in the **totoo** family
- notable seriousness shift in **totohanin / totohanan**

**Swahili**
- strong reliance on agreement, genitive, and relative-clause constructions
- dynamicity clearest in credibility rather than truth proper

**Quechua**
- smaller and less elaborated system in several subdomains
- repeated absence of common “become true/false/wrong” expressions in the supplied material
- dynamicity clearer in **error-causation** and **lying** than in truth-state change

These outliers do not invalidate the broader tendencies, but they show that the truth-related domain is structured differently across languages and registers.

### 5. Limitations and Future Work

#### 5.1 Data gaps

Several limitations remain:

- only **eight languages** were included
- language coverage is uneven across families and regions
- some languages are richer in lexical detail than others
- some construction types are unevenly documented, especially:
  - interrogatives,
  - inchoatives,
  - causatives,
  - positive person-labels,
  - support/basis constructions,
  - appearance-of-truth constructions
- **Quechua** is notably thinner in several subdomains
- some domains are register-skewed, especially in **Sanskrit**, **Hebrew**, and **Chinese**

#### 5.2 Methodological limitations

- The analysis is based on structured elicitation and curated lexical/constructional inventories, not balanced corpus counts.
- Some absences may reflect **documentation limits** rather than true grammatical impossibility.
- Some distinctions may be sharper in the dataset than in spontaneous usage, or vice versa.
- Several items are broad and polyfunctional, making clean categorization difficult.
- Dynamic naturalness judgments are especially sensitive to prompt design and lexical choice.

#### 5.3 Recommended next steps

1. **Expand language coverage**
   - especially underrepresented families and regions
   - include more languages with rich evidential, authenticity, or legal-validity systems

2. **Refine prompts for weak areas**
   - inchoatives for truth, correctness, authenticity, credibility
   - causatives for validation, falsification, correction, and support
   - interrogative checking
   - person-label asymmetries

3. **Deepen analysis of specific asymmetries**
   - liar vs truth-teller
   - fake/forged vs become-genuine
   - verification/result vs ordinary inchoative
   - credibility vs sincerity vs verification

4. **Separate subdomains more explicitly**
   - correctness vs precision
   - credibility vs trustworthiness vs honesty
   - authenticity vs reality vs essence
   - resemblance/fidelity vs plausibility

5. **Add corpus-based validation**
   - to test whether reported constructions are frequent, marginal, or register-bound
   - especially for languages where the current evidence is sparse or highly formal

---

## Overall conclusion

The global comparison supports a **multi-dimensional truth-related domain** rather than a single true/false opposition. The strongest cross-language findings concern the recurrent separation of:
- **truth/falsity**,
- **correctness**,
- **precision**,
- **credibility**,
- **authenticity**,
- and **fakery/counterfeit status**.

The most stable realization types are **static predication**, **negative predication**, **noun classification**, and **abstract nominalization**. By contrast, **dynamic change** and **causation** are uneven, often restricted, and frequently replaced by **verification**, **proof**, **correction**, **falsification**, or **authentication** constructions.

The evidence is strongest for broad structural tendencies, weaker for strict universals, and clearly incomplete in some subdomains. Further testing should focus on dynamic constructions, support/verification, and the recurrent asymmetries between positive and negative lexicalization.

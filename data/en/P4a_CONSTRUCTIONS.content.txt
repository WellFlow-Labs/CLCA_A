# CLCA Protocol v3.4 — P4a_CONSTRUCTIONS
# Language: English (en)

## 1. ATTRIBUTION

### Pattern
**Subject + copula BE + truth-domain adjective**

The basic attributive construction uses the copula "be" to predicate truth-domain properties of subjects. The adjective follows the copula directly without additional marking.

### Examples
- That statement is true. /ðæt ˈsteɪtmənt ɪz truː/ "That statement corresponds to facts."
- The document is authentic. /ðə ˈdɒkjumənt ɪz ɔːˈθɛntɪk/ "The document is of genuine origin."

### Notes
This is the unmarked construction for attributing truth-domain properties. Subject type constrains adjective selection: propositions take "true/false/correct," objects take "genuine/fake/authentic," persons take "honest/trustworthy."

---

## 2. NEGATIVE ATTRIBUTION

### Pattern
**Subject + copula BE + NEG + truth-domain adjective**
**Subject + copula BE + negated adjective (un-/in-/dis-)**

English expresses negative attribution through two strategies: analytic negation with "not" or synthetic negation via prefixes.

### Examples
- His claim is not true. /hɪz kleɪm ɪz nɒt truː/ "His claim does not correspond to facts."
- The signature is inauthentic. /ðə ˈsɪɡnətʃər ɪz ˌɪnɔːˈθɛntɪk/ "The signature is not genuine."

### Notes
Both strategies coexist with subtle differences: analytic negation ("not true") is neutral, while synthetic forms ("untrue," "false") often carry stronger implications. Some bases prefer one strategy: "dishonest" is standard, "*not honest" is marked.

---

## 3. ADNOMINAL MODIFICATION

### Pattern
**Truth-domain adjective + noun**

Truth-domain adjectives directly precede and modify nouns without additional marking. Position is fixed (prenominal).

### Examples
- a true story /ə truː ˈstɔːri/ "a factual narrative"
- an honest answer /ən ˈɒnɪst ˈɑːnsər/ "a truthful response"

### Notes
Adjective-noun combinations often show semantic specialization: "true friend" (loyal), "right answer" (correct), "sound judgment" (well-founded). Some combinations are highly conventionalized: "false alarm," "genuine article," "honest mistake."

---

## 4. BECOME / INCHOATIVE

### Pattern
**Subject + BECOME/GET/TURN/PROVE + truth-domain adjective**

Inchoative meaning is expressed through change-of-state verbs. "Become" is formal/neutral, "get" is informal, "prove" emphasizes revelation, "turn out" emphasizes discovery.

### Examples
- The rumor proved false. /ðə ˈruːmər pruːvd fɔːls/ "The rumor was revealed to be untrue."
- His predictions came true. /hɪz prɪˈdɪkʃənz keɪm truː/ "His predictions were realized."

### Notes
"Come true" is a fixed expression for realization of predictions/hopes. "Prove" + adjective emphasizes evidential discovery. "Turn out" + infinitive ("turned out to be false") is common. Simple "become" is rare with truth-domain adjectives; specific verbs dominate.

---

## 5. CAUSATIVE

### Pattern
**Agent + MAKE + object + truth-domain adjective** (limited)
**Agent + causative verb (VERIFY, FALSIFY, VALIDATE, AUTHENTICATE, CORRECT) + object**

English rarely uses analytic causatives (make + adjective) for truth-domain meanings. Instead, dedicated causative verbs predominate.

### Examples
- The lab authenticated the painting. /ðə læb ɔːˈθɛntɪkeɪtɪd ðə ˈpeɪntɪŋ/ "The lab verified the painting's authenticity."
- He corrected the error. /hiː kəˈrɛktɪd ði ˈɛrər/ "He made the error correct/fixed the error."

### Notes
Causative meaning is lexicalized in derived verbs: authenticate (make authentic), validate (make valid), falsify (make false), correct (make correct). The pattern "make + object + adjective" is marginal: "*make the statement true" is awkward; "verify the statement" is preferred.

---

## 6. ABSTRACT NOMINALIZATION

### Pattern
**Derived nominal (truth, honesty, accuracy, validity, etc.)**

Abstract nominalizations denote the quality or state of possessing a truth-domain property. Formation is irregular: -th (truth), -y/-ty (honesty, validity), -ness (correctness), -ity (authenticity).

### Examples
- The truth emerged slowly. /ðə truːθ ɪˈmɜːdʒd ˈsləʊli/ "The factual reality became known."
- We value honesty. /wiː ˈvæljuː ˈɒnɪsti/ "We value the quality of being truthful."

### Notes
Multiple nominalization strategies coexist with semantic differentiation: "truth" (abstract quality/reality), "truthfulness" (character trait), "truism" (obvious truth). Some bases have multiple nominals: "false" → "falsity" (quality), "falsehood" (false statement), "falseness" (rare).

---

## 7. AGENTIVE NOMINALIZATION

### Pattern
**Derived agentive nominal (liar, truth-teller, faker, counterfeiter, etc.)**

Agentive nominals denote persons characterized by truth-domain behavior. Formation varies: -er (faker, counterfeiter), compounds (truth-teller), or suppletive forms (liar, not *falser).

### Examples
- He's a compulsive liar. /hiːz ə kəmˈpʌlsɪv ˈlaɪər/ "He habitually tells untruths."
- She's a truth-teller. /ʃiːz ə ˈtruːθˌtɛlər/ "She habitually speaks truthfully."

### Notes
"Liar" is suppletive (not derived from "lie" by regular process). Agentive formation is productive for some bases (fake → faker, counterfeit → counterfeiter) but absent for others (*truther, *correcter are non-standard). Compounds fill gaps: "truth-teller" (not *truist), "wrongdoer" (not *wronger).

---

## 8. MANNER ADVERBIALS

### Pattern
**Verb + truth-domain adverb (-ly)**

Manner adverbs modify verbs to indicate how an action is performed relative to truth-domain standards. Regular formation via -ly suffix.

### Examples
- She answered honestly. /ʃiː ˈɑːnsərd ˈɒnɪstli/ "She answered in a truthful manner."
- He spoke truthfully. /hiː spəʊk ˈtruːθfəli/ "He spoke in accordance with facts."

### Notes
Adverbs show regular -ly derivation from adjectives. Some function as discourse markers beyond manner: "honestly" (frankly speaking), "truly" (indeed), "really" (emphasis). "Rightly" and "wrongly" span epistemic and moral domains: "rightly predicted" (correctly), "wrongly accused" (unjustly).

---

## 9. DEGREE MODIFICATION

### Pattern
**Degree modifier + truth-domain adjective**
**Comparative/superlative forms (limited)**

Truth-domain adjectives accept degree modification with constraints. Some resist gradation (true/false), others accept it freely (accurate, honest).

### Examples
- The report is completely accurate. /ðə rɪˈpɔːt ɪz kəmˈpliːtli ˈækjərət/ "The report is entirely precise."
- She's more honest than him. /ʃiːz mɔːr ˈɒnɪst ðən hɪm/ "She has a greater degree of honesty."

### Notes
Absolute terms resist gradation: "*very true," "*more false" are marked; "completely true," "entirely false" are acceptable. Gradable terms accept comparative/superlative: "more accurate," "most reliable," "less honest." Some show irregular comparison: "truer" exists but is philosophically contentious.

---

## 10. EVIDENTIAL/EPISTEMIC CONSTRUCTIONS

### Pattern
**It + copula + truth-domain adjective + that-clause**
**Subject + SEEM/APPEAR + truth-domain adjective**

These constructions express epistemic stance toward propositions or mark evidential basis for judgments.

### Examples
- It is true that he resigned. /ɪt ɪz truː ðæt hiː rɪˈzaɪnd/ "The proposition of his resignation corresponds to facts."
- The story seems authentic. /ðə ˈstɔːri siːmz ɔːˈθɛntɪk/ "The story appears to be genuine (based on evidence)."

### Notes
Extraposition with "it" is common for propositional evaluation: "It is false/correct/accurate that..." "Seem/appear" mark evidential uncertainty. "Ring true/false" is a fixed expression for intuitive authenticity judgments: "His excuse rings false."

---

## 11. FIXED DISCOURSE MARKERS

### Pattern
**Conventionalized phrases functioning as discourse markers**

Several truth-domain expressions have grammaticalized into discourse markers that frame utterances rather than directly predicate properties.

### Examples
- To be honest, I don't like it. /tə biː ˈɒnɪst aɪ dəʊnt laɪk ɪt/ "Frankly speaking (discourse frame), I don't like it."
- To tell the truth, I forgot. /tə tɛl ðə truːθ aɪ fəˈɡɒt/ "In truth (discourse frame), I forgot."

### Notes
These function as pragmatic markers rather than truth-evaluations: "to be honest," "honestly," "truthfully," "in truth," "if truth be told." They signal speaker stance (frankness, sincerity) rather than evaluating propositional content. Highly frequent in conversational discourse.

---

## 12. RESULTATIVE CONSTRUCTIONS

### Pattern
**Verb + object + truth-domain adjective (resultative)**
**Verb + object + as + truth-domain adjective**

Resultative constructions indicate the state resulting from an action. Limited productivity with truth-domain adjectives.

### Examples
- They proved him wrong. /ðeɪ pruːvd hɪm rɒŋ/ "They demonstrated that he was incorrect."
- She exposed it as fake. /ʃiː ɪkˈspəʊzd ɪt əz feɪk/ "She revealed its counterfeit nature."

### Notes
"Prove + object + adjective" is the main productive pattern: "prove him right/wrong," "prove it false." "Expose/reveal as fake/counterfeit" marks unmasking of deception. General resultatives (*"paint it true," *"make it authentic") are ungrammatical; specific verbs required.

---

## 13. COMPARATIVE CONSTRUCTIONS

### Pattern
**More/less truth-domain adjective than...**
**As truth-domain adjective as...**

Standard comparative constructions apply with gradable truth-domain adjectives.

### Examples
- This version is more accurate than the first. /ðɪs ˈvɜːʃən ɪz mɔːr ˈækjərət ðən ðə fɜːst/ "This version has greater precision."
- He's as honest as they come. /hiːz əz ˈɒnɪst əz ðeɪ kʌm/ "He has maximal honesty (idiomatic)."

### Notes
Gradable adjectives (accurate, honest, reliable, authentic) accept standard comparative morphology. Non-gradable terms resist comparison: "*more true," "*falser" are marginal. Fixed expressions use comparison idiomatically: "as honest as the day is long," "truer words were never spoken."

---

## 14. METALINGUISTIC NEGATION

### Pattern
**Subject + copula + not + adjective₁ + but + adjective₂**

Metalinguistic negation corrects or refines previous characterizations within the truth domain.

### Examples
- It's not false, just incomplete. /ɪts nɒt fɔːls dʒʌst ˌɪnkəmˈpliːt/ "The characterization as false is rejected; incomplete is more accurate."
- He's not dishonest, merely tactful. /hiːz nɒt dɪsˈɒnɪst ˈmɪəli ˈtæktfəl/ "The characterization as dishonest is rejected; tactful is more accurate."

### Notes
This construction rejects one truth-domain characterization in favor of another, often more nuanced one. Common in argumentative and clarificatory discourse. The second term typically weakens or reframes the first: "not wrong, just different," "not fake, just reproduction."

---

## 15. INTENSIFICATION CONSTRUCTIONS

### Pattern
**Intensifier + truth-domain adjective**
**Truth-domain adjective + intensifying phrase**

Intensification emphasizes degree or absoluteness of truth-domain properties.

### Examples
- That's absolutely true. /ðæts ˈæbsəluːtli truː/ "That corresponds completely to facts."
- He's honest to a fault. /hiːz ˈɒnɪst tə ə fɔːlt/ "He is excessively honest."

### Notes
Intensifiers vary by adjective: "absolutely/completely/entirely" with absolute terms (true, false), "very/extremely/highly" with gradable terms (accurate, reliable). Fixed intensifying phrases: "true to a fault," "honest to God," "dead right," "spot on." Some intensifiers are domain-specific: "gospel truth" (religious origin).

## Narrative Analysis

### Recurring Partition Patterns

**Propositional truth vs. authenticity of objects.** The most consistent partition across all eight languages is between evaluating whether a statement corresponds to reality and evaluating whether an object, person, or relationship is genuine rather than fake or counterfeit. Every language maintains this distinction with distinct vocabulary, though the boundary is not always sharp. Chinese uses 真实 for propositional truth and 真品/真迹 for genuine articles; Hebrew uses emet for propositional truth and amiti/otenti for genuineness; Sanskrit uses satya for propositional truth and halisi (borrowed into Swahili) for purity/genuineness; Greek uses αληθής for propositional truth and αυθεντικός/γνήσιος for authenticity; English uses "true" for propositional truth and "genuine/authentic" for material authenticity; Tagalog uses totoo for propositional truth and tunay for genuineness; Swahili uses kweli for propositional truth and halisi for genuineness; Quechua uses chiqaq for both, showing less differentiation between these senses. The Quechua case is notable: chiqaq appears to cover both propositional truth and material genuineness without a dedicated separate term for the latter, suggesting this partition is less grammatically enforced there than elsewhere.

**Correctness/accuracy vs. moral rightness.** All eight languages distinguish between factual or procedural correctness and moral rightness, but the degree of lexical separation varies. Chinese maintains a relatively sharp separation between 对/错 (correct/wrong in judgments and actions) and 诚/欺 (honest/deceptive in character). Hebrew shows the tsodek/tsedek pair where a single root covers both "you're right" (epistemic) and "justice" (moral/legal), merging what other languages keep separate. Swahili's haki similarly merges truth, rightness, and rights/justice in a single term. Quechua's allin covers both moral goodness and practical correctness without a clear boundary. Sanskrit's dharma covers moral law, cosmic order, and proper conduct in a way that resists separation into purely epistemic or purely moral categories. By contrast, Greek maintains a relatively clear separation between σωστός (correct, procedural) and δίκαιος (just, moral), and English similarly separates "correct" from "righteous." The pattern suggests that merger of epistemic correctness with moral rightness is common but not universal, and that the degree of merger varies considerably.

**Unintentional error vs. deliberate falsehood.** All eight languages maintain a distinction between making a mistake without moral fault and deliberately lying or deceiving. Chinese distinguishes 错 (error) from 谎言/欺骗 (lie/deception); Hebrew distinguishes ta'ut (error) from sheker (lie); Sanskrit distinguishes bhrama/viparyaya (cognitive error) from mṛṣā/chala (deliberate falsehood); Greek distinguishes λάθος (mistake) from ψέμα/ψεύδομαι (lie); English distinguishes mistake/error from lie/deceive; Tagalog distinguishes pagkakamali (mistake) from kasinungalingan/magsinungaling (lie); Swahili distinguishes implied error from uongo/danganya (deception); Quechua distinguishes pantay (to err) from llulla (lie) and pantachiy (to mislead). This partition appears to be universal in the corpus, though the lexical items used differ considerably.

**Productive abstract nominalization.** All eight languages show productive derivation of abstract nouns from truth-domain adjectives or verbs. The morphological strategies differ: Chinese uses -性/-度 suffixes; Hebrew uses -ut; Sanskrit uses -tā/-tva; Greek uses -ότητα/-εια; English uses -ity/-ness/-cy/-th; Tagalog uses ka-...-an circumfix; Swahili uses u- prefix; Quechua uses -y infinitive nominalization. Despite the morphological diversity, the functional pattern is consistent: abstract nouns derived from truth-domain terms are used in formal, philosophical, and analytical discourse across all languages.

**Productive manner adverbials.** All eight languages show productive formation of manner adverbials from truth-domain vocabulary. The strategies again differ: Chinese uses -地; Hebrew uses be- prepositional prefix; Sanskrit uses instrumental case; Greek uses -ώς/-ά suffixes; English uses -ly; Tagalog uses -ta accusative and particle constructions; Swahili uses kwa + abstract noun; Quechua uses -ta accusative. This pattern is universal in the corpus.

**Discourse markers grammaticalized from truth vocabulary.** Seven of eight languages show truth-domain vocabulary grammaticalized into discourse markers. Sanskrit shows less of this pattern, though connective particles (tathā hi, yataḥ tasmāt) function as argumentative discourse markers. The grammaticalized discourse markers in other languages (Chinese 说实话/实际上, Hebrew האמת היא ש..., Greek στην αλήθεια, English to be honest/truth be told, Tagalog sa totoo lang, Swahili kwa kweli/haki ni kwamba, Quechua chiqaqmanta rimay) share a common function of introducing frank statements, corrections, or emphatic affirmations, but the degree of grammaticalization varies.

### Where Languages Split More Finely

**Sanskrit splits epistemic sources more finely than any other language.** The pramāṇa system differentiates perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), testimony (śabda), comparison (upamāna), postulation (arthāpatti), and non-perception (anupalabdhi) as distinct epistemic instruments, each with its own technical vocabulary. No other language in the corpus approaches this degree of differentiation within the epistemic sources cluster.

**Quechua splits evidential source grammatically.** While Sanskrit differentiates epistemic sources lexically, Quechua grammaticalizes the distinction between direct knowledge (-mi), hearsay (-si), and conjecture (-cha) as obligatory morphological suffixes. This is a finer grammatical split than any other language in the corpus, where evidential distinctions are expressed lexically or constructionally rather than as obligatory morphology.

**Greek splits the "false/pseudo-" domain more finely than other languages.** The ψευδο- prefix generates compounds across scientific (ψευδοεπιστήμη, ψευδοπόδιο), legal (ψευδομαρτυρία, ψευδορκία), religious (ψευδοπροφήτης), medical (ψευδοδιάγνωση), and everyday (ψευδαίσθηση) domains. While other languages have terms for false or counterfeit things, none show this degree of productive prefix compounding across such diverse domains.

**Chinese splits the objectivity/subjectivity distinction more finely than other languages.** The 客观/主观 (objective/subjective) pair generates a productive cluster with derived forms (客观性, 主观性, 客观化, 客观地, 主观地, 主观臆断) that constitutes a distinct semantic group. No other language in the corpus shows a comparably elaborated objectivity/subjectivity vocabulary as a distinct cluster within the truth domain.

**Swahili splits the rights/entitlements domain more finely than other languages.** The haki term generates an extensive set of compounds (haki za binadamu, haki ya kura, haki ya kujieleza, haki ya kumiliki, haki ya ardhi, haki ya kazi, haki ya elimu, haki ya afya) and verbal collocations (dai haki, toa haki, pata haki, linda haki, vunja haka, kosa haki) that constitute a distinct and elaborated semantic cluster. Hebrew shows a parallel elaboration around tsedek/tsedakah/tsadik, but Swahili's elaboration is more extensive in the data.

**English splits the "false" compound domain more finely than other languages.** The "false" compounds (false alarm, false start, false dawn, false economy, false flag, false positive, false negative, false imprisonment, false arrest, false consciousness) have specialized meanings not derivable from "false" alone and constitute a distinct semantic cluster. The technical testing vocabulary (false positive/false negative) is particularly specialized and has no clear parallel in other languages.

### Where Languages Merge Distinctions

**Swahili merges truth and rights/justice in haki.** The term haki covers both "truth" (haki ni kwamba... = "the truth is that...") and "rights/justice" (haki za binadamu = "human rights"). This merger is unique in the corpus. In all other languages, truth and rights/justice are covered by distinct lexical items.

**Hebrew merges epistemic rightness and moral/legal justice in the ts-d-k root.** The root generates both tsodek (you're right, epistemic) and tsedek/tsedakah (justice, moral/legal). While other languages have terms that straddle this boundary (Swahili haki, Quechua allin), the Hebrew root shows the most systematic merger through a single productive root.

**Quechua merges moral goodness and practical correctness in allin.** The term allin covers both "good" (moral) and "well/correctly" (practical) without a clear boundary. While other languages have terms that overlap these domains (English "right," Swahili haki), Quechua's allin is the most consistently merged in the data, appearing in both moral character evaluation (allin runa = good person) and practical correctness evaluation (allinta ruway = do it well/correctly).

**Chinese merges falsity, inauthenticity, and hypothetical framing in 假.** The morpheme 假 (jiǎ) covers deliberate falsehood (假话 = lies), material inauthenticity (假冒 = counterfeit), and hypothetical framing (假如 = if, 假设 = suppose). This three-way merger in a single morpheme is unique in the corpus. Other languages use distinct vocabulary for these three functions.

**Sanskrit merges truth, existence, and moral goodness in the sat- root.** The root sat- covers ontological existence (sat = being), propositional truth (satya = true), and moral goodness (satpuruṣa = good person, satsaṅga = association with the good). This three-way merger creates extensive overlap between truth, existence, and virtue that is not paralleled in other languages.

**Tagalog merges correctness and sufficiency/cessation in tama.** The term tama covers correctness (tama ang sagot = the answer is correct) and, in the construction tama na, sufficiency and cessation (tama na = that's enough, stop now). This extension from correctness to sufficiency/cessation is documented in Tagalog and to a lesser extent in hustó na, but is not paralleled in other languages.

### Cases Where a Language Lacks a Clear Lexical Contrast Found Elsewhere

**Objectivity/subjectivity as a distinct cluster.** Chinese has the most elaborated vocabulary for this distinction (客观/主观 with productive derivation). No other language in the corpus shows a comparably elaborated objectivity/subjectivity vocabulary as a distinct semantic cluster. Hebrew, Sanskrit, Greek, English, Tagalog, Swahili, and Quechua all lack a dedicated pair of terms for this distinction that generates productive derivation.

**Dedicated causative for "falsify" or "make false."** Multiple languages lack a dedicated or common causative verb for making something false. Quechua explicitly notes this as a structural gap (no *llullachay in the sense of making something false). Swahili, Tagalog, and Greek similarly lack dedicated "falsify" causatives. English has "falsify" but it covers two distinct senses (alter to make false; prove false). Chinese, Hebrew, and Sanskrit show more elaborated verification vocabulary than falsification vocabulary. This asymmetry—productive causatives for establishing truth but not for establishing falsity—appears across at least five languages.

**Dedicated inchoative for "become false."** Several languages show that inchoative constructions for "becoming true" or "being verified" are more natural or more lexicalized than parallel constructions for "becoming false." Quechua explicitly notes that no dedicated *llullayay "become false" exists while chiqaqyay (become true) is attested. Hebrew F6 notes that "the thing became true" constructions are marginal while revelation verbs are preferred. Greek F6 notes that γίνομαι (become) is unnatural with ψέμα (lie). This asymmetry appears across at least four languages.

**Verisimilitude as a distinct cluster.** Greek has αληθοφανής (apparently true, verisimilar) and αληθοφάνεια (verisimilitude) as a distinct cluster for the appearance of truth in representations. Chinese has 逼真 (lifelike) and 写真 (realistic depiction) in a distinct group for artistic realism. No other language in the corpus shows a comparably elaborated vocabulary for verisimilitude or the appearance of truth in representations as a distinct semantic cluster.

**Pramāṇa-type epistemic source differentiation.** Sanskrit's differentiation of perception, inference, testimony, comparison, postulation, and non-perception as distinct epistemic instruments has no parallel in any other language in the corpus. Other languages distinguish direct from indirect knowledge (Quechua -mi vs. -si, Hebrew rikuywan vs. uyariywan yachay in Quechua parallel) but do not differentiate multiple types of indirect knowledge with dedicated technical vocabulary.

**Reliability of sources as a distinct cluster.** Hebrew (amin, meheyман, aminut, meheymanut) and Chinese (可靠, 可靠性, 可靠度) show the most elaborated vocabulary for reliability of sources as a distinct cluster. Swahili (mapagkakatiwalaan in Tagalog parallel, -a busara) and English (reliable, dependable, trustworthy) also show this cluster. Sanskrit and Quechua show less elaboration of reliability as a distinct cluster separate from validity of knowledge.

**Hypothetical/suppositional framing integrated with falsity vocabulary.** Chinese uniquely integrates hypothetical framing (假如, 假设, 假定, 假想) with the falsity morpheme 假. Other languages use distinct vocabulary for hypothetical framing (Hebrew im, Sanskrit yadi, Greek αν, English if, Tagalog kung, Swahili kama, Quechua -man conditional) that is not lexically connected to falsity terms. This integration in Chinese is not paralleled elsewhere.

### Distinguishing Well-Attested Cross-Language Patterns from Single-Language Observations

**Well-attested across most or all languages (6–8 languages):**
- Propositional truth vs. authenticity of objects (all 8)
- Correctness/accuracy vs. moral rightness (all 8, with varying degrees of merger)
- Unintentional error vs. deliberate falsehood (all 8)
- Productive abstract nominalization from truth-domain terms (all 8)
- Productive manner adverbials from truth-domain terms (all 8)
- Asymmetry between positive and negative agent nouns (6–7 languages)
- Discourse markers grammaticalized from truth vocabulary (7 languages)
- Productive causatives for establishing truth/verifying, with less productive causatives for falsifying (5–6 languages)

**Attested in several languages (3–5 languages):**
- Reliability/trustworthiness as a distinct cluster separate from propositional truth (Chinese, Hebrew, Greek, English, Swahili)
- Certainty/doubt as a distinct cluster (Hebrew, Greek, English, Swahili, Quechua)
- Legal/administrative validity as a distinct cluster (Hebrew, Greek, English, Swahili, Tagalog)
- Resemblance/similarity integrated into the truth domain with epistemic hedging function (Chinese, Hebrew, Greek, Tagalog)
- Asymmetric inchoative productivity (more natural for "become true" than "become false") (Hebrew, Greek, Quechua, and implied in others)

**Attested in one or two languages only:**
- Grammaticalized evidentiality as obligatory morphology (Quechua only)
- Pramāṇa-type differentiation of epistemic sources (Sanskrit only)
- Productive ψευδο- prefix compounding across diverse domains (Greek only)
- Objectivity/subjectivity as a distinct elaborated cluster (Chinese only)
- Dual function of a single term as both "truth" and "rights/justice" (Swahili haki only)
- Integration of falsity morpheme with hypothetical framing (Chinese 假 only)
- Color-modified lie severity system (Greek only)

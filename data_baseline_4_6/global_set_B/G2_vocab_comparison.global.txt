## Narrative Analysis

### Recurring Partition Patterns

**The factual truth/falsity opposition is universal.** Every language in the corpus maintains a primary lexical opposition between truth and falsehood as applied to statements: zh 真/假, he אמת/שקר, sa satya/asatya, el αλήθεια/ψέμα, en true/false, tl totoo/kasinungalingan, sw kweli/uongo, qu chiqan/llulla. This opposition is the most productive source of derived forms and constructions in every language. It is also the most consistently binary: in all eight languages, the primary truth/falsity pair is treated as a direct opposition rather than a scalar or graded contrast.

**A distinction between factual truth and object authenticity is present in most languages, but the degree of lexical separation varies considerably.** At one extreme, Tagalog has the clearest split: totoo is used for factual correspondence of statements, while tunay is used for genuineness and authenticity of persons, objects, and emotions. These two terms are not interchangeable in most contexts. At the other extreme, Quechua uses chiqan for both factual truth and object authenticity without a dedicated split. Chinese, Hebrew, Greek, English, and Swahili fall between these poles: they have specialized terms for object authenticity (zh 真品/真迹, he אמיתי/אותנטי, el γνήσιος/αυθεντικός, en genuine/authentic, sw halisi) that are distinct from the primary truth/falsity terms, but the primary terms (真, אמת, αλήθεια, true, kweli) can also be applied to objects in many contexts. Sanskrit has the least developed vocabulary for object authenticity as a commercial or material domain, with no specialized terms equivalent to zh 真品 or el γνήσιος.

**A correctness/error distinction for answers, procedures, and actions is present in all languages**, but the degree to which it is lexically separated from the factual truth/falsity distinction varies. Chinese maintains a clear separation: 对/错 (correct/wrong) is the primary pair for answers and judgments, while 真/假 is the primary pair for factual truth and object authenticity. Greek similarly separates σωστός/λάθος (correct/wrong) from αλήθεια/ψέμα (truth/lie). Hebrew uses נכון (correct) for factual and procedural correctness, distinct from אמת (truth). Tagalog separates tama/mali (correct/wrong) from totoo/kasinungalingan (true/lie). By contrast, Quechua uses chiqan for both factual truth and correctness without a dedicated correctness/error pair, and allin/mana allin covers both goodness and correctness. Sanskrit uses samyak (right/correct) and sādhu (good/proper) for correctness, but these overlap substantially with moral rightness.

**A moral honesty/sincerity grouping, distinct from factual truth, is present in all languages.** Chinese has 诚/诚实/真诚 (sincere/honest/genuine); Hebrew has כנה/יושר (honest/integrity); Sanskrit has satyavādin/satyavrata (truth-speaking/committed to truth); Greek has ειλικρινής/υποκριτής (sincere/hypocrite); English has honest/dishonest/truthful; Tagalog has tapat/katapatan (honest/honesty); Swahili has adili/uadilifu (just/justice) and the associative construction mtu wa kweli (genuine person); Quechua has chiqan runa/llulla runa (truthful person/lying person) and chiqan sunqu (true heart). In all languages, this grouping evaluates the disposition of persons toward truth-telling rather than the truth-value of specific statements. However, the degree to which this grouping is lexically distinct from the factual truth grouping varies: in Hebrew, the same root (א-מ-נ) covers both; in Chinese, 诚 is clearly distinct from 真; in Tagalog, tapat is clearly distinct from totoo.

**Discourse markers derived from truth terms are present in all languages**, and in all cases these forms have undergone some degree of pragmatic grammaticalization. Chinese 说真的 and 说实话 introduce frank statements; Hebrew באמת? expresses surprise; Greek αλήθεια? functions as a surprise marker; English "to be honest" frames admissions; Tagalog sa totoo lang introduces candid remarks; Swahili kwa kweli emphasizes sincerity; Quechua chiqap and k'ari function as emphatic particles. The cross-linguistic consistency of this pattern — truth terms being recruited as discourse management devices — is notable, though the specific forms and their degree of grammaticalization differ.

**Morphological negation is the dominant antonym-forming strategy in all languages.** No language relies primarily on dedicated lexical antonyms for truth-domain terms; instead, all use some form of negation prefix or particle: zh 不/非/无, he לא, sa a-/an-, el α-/αν-, en un-/in-/dis-, tl hindi/di-, sw si/mana, qu mana. The productivity of this strategy varies: Sanskrit's a-/an- privative is the most systematic, generating asatya, adharma, asādhu, avidyā, ayathārtha, apramā as fully lexicalized forms. English's un-/in-/dis- generates a large number of forms but with some lexical restrictions (dishonest is standard; *undishonest is not). Quechua's mana is a free particle rather than a bound morpheme, making it the most transparent negation strategy.

### Where Languages Split More Finely

**Sanskrit splits the epistemic domain most finely.** No other language in the corpus distinguishes pramāṇa (valid means of knowledge), pramā (valid cognition), apramā (invalid cognition), bhrama (error/illusion), bhrānti (confusion/error), and viparyaya (reversal/mistaken cognition) as distinct lexical items. Other languages have epistemic vocabulary, but none organizes it into a systematic taxonomy within the truth-domain vocabulary. Sanskrit also uniquely splits the reality domain into vyavahāra (conventional/empirical reality) and paramārtha (ultimate reality), with dedicated adverbial forms for each level.

**Greek splits the "false" domain most finely through the ψευδ- prefix.** While other languages have some compound-forming capacity with falsity roots, Greek generates a distinct semantic group of pseudo-X constructions (ψευδοεπιστήμη, ψευδοδημοκρατία, ψευδοδιανοούμενος, ψευδοπληροφορία) that is more productive and systematic than any equivalent in the corpus. Greek also splits the legal/testimonial falsity domain more finely than other languages, with dedicated terms for perjury (ψευδορκία), false witness (ψευδομαρτυρία), and false attribution of authorship (ψευδεπίγραφος).

**Tagalog splits the truth/authenticity domain most clearly** through the totoo/tunay distinction. While other languages have separate terms for factual truth and object authenticity, Tagalog's split is more grammatically consistent: totoo is used for factual correspondence, tunay for genuineness and sincerity, and these terms are not freely interchangeable. Tagalog also splits the deception domain more finely than most languages, with separate terms for lying in speech (sinungaling/kasinungalingan), cheating in transactions/games (dáyà), deceptive appearances (linlang), and fabrication/invention (kathâ/gawâ-gawâ).

**Chinese splits the correctness domain most finely** through the 正 root family, which generates separate groupings for correctness of answers (正确), moral uprightness (正直/正派), legitimacy/authority (正当/正统), normality/regularity (正常/正规), and spatial/temporal precision (正中/正午/正点). No other language generates this many distinct sub-groupings from a single correctness root.

### Where Languages Merge Distinctions

**Hebrew merges factual truth, reliability, faithfulness, and belief under the א-מ-נ root.** This is the most striking merger in the corpus. In all other languages, these domains are covered by distinct roots: Chinese 真 (truth) vs. 信 (belief/trust); Sanskrit satya (truth) vs. śraddhā (faith); Greek αλήθεια (truth) vs. πίστη (faith); English true vs. believe/trust; Tagalog totoo (truth) vs. paniniwala (belief); Swahili kweli (truth) vs. imani (faith); Quechua chiqan (truth) vs. yachay (knowledge). The semantic unity of the א-מ-נ root in Hebrew means that terms for "reliable source" (מקור אמין), "faithful person" (נאמן), "I believe" (אני מאמין), and "truth" (אמת) all share a common root, creating a lexical network that links epistemic, relational, and religious domains in a way not found elsewhere.

**English merges factual correctness, moral propriety, legal entitlement, and practical appropriateness under "right."** This is the broadest single-term merger in the corpus. "Right" can mean "the answer is right" (factual), "it's the right thing to do" (moral), "it's your right" (legal entitlement), and "the right tool for the job" (practical fit). No other language in the corpus has a single term with this range of application. Chinese 正 is broad but does not cover legal entitlement; Hebrew צדק covers justice and rightness but not practical fit; Quechua allin covers goodness and correctness but not legal entitlement.

**Quechua merges moral goodness, practical correctness, and aesthetic quality under allin.** While other languages have broad positive evaluation terms (Chinese 好, English good), Quechua's allin is distinctive in that it is the primary term for correctness in the truth domain as well as for goodness in the moral domain. The negated form mana allin is the standard expression for "bad/wrong" across all these domains. This merger means that Quechua does not maintain a sharp lexical distinction between "this answer is correct" and "this action is good" — both are expressed through allin/mana allin.

**Swahili merges "right/entitlement" with "truth/justice" under haki** (from Arabic ḥaqq). This term covers both "it is true" (in some contexts) and "it is your right" (legal entitlement), paralleling the Hebrew צדק merger of factual rightness and moral justice, though through a different semantic path (Arabic loanword vs. native root).

**Chinese merges substantiality, actuality, and practical implementation under 实.** The root 实 covers "solid/real" (实体, 实物), "actual/factual" (事实, 实际), "practical implementation" (实施, 实现, 落实), and "honest/sincere" (实话, 老实). This range of application — from ontological reality to practical action to moral character — is broader than any equivalent root in the other languages.

### Cases Where a Language Lacks a Clear Lexical Contrast Found Elsewhere

**The appearance/ultimate-reality philosophical distinction is present only in Sanskrit and, to a lesser degree, Chinese.** Sanskrit's vyavahāra/paramārtha distinction, with dedicated adverbial forms (vyavahārataḥ, paramārthataḥ), has no parallel in the other six languages. Chinese has 实际 (practical/actual) vs. 本质 (essential nature) and the philosophical 真理 (fundamental truth) vs. 真相 (factual truth), but these are less formally marked. The other six languages handle this domain through general vocabulary (real/apparent, genuine/fake) without dedicated philosophical terminology.

**A grammatically obligatory evidential system marking epistemic source on truth claims is present only in Quechua.** The suffixes -mi (direct knowledge), -si (hearsay), and -cha (conjecture) are grammatical requirements in main clauses, not optional emphatic particles. All other languages in the corpus have optional epistemic markers (Hebrew ודאי, Chinese 确实, English "apparently," Swahili kwa hakika), but none makes epistemic source grammatically obligatory. This means that in Quechua, the distinction between "I know this directly" and "I heard this from someone" is encoded in the grammar of every truth-related assertion, while in other languages this distinction is expressed only when the speaker chooses to mark it.

**A productive pseudo-X compound system is present only in Greek.** While other languages have some equivalent compounds (English pseudoscience, Chinese 伪科学, Sanskrit pramāṇābhāsa), none generates them as systematically from a single prefix that is also the root for "lie/false." The Greek ψευδ- prefix generates a distinct semantic group of constructions that evaluate whether something falsely claims membership in a legitimate category (science, democracy, philosophy, art). This evaluative function — assessing false claims to category membership — is not lexically systematized in the other seven languages.

**A cessation/sufficiency extension of a correctness term is present only in Tagalog.** The semantic drift of tama (correct) into tama na (enough/stop) is not attested in any other language in the corpus. Chinese 够了 (enough) and 停 (stop) are distinct from correctness terms; Hebrew מספיק (enough) is distinct from נכון (correct); English "that's enough" is distinct from "that's right." The Tagalog merger of correctness and sufficiency/cessation under tama appears to be a language-specific development.

**A dedicated agentive nominalization for "truth-teller" is absent in at least five languages.** English lacks a single derived word (*truth-teller is a compound); Greek lacks *αληθευτής; Chinese lacks a productive single-word form; Swahili lacks a dedicated agent noun for truth-telling (mwenye ukweli is a possessive construction, not an agentive nominalization); Quechua's chiqan rimaq (truth-speaker) is a compound rather than a derived form. By contrast, "liar" is lexicalized in all eight languages: zh 说谎者/骗子, he שקרן, sa mithyāvādin, el ψεύτης, en liar, tl sinungaling, sw mwongo, qu llullakuq. This asymmetry — liar is lexicalized while truth-teller is not — is one of the most consistent cross-linguistic patterns in the corpus.

**A dedicated inchoative for "becoming false/bad" is absent in at least four languages.** Quechua has allinyay (become good) but no attested parallel for deterioration. Chinese 变假 (become fake) is marginal; the preferred expression is 变得虚假. English "come true" is restricted to the positive direction (*come false is ungrammatical). Greek γίνομαι αληθινός (become true) is more natural than the reverse. Hebrew התברר (it became clear/turned out) is the standard inchoative for truth-revelation, but no parallel form for "becoming false" is attested. This asymmetry — positive change toward truth/goodness is more readily lexicalized than negative change — appears across multiple languages, though it is most clearly documented in Quechua and English.

### Distinguishing Well-Attested Cross-Language Patterns from Single-Language Observations

**Well-attested across multiple languages (five or more):**
- Primary binary truth/falsity lexical opposition
- Morphological negation as dominant antonym-forming strategy
- Discourse markers derived from truth terms
- Asymmetry between lexicalized "liar" and non-lexicalized "truth-teller"
- Distinction between factual truth and object authenticity (though degree of lexical separation varies)
- Causative constructions for verification/correction using dedicated verbs rather than direct causatives of truth adjectives
- Constructions treating truth as a hidden object that can be revealed or concealed (attested in he, el, en, tl, sw; absent or marginal in sa and qu)

**Attested in two to four languages:**
- Inchoative asymmetry (becoming true/good more lexicalized than becoming false/bad): qu, en, el, he
- Merger of factual rightness with moral justice under one root: he (צדק), zh (正), sw (haki)
- Broad positive evaluation term covering moral, practical, and epistemic domains: qu (allin), zh (好/allin-like coverage through 正), en (right, partially)
- Technical/specialized extensions of truth-domain roots into non-truth domains: zh (正 → mathematics, geometry, time), el (ορθ- → medicine, geometry, biology), en (false → music, statistics, law), sa (√mā → measurement, construction)

**Single-language observations:**
- Sanskrit's pramāṇa epistemological apparatus
- Quechua's obligatory evidential system on truth claims
- Greek's productive ψευδ- pseudo-X compound system
- Tagalog's tama na cessation/sufficiency extension
- Hebrew's א-מ-נ root merging truth, reliability, faithfulness, and belief
- Sanskrit's vyavahāra/paramārtha two-truths distinction with dedicated adverbial forms
- Swahili's Arabic loanword stratum as primary source of precision/correctness vocabulary
- Chinese's 实 root merging substantiality, actuality, and practical implementation

# CLCA Protocol v3.4 — P4a CONSTRUCTIONS
# Language: Sanskrit (sa)
# Transliteration standard: IAST

---

## 1. ATTRIBUTION

### Pattern
Nominal sentence: truth-domain adjective/noun as predicate nominative, with subject in nominative. Copula (*asti*, *bhavati*) is frequently omitted in classical prose; when present it follows the predicate. Word order is flexible; predicate-initial order is common in philosophical assertion. The neuter singular form (*satyam*, *yuktam*, *vāstavam*, *ucitam*) is the default predicative shape. The same form serves as an adverbial ("truly," "appropriately") when not predicated of a specific noun.

### Examples
- सत्यं परं धर्मः
  *satyaṃ paraṃ dharmaḥ*
  "Truth is the highest dharma." (Mahābhārata)

- असत्यं पापम्
  *asatyaṃ pāpam*
  "Untruth is sin / falsehood is a moral wrong." (Dharmaśāstra literature)

- सत्यम् एतत्
  *satyam etat*
  "this is true"

- युक्तम् एतत्
  *yuktam etat*
  "this is appropriate / this is well-reasoned"

- सत्यं ब्रह्म
  *satyaṃ brahma*
  "Brahman is truth / truth is Brahman"

- वेदः प्रमाणम् (अस्ति)
  *vedaḥ pramāṇam (asti)*
  "The Veda is a valid means of knowledge"

### Notes
The zero-copula nominal sentence is the unmarked form for truth-domain attribution in Sanskrit. The predicate typically precedes the subject in maxim-style assertions. When *bhavati* is present (e.g., *mithyā bhavati*, "it is false/illusory"), it follows the predicate and marks the construction as more explicitly propositional rather than gnomic.

Sanskrit nominal sentences regularly omit the copula; the neuter singular form (*satyam*, *yuktam*, *vāstavam*, *ucitam*) is the default predicative shape. The same form serves as an adverbial ("truly," "appropriately") when not predicated of a specific noun.

The copula *asti* is regularly absent in nominal predication; word order is flexible. The truth-domain item (*satya*, *pramāṇa*, etc.) appears in the nominative as predicate. This is the standard Sanskrit pattern for asserting that something instantiates a truth-domain property.

## 2. NEGATIVE ATTRIBUTION

### Pattern
Negative particle *na* placed immediately before the predicate nominal or before the copula. In zero-copula sentences, *na* + predicate nominal constitutes the full negation. With explicit copula: *na bhavati* or *na asti* (*nāsti*) follows the predicate. Alternatively, prefixal negation (*a-* / *an-*) is incorporated into the adjective itself. Both strategies are attested and often interchangeable.

### Examples
- भ्रमो न प्रमा
  *bhramo na pramā*
  "Error is not valid cognition." (Nyāya epistemological literature)

- प्रमाणं नास्ति
  *pramāṇaṃ nāsti*
  "There is no valid means of knowledge / there is no authority for this." (Standard dismissal formula in philosophical debate)

- न युक्तम् एतत्
  *na yuktam etat*
  "this is not appropriate / this is unreasonable"

- असत्यम् इदम्
  *asatyam idam*
  "this is untrue / this is a falsehood"

- हेत्वाभासो न हेतुः
  *hetvābhāso na hetuḥ*
  "A fallacious reason is not a (genuine) reason"

- न तत्र प्रमाणम् अस्ति
  *na tatra pramāṇam asti*
  "There is no valid means of knowledge for that"

### Notes
*Na* + zero-copula is the standard form for negating a truth-domain predication in philosophical argument. The existential negation *nāsti* (*na* + *asti*) is used when the claim is that no relevant epistemic ground exists at all, rather than that a given subject lacks a property.

Prefixal negation (*asatya*, *ayukta*, *anucita*, *aśuddha*, *adharma*) is highly productive across the truth-domain vocabulary and produces lexicalized antonyms; *na* + adjective is the syntactic alternative used when the negation is more locally scoped or emphatic.

Both constructions are attested as formulaic in philosophical debate. The first denies class membership (*X* is not a genuine *Y*); the second denies existence of a *pramāṇa* for a claim. Sanskrit also routinely handles negative attribution through prefixation (*apramāṇa*, *asatya*), making the periphrastic and morphological strategies functionally parallel.

## 3. ADNOMINAL MODIFICATION

### Pattern
Truth-domain adjective precedes the noun it modifies (standard Sanskrit adjective-noun order). The adjective agrees with the noun in gender, number, and case. Alternatively, a genitive phrase (*X-sya Y*) expresses "the Y of X." Compounds (adjective + noun written as a single unit) are the dominant form for stable collocations; syntactically free modification is used for contextual or contrastive predication. Sanskrit's compounding capacity means that adnominal modification very frequently takes the form of a *tatpuruṣa* or *karmadhāraya* compound rather than a free adjective-noun phrase.

### Examples
- यथार्थं ज्ञानम्
  *yathārthaṃ jñānam*
  "Accurate cognition / knowledge that is as the object is." (Epistemological literature, Nyāya and Mīmāṃsā)

- धर्मस्य तत्त्वं निहितं गुहायाम्
  *dharmasya tattvaṃ nihitaṃ guhāyām*
  "The true nature of dharma is hidden in a cave." (Mahābhārata, Vanaparvan)

- शुद्धः पाठः
  *śuddhaḥ pāṭhaḥ*
  "the correct / uncorrupted reading" (adjective agreeing with masculine noun)

- मिथ्याज्ञानम्
  *mithyājñānam*
  "false knowledge" (compound: *mithyā* + *jñāna*)

- मिथ्या ज्ञानम्
  *mithyā jñānam*
  "false / illusory cognition"

### Notes
Free adjectival modification (*yathārthaṃ jñānam*) is used when the truth-domain property is being predicated contextually or contrastively. The genitive construction (*dharmasya tattvaṃ*) is preferred when the modified noun is itself the topic and the truth-domain term characterizes a sub-aspect of it. Compounding (e.g., *satyavacana*, *mithyājñāna*) is the dominant form for lexicalized collocations and is treated in P2a rather than here.

Both strategies are productive and the compound form tends toward lexicalization (cf. P2a entries).

*Yathārtha* agrees with *jñāna* (neuter nominative/accusative). *Mithyā* functions here as an indeclinable adverb-adjective modifying the noun; it does not inflect. Adnominal modification is the primary syntactic role of items like *yathārtha*, *śuddha*, *vāstava*, and *mithyā* in epistemological prose.

## 4. BECOME / INCHOATIVE

### Pattern
Truth-domain noun or adjective as predicate with *bhavati* (becomes, comes to be, from root *bhū-*) or *jāyate* (arises, is produced, from root *jan-*), or *utpadyate* (is produced, from root *utpad-*). The inchoative reading ("comes to be X") is distinguished from the stative copular reading ("is X") by context and by the choice of verb: *bhavati* covers both, while *jāyate* and *utpadyate* more specifically mark production or arising.

### Examples
- प्रमा जायते
  *pramā jāyate*
  "Valid cognition arises / true knowledge is produced." (Nyāya epistemological literature, describing the outcome of employing a *pramāṇa*)

- मिथ्याज्ञानं निवर्तते
  *mithyājñānaṃ nivartate*
  "False knowledge ceases / erroneous cognition is removed." (Vedāntic literature, describing the soteriological removal of *mithyājñāna*)

- प्रमा उत्पद्यते
  *pramā utpadyate*
  "valid cognition arises / is produced"

- भ्रमः जायते
  *bhramaḥ jāyate*
  "error arises / mistaken cognition comes about"

- प्रमाणभूतः (भवति)
  *pramāṇabhūtaḥ (bhavati)*
  "He becomes / has become an authority"

### Notes
Sanskrit does not have a dedicated inchoative morpheme for this domain. The inchoative sense is expressed periphrastically through *jāyate* (arising/production) or through the cessation verb *nivartate* (ceases, is removed), which marks the inverse transition. The *pramā jāyate* construction specifically marks *pramā* as the output of an epistemic process rather than a standing state.

*Utpad-* and *jā-* are the standard inchoative verbs for cognitive events in Sanskrit epistemological literature; they take the arising cognition (valid or erroneous) as grammatical subject. The construction is symmetric: *pramā utpadyate* and *bhramaḥ jāyate* are structurally parallel and explicitly contrasted in epistemological texts.

The compound *pramāṇa-bhūta* (past participle of *bhū-*) lexicalizes the inchoative result ("one who has become an authority"), showing that Sanskrit often handles the inchoative through compounding rather than periphrasis.

## 5. CAUSATIVE

### Pattern
Two attested causative patterns in this domain:

**(a) Morphological causative:** Root + causative suffix *-aya-* + personal endings. Attested for *āp-* (to reach/attain) → causative *āpaya-*, yielding *satyāpana* (causing truth to be established, verification) and *satyāpita* (caused to be verified, past passive participle). Also attested: causative of *jñā-* ("to know") → *jñāpayati* ("causes to know / informs"); causative of *gam-* → *gamaya* ("cause to go"), as in the liturgical formula *asato mā sad gamaya*. This is the productive morphological causative in the administrative-legal register.

**(b) Periphrastic causative:** Truth-domain noun + *karaṇa* (making, from root *kṛ-*), yielding compounds such as *pramāṇīkaraṇa* (making into an authority, validation). Also *āpādaya-* (to bring about), taking the truth-domain nominal as object. This pattern is more common in modern Sanskrit-influenced administrative usage.

### Examples
- सत्यापन
  *satyāpana*
  "Verification, attestation" — the act of causing the truth of a statement to be established. (Legal and administrative Sanskrit)

- प्रमाणीकरण
  *pramāṇīkaraṇa*
  "Validation, authentication, certification" — making something into an authoritative/valid source. (Modern Sanskrit-influenced administrative language)

- दोषम् आपादयति
  *doṣam āpādayati*
  "brings about a fault / introduces a defect (in the opponent's position)"

- सत्यापयति
  *satyāpayati*
  "causes (something) to be verified / authenticates" (causative formation on *satya* via *āp-*)

- असतो मा सद्गमय
  *asato mā sad gamaya*
  "Lead me from the unreal to the real" (causative imperative of *gam-*: *gamaya* = "cause to go")

### Notes
The morphological causative on truth-domain roots is not broadly productive in classical philosophical Sanskrit; the domain's causative meanings are largely expressed through nominal compounds with *karaṇa* or through the *satyā-* + causative-of-*āp-* pattern. No common causative form of *satya*, *pramā*, or *dharma* as bare roots is attested in the philosophical literature.

A direct morphological causative of a truth-predicate adjective (e.g., *\*satyayati*, "makes true") is not a productive pattern in Sanskrit; instead, causation in this domain is expressed either through the *satyāpana* / *satyāpita* formation (P2a item 1) or through periphrastic constructions with *āpādaya-* and a nominal object. The *pramāṇīkaraṇa* formation (P2a item 4) represents a further periphrastic strategy.

The causative of *gam-* (*gamaya*) in the *Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad* prayer is a fixed liturgical formula. *Satyāpayati* is a denominative causative ("to make true / to verify"), productive in legal and administrative contexts. Sanskrit causatives are morphological, not periphrastic; the pattern is fully regular.

## 6. ABSTRACT NOMINALIZATION

### Pattern
Two productive suffixes:

**(a)** *-tā* (feminine abstract): attaches to adjectives and nominals to yield abstract nouns of property. Fully productive across the domain. More common in general philosophical prose.

**(b)** *-tva* (neuter abstract): parallel to *-tā*; more frequent in technical philosophical argument, particularly in Navya-Nyāya technical contexts. Both suffixes are freely applicable; both forms are often attested for the same base.

The resulting nominals function as subjects and objects of predication in philosophical argument, naming the property at issue rather than a particular instance of it.

### Examples
- सत्यता / सत्यत्व
  *satyatā / satyatva*
  "Truthfulness, the property of being true." (Philosophical and ethical discourse)

- मिथ्यात्व
  *mithyātva*
  "The property of being *mithyā* / illusoriness." (Advaita Vedānta; the abstract property that is the central object of ontological debate in that tradition)

- सत्यता
  *satyatā*
  "the property of being true, truthfulness"

- मिथ्यात्वम्
  *mithyātvam*
  "the property of being illusory / falsity" (Advaita technical term)

### Notes
Both *-tā* and *-tva* are fully productive on truth-domain bases: *satyatā/satyatva*, *yathārthatā/yathārthatva*, *tathyatā/tathyatva*, *vāstavatā/vāstavatva*, *mithyātva*, *bhramatva*, *prāmāṇya* (the last formed with *-ya* after vṛddhi, a third pattern specific to *pramāṇa*-family terms). The choice between *-tā* and *-tva* is partly stylistic and partly school-conventional; no consistent semantic distinction between them is supported by the data.

The *-tā* / *-tva* pair is the most productive derivational pattern across the entire truth-domain vocabulary (attested on *satya*, *yathārtha*, *tathya*, *pramā*, *dharma*, *yukta*, *ucita*, *sādhu*, *vāstava*, *paramārtha*, *tattva*, and others); *-tva* forms tend to appear in formal definitional contexts where the property is treated as a logical object of analysis.

Abstract nominalizations are the primary means by which Sanskrit expresses truth-domain properties as objects of predication and philosophical argument. *Mithyātva* is a technical Advaita Vedānta term with a specific ontological meaning (neither real nor unreal) beyond simple nominalization.

## 7. AGENTIVE NOMINALIZATION

### Pattern
Suffix *-in* (possessive/agentive, yielding "one who has/does X") or compound with *vādin* (one who speaks/asserts, from root *vad-*) or *darśin* (one who sees, from root *dṛś-*) or *bhāṣin* (from root *bhāṣ-*, "to speak") or *-jña* ("one who knows X"). Also agent suffix *-tṛ* (primary agent noun). The *-vādin* compound is the dominant pattern for truth-domain agents in ethical and epistemological literature.

### Examples
- सत्यवादिन्
  *satyavādin*
  "One who speaks truth, a truth-teller." (Epic and Dharmaśāstra literature; used as an epithet of virtuous figures and as a category in discussions of reliable testimony)

- असत्यवादिन्
  *asatyavādin*
  "One who speaks falsely, a liar." (Ethical and legal literature; used to characterize an unreliable speaker or witness)

- अनृतभाषिन्
  *anṛta-bhāṣin*
  "one who speaks falsehood / a liar" (*bhāṣin* from root *bhāṣ-*, "to speak")

### Notes
The *-vādin* compound is the standard agentive form for truth-domain agents in Sanskrit: *satyavādin*, *asatyavādin*, *mithyāvādin*, *yathārthavādin*. The suffix *-in* is used for the logical subject (*dharmin*, "the locus of a property") but not productively for truth-domain agents in the ethical-epistemological sense. The *-vādin* pattern is asymmetric: positive (*satyavādin*) and negative (*asatyavādin*, *mithyāvādin*) forms are both well-attested, but no agentive form for the knower-of-truth in the epistemological sense is built on *-vādin*; that role is filled by *pramātṛ* (the knowing subject, from root *mā-* with agent suffix *-tṛ*).

The agent suffix *-tṛ* generates *pramātṛ* (the knowing subject, one who has valid cognition), a technical epistemological term forming part of the standard triad *pramātṛ / pramāṇa / prameya*.

*Satyavādin* and *anṛta-bhāṣin* are the standard agentive nominalizations for truth-teller and liar respectively in *Dharmaśāstra* witness-evaluation contexts. The two suffixes (*-vādin*, *-bhāṣin*) are near-synonymous in this function; *-vādin* is more frequent in the *satya*-family, *-bhāṣin* in the *anṛta*-family. A third pattern, *-jña* ("one who knows X": *ṛta-jña*, "knower of cosmic order"), produces agentive nominalizations in the epistemic rather than speech domain.

## 8. PRESCRIPTIVE / INJUNCTIVE CONSTRUCTION

### Pattern
Truth-domain noun in accusative + verb of speaking (*vad-*, *brū-*) or conduct (*car-*) in the optative (*-yāt* endings) or imperative, or gerundive (*-tavya*, *-anīya*). The optative expresses normative obligation ("one should"); the imperative expresses direct command; the gerundive expresses stronger obligation. Negative prescriptions use *na* + optative or *na* + gerundive. This construction type is so frequent and structurally distinctive in the Sanskrit truth domain that it warrants separate treatment.

### Examples
- सत्यं ब्रूयात्
  *satyaṃ brūyāt*
  "One should speak the truth." (Manusmṛti 4.138 and Dharmaśāstra literature generally; the standard prescriptive formula for the duty of truthful speech)

- असत्यं न वदेत्
  *asatyaṃ na vadet*
  "One should not speak untruth." (Dharmaśāstra; the standard prohibitive formula, parallel in structure to the positive injunction)

- सत्यं वद
  *satyaṃ vada*
  "speak the truth" (imperative injunction; Taittirīya Upaniṣad 1.11.1)

- सत्यात् न प्रमदितव्यम्
  *satyāt na pramaditavyam*
  "One must not deviate from truth" (gerundive with negation)

### Notes
The optative of *brū-* (*brūyāt*) and *vad-* (*vadet*) with a truth-domain accusative is the canonical syntactic form for ethical prescriptions about speech in Sanskrit Dharmaśāstra. The imperative form (*satyaṃ vada*, Taittirīya Upaniṣad 1.11.1) is used in foundational injunctive contexts. This construction type is specific to the speech-evaluating and conduct-evaluating groups (P3 Groups 3 and 6) and does not extend to the epistemological or ontological groups.

The optative expresses normative expectation; the gerundive expresses stronger obligation. Both are formulaic in the attested literature (*Taittirīya Upaniṣad* 1.11.1, *Manusmṛti* 4.138). The pattern is specific to the truth-domain in that the accusative of *satya* / *anṛta* with verbs of speaking (*vad-*, *brū-*, *bhāṣ-*) constitutes a recognized formulaic type across multiple text genres.

## 9. ABLATIVE DEVIATION CONSTRUCTION

### Pattern
Truth-domain noun in ablative (*-āt* ending) + negative verb of motion or deviation (*na calati*, *na pracalati*, *na cyavate*): "does not deviate/move from X." The ablative marks the standard from which deviation is negated. The positive counterpart (*cyuta*, "fallen from," past passive participle + ablative) expresses deviation. This construction is productive across ethical and aesthetic vocabulary in the truth domain.

### Examples
- सत्यात् न प्रचलति
  *satyāt na pracalati*
  "Does not deviate from truth / does not swerve from truth." (Epic literature; characterization of a person of unwavering truthfulness)

- औचित्यात् च्युतम्
  *aucityāt cyutam*
  "Fallen from propriety / deviated from what is fitting." (Sanskrit aesthetic literature; standard formula for a composition or act that has violated the standard of fittingness)

### Notes
The ablative + *na calati/pracalati* construction is the standard Sanskrit formula for expressing steadfast adherence to a normative standard by negating departure from it. The inverse — ablative + *cyuta* (fallen from) — expresses the positive fact of deviation. Both patterns are productive across the conduct-evaluating (P3 Group 6) and fittingness-evaluating (P3 Group 10) domains. The construction does not appear in the epistemological groups, where deviation from a standard is expressed differently (e.g., *pramāṇaviruddha*).

## 10. DISCOURSE-MARKING ADVERBIAL

### Pattern
Ablative/adverbial form of a truth-domain nominal (suffix *-taḥ* or *-ena*) used sentence-initially or clause-initially as a discourse marker, signalling that the following proposition represents a deeper, corrective, or ultimate-level assessment. Structurally distinct from simple adverbial use of a neuter accusative (as in *satyam*, *yuktam*): the *-taḥ* forms function as level-of-analysis markers in philosophical prose, explicitly locating the following claim within a framework of degrees of reality or depth of analysis.

### Examples
- वस्तुतस्तु
  *vastutastu*
  "but in reality / but from the standpoint of the real thing" (introduces corrective claim)

- परमार्थतः न विद्यते
  *paramārthataḥ na vidyate*
  "it does not exist at the ultimate level"

- वस्तुतः
  *vastutaḥ*
  "in reality / as a matter of fact" (modifying the entire proposition)

- तत्त्वतः
  *tattvataḥ*
  "in truth / according to the real nature"

### Notes
This pattern is pervasive in Sanskrit commentary literature and constitutes a recognizable discourse genre feature (cf. P3 Group 9).

These adverbial forms function as propositional operators, asserting that the accompanying claim corresponds to the actual state of affairs rather than to appearance or convention. They are widely used in Sanskrit commentarial prose as a conventional formula for asserting factual or essential grounding. No parallel periphrastic construction ("it is true that…") is commonly used for this function; the adverbial form is the standard device.

## 11. OATH / PERFORMATIVE FORMULA

### Pattern
Truth-domain nominal in the instrumental case (*-ena*) with a verb of swearing or pledging (*śap-*, *pratijñā-*), or in the accusative as the object of a verb of speaking (*vad-*, *brū-*) in the imperative, constituting a performative or quasi-performative speech act.

### Examples
- सत्येन शपामि
  *satyena śapāmi*
  "I swear by truth" (oath formula; instrumental marks truth as the ground of the oath)

- सत्यं वद
  *satyaṃ vada*
  "speak the truth" (imperative injunction; accusative marks truth as the content of the commanded speech act)

### Notes
These constructions are attested as fixed formulas in Vedic, legal, and epic literature and represent a pragmatically distinct use of truth-domain vocabulary: rather than attributing a truth-property to a proposition, they invoke truth as the ground or content of a speech act. The instrumental *satyena* and the accusative *satyam* occupy different syntactic roles but both participate in this performative pattern.

## 12. CONCESSIVE CONSTRUCTION

### Pattern
Adverbially-used *satyam* (neuter accusative) followed by an adversative particle (*tu*, *tathāpi*, *kintu*), conceding a point before introducing a qualification or counter-consideration. Functions as a discourse-level concession marker.

### Examples
- सत्यं तु
  *satyaṃ tu*
  "true, but… / granted, yet…"

- सत्यं तथापि
  *satyaṃ tathāpi*
  "true, yet even so…"

### Notes
This construction is structurally parallel to the English "granted, but…" or "true enough, however…" and is a recognizable device in Sanskrit philosophical argumentation for acknowledging an opponent's point before responding. *Satyam* here does not predicate truth of a specific proposition but functions as a concessive discourse particle; it is the only truth-domain term that participates in this pattern with this frequency.

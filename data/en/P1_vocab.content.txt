# CLCA Protocol v3.2 — P1 CORE VOCABULARY COLLECTION
# Language: English (en)

## Lexical Items

1. **true**
   - Literal meaning: From Old English trēowe "faithful, trustworthy"
   - Typical translations: véridique (French), wahr (German), verdadero (Spanish)
   - Usage: Used to evaluate statements as corresponding to reality or facts ("that's true"), to describe genuine instances of a category ("a true friend"), and to assess accuracy of representations. Commonly appears in both everyday and philosophical discourse.
   - [CORE]

2. **false**
   - Literal meaning: From Latin falsus "deceived, erroneous"
   - Typical translations: faux (French), falsch (German), falso (Spanish)
   - Usage: Evaluates statements as not corresponding to reality or facts, describes counterfeit or deceptive representations. Used in logical, moral, and everyday contexts to mark inaccuracy or deception.
   - [CORE]

3. **real**
   - Literal meaning: From Latin realis "actual, relating to things"
   - Typical translations: réel (French), wirklich (German), real (Spanish)
   - Usage: Distinguishes genuine existence from appearance, pretense, or imagination ("is this real?"). Applied to objects, situations, and persons to evaluate authenticity or actual existence versus simulation.
   - [CORE]

4. **genuine**
   - Literal meaning: From Latin genuinus "native, natural, innate"
   - Typical translations: authentique (French), echt (German), genuino (Spanish)
   - Usage: Evaluates objects or persons as authentic rather than counterfeit, imitation, or affected. Often used for artifacts, emotions, and character assessments where authenticity is at issue.
   - [CORE]

5. **authentic**
   - Literal meaning: From Greek authentikos "original, primary, at first hand"
   - Typical translations: authentique (French), authentisch (German), auténtico (Spanish)
   - Usage: Assesses whether something is of undisputed origin or authorship, not copied or false. Used particularly for documents, artworks, experiences, and cultural practices.
   - [CORE]

6. **accurate**
   - Literal meaning: From Latin accuratus "done with care," from ad- + cura "care"
   - Typical translations: précis (French), genau (German), preciso (Spanish)
   - Usage: Evaluates representations, measurements, or statements for precision and correctness in detail. Commonly applied to descriptions, calculations, and reproductions where exactness matters.
   - [CORE]

7. **correct**
   - Literal meaning: From Latin correctus "made straight, amended"
   - Typical translations: correct (French), richtig (German), correcto (Spanish)
   - Usage: Marks conformity to standards, rules, or facts across diverse domains including grammar, behavior, answers, and procedures. Implies alignment with an established norm or truth.
   - [CORE]

8. **right**
   - Literal meaning: From Old English riht "just, good, proper, straight"
   - Typical translations: juste/bon (French), richtig/recht (German), correcto/justo (Spanish)
   - Usage: Evaluates correctness of answers, moral propriety of actions, or appropriate fit of situations. Highly polysemous, spanning epistemic, moral, and practical domains.
   - [CORE]

9. **wrong**
   - Literal meaning: From Old English wrang "crooked, twisted, unjust"
   - Typical translations: faux/mauvais (French), falsch/unrecht (German), incorrecto/malo (Spanish)
   - Usage: Marks incorrectness of statements, moral impropriety of conduct, or malfunction. Oppositional to "right" across epistemic, moral, and practical contexts.
   - [CORE]

10. **valid**
    - Literal meaning: From Latin validus "strong, effective"
    - Typical translations: valide (French), gültig (German), válido (Spanish)
    - Usage: Evaluates logical soundness of arguments, legal force of documents, or legitimacy of claims. Technical usage in logic and law, but also in everyday assessment of reasoning.
    - [CORE]

11. **sound**
    - Literal meaning: From Old English sund "healthy, whole"
    - Typical translations: solide/sain (French), vernünftig (German), sólido (Spanish)
    - Usage: Assesses reasoning, judgment, or arguments as well-founded and reliable. Also evaluates physical integrity, but in epistemic contexts marks thorough correctness.
    - [CORE]

12. **reliable**
    - Literal meaning: From rely + -able; rely from Latin religare "to bind"
    - Typical translations: fiable (French), zuverlässig (German), fiable (Spanish)
    - Usage: Evaluates sources, persons, or mechanisms as consistently trustworthy and dependable. Applied to testimony, instruments, and character where consistency matters.
    - [CORE]

13. **trustworthy**
    - Literal meaning: Compound: trust + worthy "deserving of trust"
    - Typical translations: digne de confiance (French), vertrauenswürdig (German), digno de confianza (Spanish)
    - Usage: Assesses persons, sources, or institutions as meriting confidence and belief. Emphasizes moral character and reliability in interpersonal and institutional contexts.
    - [CORE]

14. **honest**
    - Literal meaning: From Latin honestus "honorable, respectable"
    - Typical translations: honnête (French), ehrlich (German), honesto (Spanish)
    - Usage: Evaluates persons or statements as free from deceit or fraud, truthful in representation. Applied to character assessment and evaluation of testimony or claims.
    - [CORE]

15. **truthful**
    - Literal meaning: Compound: truth + -ful "full of truth"
    - Typical translations: véridique (French), wahrhaftig (German), veraz (Spanish)
    - Usage: Describes persons as habitually telling the truth or statements as conforming to facts. Emphasizes both accuracy and sincerity in representation.
    - [CORE]

16. **faithful**
    - Literal meaning: Compound: faith + -ful "full of faith"
    - Typical translations: fidèle (French), treu (German), fiel (Spanish)
    - Usage: Evaluates representations as accurately corresponding to originals (translations, copies) or persons as loyal and constant. Spans both resemblance and moral loyalty.
    - [CORE]

17. **fake**
    - Literal meaning: Origin uncertain, possibly from German fegen "to polish, sweep"
    - Typical translations: faux (French), gefälscht (German), falso (Spanish)
    - Usage: Marks objects or representations as counterfeit, not genuine, deliberately made to deceive. Commonly used for artifacts, credentials, and affected behavior.
    - [CORE]

18. **counterfeit**
    - Literal meaning: From Old French contrefait "imitated," from contre- "against" + fait "made"
    - Typical translations: contrefait (French), gefälscht (German), falsificado (Spanish)
    - Usage: Evaluates objects, especially currency and documents, as fraudulent imitations made to deceive. More formal and specific than "fake," often with legal implications.
    - [CORE]

19. **bogus**
    - Literal meaning: Origin uncertain, possibly from bogus apparatus (counterfeit coin maker)
    - Typical translations: faux/bidon (French), unecht (German), falso (Spanish)
    - Usage: Marks claims, objects, or credentials as spurious or fraudulent, not genuine. Informal register, commonly used for dubious claims or fake credentials.
    - [CORE]

20. **spurious**
    - Literal meaning: From Latin spurius "illegitimate, false"
    - Typical translations: fallacieux (French), unecht (German), espurio (Spanish)
    - Usage: Evaluates arguments, claims, or objects as superficially plausible but actually false or illegitimate. More formal register, often in academic or technical contexts.
    - [CORE]

21. **veridical**
    - Literal meaning: From Latin veridicus, from verus "true" + dicere "to say"
    - Typical translations: véridique (French), wahrheitsgemäß (German), verídico (Spanish)
    - Usage: Technical term in philosophy and psychology for perceptions or reports that accurately correspond to reality. Primarily used in academic and specialized discourse.
    - [MARGINAL]

22. **fallacious**
    - Literal meaning: From Latin fallacia "deception," from fallere "to deceive"
    - Typical translations: fallacieux (French), trügerisch (German), falaz (Spanish)
    - Usage: Evaluates reasoning or arguments as logically flawed or deceptive. Technical usage in logic and rhetoric, though also in educated general discourse.
    - [CORE]

23. **erroneous**
    - Literal meaning: From Latin erroneus "wandering, straying," from errare "to wander, err"
    - Typical translations: erroné (French), irrig (German), erróneo (Spanish)
    - Usage: Marks beliefs, statements, or conclusions as mistaken or incorrect. More formal than "wrong," commonly in academic, legal, and technical writing.
    - [CORE]

24. **apt**
    - Literal meaning: From Latin aptus "fitted, suitable"
    - Typical translations: approprié/juste (French), passend (German), apropiado (Spanish)
    - Usage: Evaluates descriptions, comparisons, or responses as particularly fitting or appropriate to the situation. Emphasizes suitability and accuracy of fit.
    - [CORE]

25. **fitting**
    - Literal meaning: From fit + -ing; fit possibly from Middle English fitten "to be suitable"
    - Typical translations: approprié (French), passend (German), apropiado (Spanish)
    - Usage: Assesses actions, responses, or descriptions as appropriate or suitable to circumstances. Emphasizes propriety and contextual appropriateness.
    - [CORE]

26. **proper**
    - Literal meaning: From Latin proprius "one's own, particular"
    - Typical translations: propre/approprié (French), richtig/eigen (German), propio/apropiado (Spanish)
    - Usage: Evaluates conformity to standards of correctness, appropriateness, or belonging. Used across domains from grammar to etiquette to categorization.
    - [CORE]

27. **legitimate**
    - Literal meaning: From Latin legitimatus, from legitimus "lawful," from lex "law"
    - Typical translations: légitime (French), legitim (German), legítimo (Spanish)
    - Usage: Assesses claims, authority, or status as conforming to law, rules, or accepted standards. Spans legal, political, and general evaluative contexts.
    - [CORE]

28. **bona fide**
    - Literal meaning: Latin "in good faith," from bonus "good" + fides "faith"
    - Typical translations: de bonne foi (French), gutgläubig (German), de buena fe (Spanish)
    - Usage: Fixed Latin expression used in English to evaluate offers, credentials, or persons as genuine and made in good faith. Common in legal and business contexts.
    - [CORE]

29. **veritable**
    - Literal meaning: From Old French veritable, from verité "truth"
    - Typical translations: véritable (French), wahrhaft (German), verdadero (Spanish)
    - Usage: Emphasizes that something truly is what it is called, often used for rhetorical emphasis ("a veritable feast"). Somewhat formal or literary register.
    - [CORE]

30. **so-called**
    - Literal meaning: Compound: so + called "named thus"
    - Typical translations: soi-disant (French), sogenannt (German), llamado/supuesto (Spanish)
    - Usage: Fixed expression marking skepticism about whether something deserves the name or description applied to it. Signals doubt about genuineness or appropriateness of designation.
    - [CORE]

# G1 — Cross-Language Summary of Observed Semantic Organization

---

## 1. Language Inventory

- **zh (Chinese)** — Morphologically isolating language with rich compounding and fixed-expression vocabulary; truth-domain organized around roots 真 (zhēn), 实 (shí), 假 (jiǎ), 对 (duì), 错 (cuò), with extensive idiomatic and four-character expressions.
- **he (Hebrew)** — Semitic language with root-and-pattern morphology; truth-domain organized around roots ʾ-m-t (truth), sh-k-r (falsehood), n-k-w-n (correctness), ts-d-k (justice/rightness), with productive prepositional adverbials and zero-copula attribution.
- **sa (Sanskrit)** — Classical Indo-European language with highly productive affixal morphology; truth-domain organized around satya/asatya, ṛta/anṛta, dharma/adharma, pramāṇa (valid means of knowledge), with extensive technical epistemological vocabulary.
- **el (Greek)** — Modern Indo-European language with productive prefixation (ψευδο-, ορθο-) and suffixation; truth-domain organized around αλήθεια (truth), ψέμα (lie), σωστός (correct), λάθος (error), with distinct legal/administrative validity vocabulary.
- **en (English)** — Germanic language with mixed morphological strategies (prefixation, suffixation, compounding); truth-domain organized around true/false, right/wrong, correct/incorrect, genuine/fake, with extensive phrasal and idiomatic constructions.
- **tl (Tagalog)** — Austronesian language with circumfix-based morphology and focus-marking system; truth-domain organized around totoo (true), tama (correct), tapat (honest), mali (wrong), with particle-based discourse marking and aspectual verbal morphology.
- **sw (Swahili)** — Bantu language with noun-class agreement and productive prefixation; truth-domain organized around kweli (truth), sahihi (correct, from Arabic), haki (right/justice, from Arabic), uongo (falsehood), with extensive Arabic loanword integration.
- **qu (Quechua)** — Agglutinative language with obligatory evidential suffixes; truth-domain organized around chiqaq (true), allin (good), llulla (false/lie), pantay (to err), with grammaticalized evidentiality and productive inchoative/causative derivation.

## 2. Per-Language Semantic Sketch

### zh (Chinese)

**Main lexical distinctions and overlaps (F1–F3):** The truth domain is organized around two primary root morphemes: 真 (zhēn, true/genuine) and 实 (shí, actual/solid), which overlap substantially but are not identical. 真 emphasizes genuineness and authenticity; 实 emphasizes actuality and substantiality. Their antonyms 假 (jiǎ, false/fake) and 虚 (xū, empty/unreal) similarly overlap. A separate pair 对/错 (correct/wrong) handles correctness evaluation, particularly in educational and practical contexts. The F3 analysis identifies 20 semantic groups, including distinct groups for factual correspondence, correctness in judgment, precision/accuracy, authenticity, sincerity, deception, reliability, objectivity/subjectivity, substance vs. appearance, practical implementation, physical solidity, character traits, hypothetical reasoning, artistic realism, discourse markers, evidential processes, intensification, ambiguity, concrete objects, and fulfillment. Several items (真理, 真知, 假期) are ungrouped due to specialized or semantically distant usage.

**Derivational/morphological asymmetries:** Chinese uses suffixes -性 (-xìng, quality), -度 (-dù, degree), -化 (-huà, -ify), -者 (-zhě, agent), and -地 (-de, adverbial) productively. Resultative verb compounds (证实, 核实, 落实) are highly productive with 实 as complement. Reduplication (AABB pattern: 真真实实, 老老实实) intensifies meaning but is lexically restricted. No dedicated morphological negation prefix; negation uses 不 or 没有 analytically.

**Constructional patterns (F4):** The copula 是 with 的 is the standard attributive construction; bare adjective predicates also occur. Resultative complements (说对了, 做错了) are a fundamental construction type. Potential complements (说得对/说不对) systematically derive ability/possibility readings. Discourse markers (说实话, 实际上, 事实上) are highly grammaticalized. Conformity constructions (符合事实, 与事实不符) are productive in formal registers.

**Oppositional structures (F5):** The primary lexical opposition is 真/假. Secondary lexical oppositions include 对/错, 实/虚, 诚实/欺骗. Morphological negation (不真实, 不可靠, 不准确) is productive. Many fixed expressions contain internal reinforcing combinations (真凭实据, 弄虚作假) rather than oppositions. Several four-character expressions contain internal oppositions (以假乱真, 去伪存真).

### he (Hebrew)

**Main lexical distinctions and overlaps (F1–F3):** The truth domain is organized around several distinct conceptual clusters. The root ʾ-m-t (emet) covers truth as both propositional accuracy and moral faithfulness. The root n-k-w-n (nakhon) covers correctness and accuracy, with a secondary meaning of readiness/preparedness (nekhonut). The root ts-d-k covers both epistemic rightness (tsodek = "you're right") and moral/legal justice (tsedek, tsedakah). The root ʾ-m-n covers reliability (amin), trustworthiness (meheyман), faith (emunah), and faithfulness (ne'eman). F3 identifies 11 semantic groups: truth-falsehood opposition, correctness assessment, reliability/trustworthiness, genuineness/authenticity, rightness/justice, honesty/sincerity, certainty/doubt, error/mistake, reality/actuality, affirmation/negation particles, and resemblance/appearance. Several items are ungrouped due to dual meanings or peripheral status.

**Derivational/morphological asymmetries:** The -ut suffix productively derives abstract nouns (aminut, meheymanut, vada'ut, khenut). The be- prepositional prefix productively derives manner adverbials (be-emet, be-tsedek, be-yosher, be-khenut). Agentive -an suffix (shakran "liar," safkan "skeptic") is productive for negative agents but less so for positive agents. The root ts-d-k shows extensive derivation (tsadik, tsedakah, tsidkani, hitsdik, hatsdakah) but the charitable giving sense of tsedakah has diverged semantically from the truth/justice cluster.

**Constructional patterns (F4):** Zero copula in present tense is the default attribution pattern. The complementizer she- introduces propositional complements with truth predicates (nakhon she-, vadai she-, batuakh she-). Dative experiencer constructions (domeh li she-, nir'eh li she-) are the standard hedging strategy. Existential constructions (yesh safek, ein safek) are productive for certainty/doubt. Prepositional adverbials (be-emet, be-vadai, be-diyuk) are highly frequent. Revelation verbs (hitgalah, hitbarar) are preferred over inchoative "become true" constructions.

**Oppositional structures (F5):** Standard negation uses lo (colloquial) or ein/eino (formal). The prefix bilti- creates lexicalized negatives (bilti amin). Several partial oppositions are noted where the negative term is not attested (avel for injustice, boged for traitor, tsavu'a for hypocrite). The opposition between bkhavanah/be-ta'ut (intentionally/by mistake) is a constructional opposition using parallel prepositional phrases.

### sa (Sanskrit)

**Main lexical distinctions and overlaps (F1–F3):** Sanskrit shows the most elaborate technical epistemological vocabulary of all languages in the corpus. The pramāṇa system (valid means of knowledge) constitutes a distinct semantic cluster with no clear parallel in other languages: pramāṇa (valid means), pramā (valid cognition), pramātā (knower), prameya (object of knowledge), prāmāṇya (validity), and specific types (pratyakṣa-pramāṇa, anumāna-pramāṇa, śabda-pramāṇa, etc.). The root sat- (being/truth) covers both ontological existence and moral goodness, creating extensive overlap between truth, existence, and virtue. The root ṛta covers Vedic cosmic order and is largely archaic in classical usage. F3 identifies 13 semantic groups: factual correspondence, epistemic validation, cognitive error, deliberate falsehood, moral/cosmic order, logical/rational propriety, essential nature/reality, appearance/semblance, correctness/completeness, negation terms, positive moral qualities, Vedic cosmic order terms, and measurement/standard. Several highly polysemous terms (satya, nyāya, rūpa, sādhu) resist grouping.

**Derivational/morphological asymmetries:** The -tā and -tva suffixes both derive abstract nouns (satyatā/satyatva), with -tva preferred in technical philosophical contexts. The -tas ablative suffix derives adverbials (tattvataḥ, vastutaḥ). The -ika suffix with vṛddhi derives adjectives from nouns (dhārmika, tāttvika, prāmāṇika). Negative prefix a- is highly productive (asatya, adharma, ayukta, apramāṇa, apramā). Causative -ish- is productive for verification/validation verbs (hakikisha in Swahili shows parallel borrowing). Positive evaluation terms show richer semantic differentiation and more productive compounding than their negative counterparts.

**Constructional patterns (F4):** Copula omission in present tense is standard. The instrumental case (pramāṇena, satyena, dharmeṇa) productively expresses means or manner. The ablative (dharmāt na chalitavyam) expresses deviation from standards. Gerundive constructions (satyaṃ vaktavyam, mṛṣā na vaktavyam) express obligation. Dvandva dual compounds (dharmādharmau, satyāsatye) present paired opposites. The emphatic particle eva (satyam eva jayate) is highly productive. Inchoative constructions with √bhū (bhavati, bhūtam) are standard for change-of-state.

**Oppositional structures (F5):** Morphological negation with a- is the primary opposition-forming strategy (satya/asatya, dharma/adharma, yukta/ayukta, pramāṇa/apramāṇa). Several constructional oppositions involve contrasting instrumental expressions (tattvena vs. ābhāsatayā, vastutaḥ vs. ābhāsamātram). Several implied oppositions are noted as uncertain because the negative term is not explicitly attested (anupapatti, asattva).

### el (Greek)

**Main lexical distinctions and overlaps (F1–F3):** Greek shows the most productive prefix-based compounding in the corpus. The prefix ψευδο- (false) is exceptionally productive, generating compounds across scientific (ψευδοεπιστήμη, ψευδοπόδιο), legal (ψευδομαρτυρία, ψευδορκία), religious (ψευδοπροφήτης), and everyday (ψευδαίσθηση) domains. The prefix ορθο- (correct/straight) is similarly productive across educational (ορθογραφία), religious (ορθόδοξος), medical (ορθοπεδική), and logical (ορθολογισμός) domains. F3 identifies 12 semantic groups: propositional correspondence, authenticity/genuineness, procedural/normative correctness, legal/administrative validity, sincerity/intentional deception, reliability/trustworthiness, fabrication/construction, justice/fairness, appearance/reality distinctions, scientific/technical pseudo-terms, rationality/logical correctness, and physical straightness/uprightness. The ορθ- root shows polysemy between physical straightness and abstract correctness.

**Derivational/morphological asymmetries:** The -ότητα suffix productively derives abstract nouns from adjectives (αληθινότητα, αυθεντικότητα, ορθότητα, σωστότητα). The -ώνω suffix derives causative verbs (ακυρώνω, βεβαιώνω). Agentive nominalization is productive for negative agents (ψεύτης, ψεύτρα, ψευταράς, απατεώνας) but no common agentive for "truth-teller" exists in everyday use (αληθολόγος is marginal/literary). The diminutive -άκι (ψεματάκι, λαθάκι) productively minimizes severity of lies and errors.

**Constructional patterns (F4):** The copula είναι is standard for attribution; γίνομαι (become) is used for inchoative constructions but is unnatural with core truth terms (αλήθεια, ψέμα) while natural with correctness terms (σωστός) and legal terms (έγκυρος). The light verb λέω (say/tell) is obligatory for truth-telling and lying constructions (λέω την αλήθεια, λέω ψέματα); no single-word verb for "lie" exists in standard usage. The light verb κάνω (make/do) is obligatory for error constructions (κάνω λάθος). Color-modified lie constructions (άσπρο ψέμα, μαύρο ψέμα, κόκκινο ψέμα) form a scalar severity system.

**Oppositional structures (F5):** Standard negation uses δεν (before copula) or inherently negative lexical items. Several asymmetric oppositions are noted: ψεύτης (liar) has no common positive counterpart; ψευδής συναγερμός (false alarm) has no positive counterpart; αληθοφανής (apparently true) has no attested opposite. The opposition between ακυρώνω (invalidate) and βεβαιώνω (certify) is a lexical opposition between causative verbs.

### en (English)

**Main lexical distinctions and overlaps (F1–F3):** English shows the most extensive compound and phrasal vocabulary in the corpus. The truth domain is organized around several overlapping clusters: propositional truth (true/false), correctness (correct/incorrect, right/wrong), authenticity (genuine/fake, authentic/inauthentic), validity (valid/invalid, sound/unsound), reliability (reliable/unreliable), and sincerity (honest/dishonest, sincere/insincere). The term "right" is notably polysemous across epistemic correctness, moral rightness, and practical appropriateness. F3 identifies 15 semantic groups including a distinct group for technical testing/diagnostic terms (false positive, false negative) and a group for realization/actualization (come true, realize). The "false" compounds form a large specialized group (false alarm, false start, false dawn, false economy, false flag, etc.) with distinct meanings not derivable from "false" alone.

**Derivational/morphological asymmetries:** English shows the most varied negation strategies: un- (untrue, unreliable, unfaithful), in-/im-/il- (incorrect, inaccurate, illegitimate), dis- (dishonest), and analytic "not" (not true, not correct). The -ity/-ness/-cy/-th suffixes derive abstract nouns with distribution conditioned by etymology (Latin-derived adjectives take -ity; Germanic roots take -ness or -th). The -ify suffix (falsify, verify) derives causative verbs. The -able suffix (falsifiable, verifiable) derives possibility adjectives. The term "falsifiable/falsifiability" is marked as marginal, reflecting its restriction to philosophy of science discourse.

**Constructional patterns (F4):** The copula "be" is standard for attribution. The verb "become" is productive for inchoative constructions with most truth-domain adjectives but "come true" is a specialized idiom restricted to predictions, wishes, and dreams. Causative constructions show a rich lexical inventory: make/render (analytic), validate, authenticate, verify, confirm, prove, correct, legitimize, realize, disprove, refute, debunk, expose (lexical). Phrasal verbs (go wrong, get wrong, hold true, ring true/false, stand corrected) are numerous. Discourse markers (to be honest, truth be told, in truth, to tell the truth) are highly grammaticalized.

**Oppositional structures (F5):** English shows the most systematic morphological negation in the corpus, with productive un-, in-/il-/im-, and dis- prefixes applying across adjectives and their derived nominals and adverbials. Several asymmetric oppositions are noted: "authenticate" has no morphological opposite (*inauthenticate); "realize" has no morphological opposite (*unrealize); "bona fide" has no productive negation. The opposition between true positive/false positive and true negative/false negative is a specialized constructional opposition in diagnostic contexts.

### tl (Tagalog)

**Main lexical distinctions and overlaps (F1–F3):** Tagalog organizes the truth domain around totoo (true/real), tama (correct/right), tapat (honest/faithful), mali (wrong/incorrect), and tunay (genuine/authentic). The term tama shows a notable semantic extension: in the construction tama na, it shifts from correctness to sufficiency/cessation ("that's enough"), a pattern also seen with hustó na. The term tapat covers both honesty in speech and faithfulness/loyalty in conduct, without a clear boundary between these senses. F3 identifies 10 semantic groups: factual correspondence, authenticity/genuineness, correctness evaluation, honesty/truthfulness in conduct, trust/reliability, resemblance/similarity, emphatic confirmation/reality markers, sufficiency/cessation markers, essence/true nature, and physical impact/hitting. The last group (tamaan, tumama) reflects polysemy of the tama root between correctness and physical hitting.

**Derivational/morphological asymmetries:** Tagalog uses circumfix ka-...-an to derive abstract nouns (katotohanan, katapatan, katamaan, kasinungalingan). The mag- prefix derives verbs (magtapat, magsinungaling, magkatotoo). The pa-...-an causative (patunayan) is productive for verification. The -sapa abundance suffix (napakatotoo, napakatapat) intensifies. The particle system (ba, nga, naman, pa, rin, kaya, lang, puni) creates a rich set of discourse-marking constructions without morphological changes to the base form. No dedicated morphological negation prefix; negation uses hindi or di- analytically.

**Constructional patterns (F4):** Predicate-initial word order is the unmarked pattern for attribution. The ba particle forms yes/no questions. The kung conditional introduces hypothetical truth conditions. The mag-ka- inchoative (magkatotoo) expresses becoming true/realized. The i- causative (itama, iwasto) expresses correction. Reciprocal mag-...-an (magtapatan, magtotohanan) expresses mutual honesty. The walang + nominalization construction (walang katotohanan, walang katapatan) creates stronger categorical negation than hindi. The sa totoo lang discourse marker is highly grammaticalized for introducing frank statements.

**Oppositional structures (F5):** The primary lexical opposition is totoo/kasinungalingan (true/lie) and tama/mali (correct/wrong). Morphological negation uses hindi or walang. The may/walang opposition (may katotohanan vs. walang katotohanan) expresses presence vs. absence of truth as a possessed quality. No dedicated causative for "falsify" or "make incorrect" is attested, creating an asymmetry with the productive itama (to correct).

### sw (Swahili)

**Main lexical distinctions and overlaps (F1–F3):** Swahili shows the most extensive Arabic loanword integration in the truth domain: sahihi (correct, from Arabic ṣaḥīḥ), haki (right/justice, from Arabic ḥaqq), halisi (genuine, from Arabic khāliṣ), hakika (certainty, from Arabic ḥaqīqa), batili (false/invalid, from Arabic bāṭil), hoja (argument, from Arabic ḥujja), ushahidi (evidence, from Arabic shahīd), adilifu (just, from Arabic ʿadl). The term haki is notably polysemous, covering both truth ("haki ni kwamba..." = "the truth is that...") and rights/justice ("haki za binadamu" = "human rights"), a dual function not paralleled in other languages in the corpus. F3 identifies 13 semantic groups including a distinct group for document authentication/validation and a group for rights/entitlements that is more elaborated than in any other language.

**Derivational/morphological asymmetries:** The u- prefix (noun class 11/14) productively derives abstract nouns (ukweli, uongo, usahihi, uhalisi, uadilifu, uhakika, ubovu, unyofu). The -ish- causative suffix is productive for verification/validation verbs (hakikisha, thibitisha, kamilisha, batilisha, dhihirisha). The -ifu adjectival suffix derives character adjectives (-adilifu, -danganyifu, -nyofu). The -ana reciprocal suffix is limited in the truth domain (hojiana). No dedicated causative for "falsify" is attested; the concept requires circumlocution.

**Constructional patterns (F4):** The invariant copula ni (affirmative) and si (negative) are used for attribution without agreement marking. The complementizer kwamba introduces propositional complements. The kwa + abstract noun pattern productively derives manner adverbials (kwa kweli, kwa hakika, kwa usahihi, kwa haki, kwa uadilifu). The -enye possessive construction identifies persons characterized by truth-domain qualities (mwenye kweli, mwenye haki). The -a associative construction (rafiki wa kweli, habari za uongo) productively marks genuineness or falsity across noun classes. Proverbs (ukweli ni uchungu, ukweli hauna miguu) are noted as fixed expressions.

**Oppositional structures (F5):** The primary lexical opposition is kweli/uongo. The ni/si copula opposition is the standard affirmation/negation pattern. The thibitisha/batilisha opposition is a lexical opposition between causative verbs for validating and invalidating. The linda haki/vunja haki opposition is a constructional opposition for protecting vs. violating rights. No dedicated causative for "falsify" creates an asymmetry with thibitisha (verify).

### qu (Quechua)

**Main lexical distinctions and overlaps (F1–F3):** Quechua is unique in the corpus for having grammaticalized evidentiality as an obligatory morphological system. The suffixes -mi (direct knowledge/personal assertion), -si (hearsay/reportative), and -cha (conjecture/inference) must appear in main clauses and mark the epistemic source of information. This creates a three-way grammatical distinction not present as morphology in any other language in the corpus. The truth domain is organized around chiqaq (true/genuine), allin (good/well), llulla (lie/false), pantay (to err), sut'i (clear/evident), and chanin (valid/legitimate). The term allin covers both moral goodness and practical correctness without a clear boundary. F3 identifies 13 semantic groups: truth/falsity in speech, moral/practical goodness/badness, error/mistake/correctness, clarity/obviousness, knowledge/belief/thought, perception/evidence, completeness/fullness/deficiency, magnitude/importance, legitimacy/authority/value, social status/resources, aesthetic/affective evaluation, honor/respect/recognition, and physical shape/moral straightness.

**Derivational/morphological asymmetries:** The inchoative -ya- suffix is highly productive (allinyay, chiqaqyay, sut'iyay, hatunyay, sumaqyay, millayyay, qhapaqyay, wakchayay, k'uchiyay, hunt'ayay, ch'usaqyay). The causative -chi- suffix is productive (allinyachiy, chiqanchay, pantachiy, sut'iyachiy, hatunchay, huch'uyachiy, sumaqyachiy, millaychay, hunt'achiy, ch'usaqchay). However, several notable asymmetries exist: no dedicated inchoative for "become false" (*llullayay), no dedicated inchoative for "become incorrect," no dedicated causative for "falsify" (*llullachay in the sense of making something false), no dedicated causative for "make worse," no dedicated causative for "make straight" (physical/moral). The -sapa abundance suffix and -puni emphatic suffix are productive across the domain.

**Constructional patterns (F4):** Evidential suffixes (-mi, -si, -cha) are obligatory in main clauses. The -ta accusative suffix productively derives manner adverbials (chiqaqta rimay, allinta ruway). The -man allative expresses direction toward a state (allinman kutiy, chiqaqman). The -pi locative expresses being in a state (pantaypi kay, allinpi kay). Comparison uses hina/shina (as...as) without morphological comparative. The -raq continuative suffix expresses stability of states (chiqaqraqmi = "still true"). Cultural concepts allin kawsay and sumaq kawsay are semi-lexicalized. A structural gap is noted: no construction for disconfirmation (proving false) exists as a dedicated or common expression.

**Oppositional structures (F5):** The primary lexical opposition is chiqaq/llulla. The -mi/-si/-cha evidential opposition is a three-way grammatical opposition. The rikuywan yachay/uyariywan yachay opposition distinguishes visual from auditory evidence. Several asymmetric oppositions are noted: ch'ulla (unpaired) has no dedicated lexical opposite; k'uchi (crooked/dishonest) has no parallel physical-to-moral term for straightness/honesty; allinyay (improve) has no dedicated opposite verb for worsening. The -lla limitative and -puni emphatic suffixes form a morphological opposition in degree of assertion.

## 3. Cross-Language Summary

### Recurring Convergences (patterns observed in many languages)

- **Separation of propositional truth from authenticity/genuineness.** All eight languages distinguish between evaluating whether a statement corresponds to reality (propositional truth) and evaluating whether an object, person, or relationship is genuine rather than fake or counterfeit. In Chinese this is 真实 vs. 真品/真迹; in Hebrew emet vs. amiti/otenti; in Sanskrit satya vs. halisi (borrowed into Swahili); in Greek αληθής vs. αυθεντικός/γνήσιος; in English true vs. genuine/authentic; in Tagalog totoo vs. tunay; in Swahili kweli vs. halisi; in Quechua chiqaq vs. chanin. The boundary between these clusters is not always sharp, and several languages show terms that participate in both.

- **Separation of correctness/accuracy from moral rightness.** All eight languages show a distinction between evaluating whether something is factually accurate or procedurally correct and evaluating whether it is morally right or just. In Chinese 对/错 (correct/wrong) vs. 诚/欺 (honest/deceptive); in Hebrew nakhon (correct) vs. tsodek (right/just); in Sanskrit samyak (correct) vs. dharma (righteous); in Greek σωστός (correct) vs. δίκαιος (just); in English correct vs. righteous; in Tagalog tama (correct) vs. tapat (honest/faithful); in Swahili sahihi (correct) vs. haki (just/right); in Quechua chiqan (exact/correct) vs. allin (good/right). However, several languages show terms that straddle this boundary (Hebrew tsodek, Swahili haki, Quechua allin).

- **Productive abstract nominalization from truth-domain adjectives.** All eight languages show productive derivation of abstract nouns from truth-domain adjectives. Chinese uses -性 and -度; Hebrew uses -ut; Sanskrit uses -tā and -tva; Greek uses -ότητα and -εια; English uses -ity, -ness, -cy, -th; Tagalog uses ka-...-an circumfix; Swahili uses u- prefix; Quechua uses -y infinitive nominalization and -na instrumental nominalization. The resulting abstract nouns are used in formal and philosophical discourse across all languages.

- **Productive manner adverbials from truth-domain terms.** All eight languages show productive formation of manner adverbials from truth-domain vocabulary. Chinese uses -地 (真诚地, 客观地); Hebrew uses be- prepositional prefix (be-emet, be-tsedek, be-yosher); Sanskrit uses instrumental case (satyena, dharmeṇa) and -tas ablative (tattvataḥ); Greek uses -ώς suffix (ορθώς, ψευδώς) and -ά (ορθά, σωστά); English uses -ly (truly, honestly, correctly); Tagalog uses -ta accusative (chiqaqta in Quechua parallel) and particle constructions; Swahili uses kwa + abstract noun (kwa kweli, kwa hakika, kwa usahihi); Quechua uses -ta accusative (chiqaqta, allinta). This pattern is universal across the corpus.

- **Asymmetry between positive and negative agent nouns.** Six of eight languages show that agent nouns for liars, deceivers, or error-makers are more lexically elaborated or more commonly used than agent nouns for truth-tellers or honest persons. In Greek, ψεύτης (liar) is core everyday vocabulary while αληθολόγος (truth-teller) is marginal/literary. In English, "liar" is core while "truth-teller" is a compound. In Hebrew, shakran (liar) is common while no parallel positive agent is noted. In Sanskrit, mṛṣāvādin (liar) and satyavādin (truth-speaker) are parallel compounds, but the negative agent is more frequently used in moral condemnation. In Swahili, mdanganyifu (deceiver) is attested while mtu wa kweli (person of truth) is periphrastic. In Tagalog, sinungaling (liar) is a simple lexical item while no parallel simple term for "truth-teller" is noted. Chinese and Quechua show similar patterns.

- **Discourse markers grammaticalized from truth vocabulary.** Seven of eight languages show truth-domain vocabulary grammaticalized into discourse markers that introduce frank statements, corrections, or emphatic affirmations. Chinese: 说实话, 实际上, 事实上; Hebrew: האמת היא ש..., לאמיתו של דבר; Greek: στην αλήθεια, η αλήθεια είναι ότι; English: to be honest, truth be told, in truth; Tagalog: sa totoo lang, ang totoo; Swahili: kwa kweli, haki ni kwamba; Quechua: chiqaqmanta rimay. Sanskrit shows less grammaticalization of this type, though tathā hi and yataḥ tasmāt function as connective discourse markers.

- **Distinction between unintentional error and deliberate falsehood.** All eight languages maintain a distinction between making a mistake (without moral fault) and deliberately lying or deceiving. Chinese: 错 (error) vs. 谎言/欺骗 (lie/deception); Hebrew: ta'ut (error) vs. sheker (lie); Sanskrit: bhrama/viparyaya (cognitive error) vs. mṛṣā/chala (deliberate falsehood); Greek: λάθος (mistake) vs. ψέμα/ψεύδομαι (lie); English: mistake/error vs. lie/deceive; Tagalog: pagkakamali (mistake) vs. kasinungalingan/magsinungaling (lie); Swahili: (implied distinction between pantay-type error and uongo/danganya-type deception); Quechua: pantay (to err) vs. llulla (lie) and pantachiy (to mislead/deceive).

### Major Divergences (patterns observed in few or one language)

- **Grammaticalized evidentiality (Quechua only).** Only Quechua has obligatory morphological evidential suffixes (-mi, -si, -cha) that must appear in main clauses to mark the epistemic source of information. All other languages express evidential distinctions lexically or constructionally (e.g., Hebrew dative experiencer constructions, Sanskrit instrumental of means, Swahili verbal collocations with rikuy/uyariy in Quechua parallel), but none grammaticalize this as obligatory morphology.

- **Elaborated rights/entitlements vocabulary (Swahili, Hebrew).** Swahili shows the most elaborated vocabulary for rights and entitlements, with haki generating an extensive set of compounds (haki za binadamu, haki ya kura, haki ya kujieleza, etc.) and verbal collocations (dai haki, toa haki, linda haki, vunja haki). Hebrew shows a parallel elaboration around tsedek/tsedakah/tsadik. Other languages have terms for justice or rightness but do not show this degree of elaboration in the rights/entitlements domain.

- **Technical epistemological vocabulary (Sanskrit only).** Sanskrit has a uniquely elaborated technical vocabulary for epistemology, centered on the pramāṇa system (valid means of knowledge) with specific terms for perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), testimony (śabda), comparison (upamāna), postulation (arthāpatti), and non-perception (anupalabdhi) as distinct epistemic instruments. No other language in the corpus shows this degree of technical differentiation of epistemic sources.

- **Productive prefix compounding for false/pseudo- terms (Greek only).** Greek shows uniquely productive ψευδο- prefix compounding across scientific, medical, legal, religious, and everyday domains, generating dozens of compounds. While other languages have terms for false or counterfeit things, none show this degree of productive prefix compounding. The ορθο- prefix is similarly productive across multiple domains.

- **Dual meaning of haki as both "truth" and "rights/justice" (Swahili only).** The Swahili term haki covers both the sense of "truth" (as in "haki ni kwamba..." = "the truth is that...") and "rights/justice" (as in "haki za binadamu" = "human rights"). This dual function is not paralleled in any other language in the corpus, where truth and rights/justice are covered by distinct lexical items.

- **Semantic extension of correctness to sufficiency/cessation (Tagalog).** Tagalog shows a notable semantic extension where tama na and hustó na shift from correctness/exactness to sufficiency and cessation ("that's enough," "stop now"). While other languages show some semantic extension of correctness terms, this specific extension to a cessation marker is most clearly documented in Tagalog.

- **Extensive sat- root compounds for moral character (Sanskrit).** Sanskrit shows an unusually large set of compounds using sat- (good/true/existing) to describe positive moral qualities and social categories: satpuruṣa (good person), sajjana (good people), sadguṇa (good quality), satsaṅga (association with the good), satkarma (good deed), satpatha (right path), sadvṛtta (good conduct), sadvacana (true speech). While other languages have terms for good persons and good conduct, none show this degree of systematic compounding with a single root.

### Asymmetries, Gaps, and Irregularities

- **Asymmetry between causatives for making true vs. making false.** Multiple languages show productive causative verbs for establishing truth or verifying (Chinese: 证实, 核实; Hebrew: βεβαιώνω; Sanskrit: thibitisha; Greek: βεβαιώνω; English: verify, confirm, authenticate; Tagalog: patunayan; Swahili: thibitisha, hakikisha; Quechua: chiqanchay) but lack dedicated or common causative verbs for making something false or falsifying. Quechua explicitly notes this as a structural gap. Swahili, Tagalog, and Greek similarly lack dedicated "falsify" causatives. English has "falsify" but it is noted as having two distinct senses (alter to make false; prove false). This asymmetry appears across at least five languages.

- **Asymmetry between inchoative "become true" and "become false."** Several languages show that inchoative constructions for "becoming true" or "being verified" are more natural or more lexicalized than parallel constructions for "becoming false." Quechua explicitly notes that no dedicated *llullayay "become false" exists, while chiqaqyay (become true) is attested. Hebrew F6 notes that "the thing became true" constructions are marginal while revelation verbs (hitgalah, hitbarar) are preferred. Greek F6 notes that γίνομαι (become) is unnatural with ψέμα (lie) while natural with σωστός (correct). This asymmetry appears across at least four languages.

- **Asymmetry between positive and negative evaluation terms in derivational productivity.** Across multiple languages, negative evaluation terms (lie, error, falsehood, corruption) show somewhat different derivational productivity than positive terms. In Quechua, several inchoative and causative forms exist for negative states (k'uchiyay, wakchayay) but their positive counterparts (becoming straight, becoming rich) show asymmetric productivity. In Greek, the negative agent vocabulary (ψεύτης, ψεύτρα, ψευταράς, απατεώνας) is more elaborated than positive agent vocabulary. In English, the "false" compound vocabulary is more extensive than the "true" compound vocabulary.

- **Variable treatment of resemblance/similarity within the truth domain.** Languages differ in how closely they integrate resemblance/similarity vocabulary with truth evaluation. Chinese includes 像 and 似 (resemble/seem) in the truth domain vocabulary. Hebrew includes דומה (domeh, resemble/seem) prominently, with its epistemic hedging function (domeh li she- = "it seems to me that"). Greek includes αληθοφανής (apparently true, verisimilar). Sanskrit includes sadṛśa (similar) and sādṛśya (similarity) as part of the truth domain. Tagalog includes kawangis, katulad, and kamukha (resemblance terms) as a distinct semantic group. Swahili and Quechua show less integration of resemblance vocabulary into the truth domain.

- **Variable elaboration of the objectivity/subjectivity distinction.** Chinese shows the most elaborated vocabulary for objectivity/subjectivity as an epistemic distinction, with 客观/主观 (objective/subjective) forming a productive pair with derived forms (客观性, 主观性, 客观化, 客观地, 主观地, 主观臆断). No other language in the corpus shows a comparably elaborated objectivity/subjectivity vocabulary as a distinct semantic cluster within the truth domain.

- **Variable treatment of hypothetical/suppositional reasoning.** Chinese shows a distinct semantic group for hypothetical and suppositional reasoning using the 假 (false/suppose) morpheme (假如, 假设, 假定, 假想, 假以时日), where the same morpheme that marks falsity also marks hypothetical framing. Sanskrit shows a parallel pattern where hypothetical constructions use conditional particles (yadi...tadā) without lexical connection to falsity terms. Other languages do not show this integration of falsity vocabulary into hypothetical framing.

- **Structural gap in disconfirmation vocabulary (Quechua, noted; others implied).** Quechua explicitly notes a structural gap: no construction exists for disconfirmation (proving something false) as a dedicated or common expression. The concept requires circumlocution. While other languages have terms for disproving (English: disprove, refute, debunk; Greek: διαψεύδω; Hebrew: הפריך; Sanskrit: pramāṇabādha), the Quechua data suggests this may be a more general pattern where verification vocabulary is more elaborated than disconfirmation vocabulary. The Swahili and Tagalog data similarly show less elaborated disconfirmation vocabulary compared to verification vocabulary.

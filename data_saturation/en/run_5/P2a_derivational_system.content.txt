# CLCA Protocol v3.4 — P2a Derivational System
**Language: English (en)**

---

**Note on transliteration:** English uses the Latin script natively; transliteration is not applicable for any item below.

---

**Scope note:** The following documents attested derivational morphology for each P1 base form. Forms already fully documented in P1b are cross-referenced rather than re-expanded. Derivations are listed per base form; no paradigm completion is assumed. Gaps and asymmetries are preserved as found.

---

## 1. Base Form: *true*

---

**Derived Form 1**
- Base Form: *true*
- Derived Form: *truly*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *true* + *-ly* (adverbial suffix)
- Natural English translation: in a true or genuine manner; used also as an intensifier
- Usage: Extremely common across all registers; functions as adverb of manner ("truly representative"), intensifier ("truly remarkable"), and in fixed epistolary closings ("Yours truly"). See P1b Root 2, item 1.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 2**
- Base Form: *true*
- Derived Form: *truth*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: Old English *trēowe* + *-th* (abstract noun suffix)
- Natural English translation: the property of being true; a true statement or fact
- Usage: Primary nominalization of *true*; documented as P1 item 2 and P1b Root 2, item 2. Operates across philosophical, legal, moral, and everyday contexts.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 3**
- Base Form: *true*
- Derived Form: *trueness*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *true* + *-ness* (nominal suffix)
- Natural English translation: the quality or state of being true, accurate, or properly aligned
- Usage: Less common than *truth*; used in technical contexts (e.g., mechanical alignment: "the trueness of a wheel") and in general evaluative contexts. Occupies a narrower register than *truth*.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 4**
- Base Form: *true*
- Derived Form: *untrue*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *un-* (negation) + *true*
- Natural English translation: not true; false; also, not faithful or loyal
- Usage: Applied to statements and propositions to indicate falsity, and to persons or relationships to indicate disloyalty. Slightly more formal than *false* in some contexts. "The accusation was untrue."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 5**
- Base Form: *true*
- Derived Form: *truer*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *true* + *-er* (comparative suffix)
- Natural English translation: more true; more accurate or genuine
- Usage: Comparative form; used in everyday and philosophical discourse. "No truer statement has ever been made." Somewhat restricted in use given that truth is often treated as non-gradable in formal contexts.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 6**
- Base Form: *true*
- Derived Form: *truest*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *true* + *-est* (superlative suffix)
- Natural English translation: most true; most genuine or faithful
- Usage: Superlative form; used in everyday and literary discourse. "In the truest sense of the word." Appears in fixed expressions and evaluative contexts.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Note on productivity:** The *true* root is highly productive derivationally, with the primary nominalization *truth* generating its own substantial derivational family (documented separately below under item 2). The adjectival base *true* itself yields a more limited set of direct derivatives (*truly*, *trueness*, *untrue*, comparative and superlative forms).

## 2. Base Form: *truth*

---

**Derived Form 1**
- Base Form: *truth*
- Derived Form: *truthful*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *truth* + *-ful* (adjectival suffix)
- Natural English translation: habitually telling the truth; characterized by truth
- Usage: Applied to persons, statements, and accounts in moral and evaluative contexts. "She gave a truthful account of what had happened." See P1b Root 2, item 3.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 2**
- Base Form: *truth*
- Derived Form: *truthfully*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *truthful* + *-ly*
- Natural English translation: in a truthful manner; honestly
- Usage: Adverbial form; used in moral and evaluative contexts. "He answered every question truthfully." See P1b Root 2, item 4.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 3**
- Base Form: *truth*
- Derived Form: *truthfulness*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *truthful* + *-ness*
- Natural English translation: the quality of being truthful
- Usage: Used in moral evaluation of persons and in assessments of accounts or testimony. "The court was satisfied with the truthfulness of her statement." See P1b Root 2, item 5.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 4**
- Base Form: *truth*
- Derived Form: *untruth*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *un-* (negation) + *truth*
- Natural English translation: a false statement; a lie (often used as a euphemism)
- Usage: Used as a formal or euphemistic alternative to *lie* or *falsehood*, particularly in political and diplomatic discourse. "The report contained several untruths." See P1b Root 2, item 6.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 5**
- Base Form: *truth*
- Derived Form: *untruthful*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *un-* + *truthful*
- Natural English translation: not truthful; given to telling untruths
- Usage: Applied to persons or statements in evaluative contexts. "The witness was found to be untruthful on several points." See P1b Root 2, item 7.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 6**
- Base Form: *truth*
- Derived Form: *untruthfully*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *un-* + *truthfully*
- Natural English translation: in an untruthful manner
- Usage: Adverbial form; used in legal and evaluative contexts. "He had answered untruthfully under oath." See P1b Root 2, item 8.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 7**
- Base Form: *truth*
- Derived Form: *untruthfulness*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *un-* + *truthfulness*
- Natural English translation: the quality of being untruthful
- Usage: Used in moral and legal evaluation. "The untruthfulness of the account was established by documentary evidence." See P1b Root 2, item 9.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 8**
- Base Form: *truth*
- Derived Form: *truthiness*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *truth* + *-i-* (linking element) + *-ness*
- Natural English translation: the quality of seeming or feeling true without being supported by evidence or fact
- Usage: A satirical/informal coinage (Stephen Colbert, 2005) that entered broader usage; refers to the quality of appearing true based on intuition or feeling rather than evidence. Documented in P1b Root 2 peripheral extensions. The suffix *-iness* rather than *-ness* gives the form a colloquial, diminutive character.
- Epistemic status: **[MARGINAL]**

---

**Derived Form 9**
- Base Form: *truth*
- Derived Form: *post-truth*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *post-* (Latin "after, subsequent to") + *truth*
- Natural English translation: relating to or denoting circumstances in which objective facts are less influential in shaping public opinion than appeals to emotion or personal belief
- Usage: Entered widespread use in political and media discourse from approximately 2016; named Oxford Dictionaries Word of the Year 2016. Used attributively ("post-truth politics," "post-truth era"). Documented in P1b Root 2 peripheral extensions.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Note on productivity:** *Truth* is more derivationally productive than its base *true*, generating the *truthful* family and the *untruth* family as well as newer coinages. The *post-truth* and *truthiness* forms represent recent extensions at the boundary of standard derivation.

## 3. Base Form: *accurate*

---

**Derived Form 1**
- Base Form: *accurate*
- Derived Form: *accurately*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *accurate* + *-ly*
- Natural English translation: in an accurate manner; without error
- Usage: Common adverbial form used across scientific, journalistic, and everyday registers. "The instrument measured temperature accurately."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 2**
- Base Form: *accurate*
- Derived Form: *accuracy*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *accurate* + *-cy* (nominal suffix, variant of *-cy* from *-acy*)
- Natural English translation: the quality or degree of being accurate; closeness to a true value or standard
- Usage: Used extensively in scientific measurement, journalism, and legal contexts. Contrasts with *precision* in technical usage. "The accuracy of the report was questioned."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 3**
- Base Form: *accurate*
- Derived Form: *inaccurate*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *in-* (negation) + *accurate*
- Natural English translation: not accurate; containing errors or deviating from fact
- Usage: Applied to descriptions, measurements, and reports. "The map was inaccurate in several respects."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 4**
- Base Form: *accurate*
- Derived Form: *inaccurately*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *inaccurate* + *-ly*
- Natural English translation: in an inaccurate manner; with errors
- Usage: Adverbial form; used in evaluative and critical contexts. "The figures had been inaccurately reported."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 5**
- Base Form: *accurate*
- Derived Form: *inaccuracy*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *in-* + *accuracy*
- Natural English translation: the quality of being inaccurate; a specific error or deviation from fact
- Usage: Used both as a mass noun (referring to the general property) and as a count noun (referring to specific errors). "The article contained several inaccuracies."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Note on productivity:** The *accurate* family is moderately productive, yielding a clean positive/negative pair (*accurate*/*inaccurate*) with corresponding adverbs and nominals. No verbal derivation from *accurate* is attested in standard English (the related verb is *verify* or *confirm*, not *\*accurize* in general usage, though *accurize* exists as a technical firearms term).

## 4. Base Form: *genuine*

---

**Derived Form 1**
- Base Form: *genuine*
- Derived Form: *genuinely*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *genuine* + *-ly*
- Natural English translation: in a genuine manner; authentically; sincerely
- Usage: Common adverbial form used across evaluative, moral, and everyday registers. "She was genuinely surprised by the result."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 2**
- Base Form: *genuine*
- Derived Form: *genuineness*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *genuine* + *-ness*
- Natural English translation: the quality of being genuine; authenticity
- Usage: Used in evaluative contexts concerning objects, persons, and emotions. Less common than *authenticity* in formal discourse but fully standard. "The genuineness of the document was confirmed by experts."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 3**
- Base Form: *genuine*
- Derived Form: *disingenuine*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *dis-* (negation/reversal) + *genuine*
- Natural English translation: not genuine; insincere
- Usage: Attested but considerably less common than *disingenuous* (which is the standard form); *disingenuine* appears in informal and non-specialist usage. Its status is contested; many style guides prefer *disingenuous*.
- Epistemic status: **[UNCERTAIN]**

---

**Note on the *disingenuous* gap:** The standard negative/pejorative counterpart to *genuine* in the sense of "not straightforward, insincere" is *disingenuous*, which is not a direct derivation from *genuine* but from *ingenuous* (Latin *ingenuus* "freeborn, frank") via *dis-*. The semantic overlap with *genuine* is strong but the morphological connection is indirect. *Disingenuous* is documented below under its own entry as it is not a P1 base form but is closely related.

---

**Derived Form 4**
- Base Form: *genuine*
- Derived Form: *disingenuous*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *dis-* + *ingenuous* (Latin *ingenuus* "freeborn, frank, open") — not a direct derivation from *genuine* but semantically adjacent
- Natural English translation: not candid or sincere; giving a false impression of frankness
- Usage: Widely used in formal, political, and journalistic discourse to describe statements or persons that appear open but are actually misleading. "It would be disingenuous to claim ignorance of the policy."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 5**
- Base Form: *genuine* (via *disingenuous*)
- Derived Form: *disingenuously*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *disingenuous* + *-ly*
- Natural English translation: in a disingenuous manner; with false candor
- Usage: Adverbial form; used in formal and critical discourse. "He disingenuously claimed to have no prior knowledge."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 6**
- Base Form: *genuine* (via *disingenuous*)
- Derived Form: *disingenuousness*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *disingenuous* + *-ness*
- Natural English translation: the quality of being disingenuous
- Usage: Used in formal evaluative contexts. "The disingenuousness of the response was widely noted."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Note on productivity:** *Genuine* has limited direct derivational productivity in English; the primary derivatives are *genuinely* and *genuineness*. The semantically related *disingenuous* family is productive but derives from a different Latin base.

## 5. Base Form: *authentic*

---

**Derived Form 1**
- Base Form: *authentic*
- Derived Form: *authentically*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *authentic* + *-ally* (adverbial suffix following *-ic* stems)
- Natural English translation: in an authentic manner; genuinely; in a way that is true to an original or to oneself
- Usage: Used across aesthetic, philosophical, and everyday registers. "The dish was authentically prepared using traditional methods."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 2**
- Base Form: *authentic*
- Derived Form: *authenticity*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *authentic* + *-ity*
- Natural English translation: the quality of being authentic; genuineness; fidelity to an original or to one's true self
- Usage: Used extensively in philosophical discourse (existentialist authenticity), art history (authenticity of artworks), and popular culture. "Questions of authenticity are central to the study of folk music."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 3**
- Base Form: *authentic*
- Derived Form: *authenticate*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *authentic* + *-ate* (verbal suffix)
- Natural English translation: to establish or confirm the authenticity of something; to verify as genuine
- Usage: Used in legal, archival, art historical, and digital security contexts. "The signature was authenticated by a handwriting expert."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 4**
- Base Form: *authentic* (via *authenticate*)
- Derived Form: *authentication*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *authenticate* + *-ion*
- Natural English translation: the process of establishing authenticity; a verified confirmation of identity or genuineness
- Usage: Used in legal, archival, and digital security contexts. Particularly prominent in computing and cybersecurity ("two-factor authentication"). "Authentication of the document took several weeks."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 5**
- Base Form: *authentic* (via *authenticate*)
- Derived Form: *authenticator*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *authenticate* + *-or* (agentive suffix)
- Natural English translation: a person or device that authenticates; a tool or system used for authentication
- Usage: Used in legal contexts for persons who authenticate documents, and in computing for software or hardware authentication tools. "The authenticator app generates a one-time code."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 6**
- Base Form: *authentic*
- Derived Form: *inauthenticity*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *in-* (negation) + *authenticity*
- Natural English translation: the quality of being inauthentic; lack of genuineness or fidelity to an original or to oneself
- Usage: Used in philosophical (especially existentialist) and cultural discourse. "Sartre's analysis of inauthenticity describes the flight from freedom."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 7**
- Base Form: *authentic*
- Derived Form: *inauthentic*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *in-* + *authentic*
- Natural English translation: not authentic; not genuine; failing to correspond to an original or to one's true self
- Usage: Used in philosophical, aesthetic, and everyday evaluative contexts. "The performance felt inauthentic and rehearsed."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 8**
- Base Form: *authentic*
- Derived Form: *inauthentically*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *inauthentic* + *-ally*
- Natural English translation: in an inauthentic manner
- Usage: Adverbial form; used in philosophical and evaluative contexts. Less common than the adjectival form. "He lived inauthentically, always performing for others."
- Epistemic status: **[MARGINAL]**

---

**Note on productivity:** *Authentic* is among the more derivationally productive items in the P1 set, generating a verbal form (*authenticate*) that itself produces further derivatives. The *in-* negation applies at multiple levels (*inauthentic*, *inauthenticity*).

## 6. Base Form: *valid*

---

**Derived Form 1**
- Base Form: *valid*
- Derived Form: *validity*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *valid* + *-ity*
- Natural English translation: the quality of being valid; in logic, the property of an argument whose conclusion follows necessarily from its premises; in law, the property of being legally effective
- Usage: Used extensively in logic, law, statistics, and psychometrics. "The validity of the argument was disputed." In psychometrics, *validity* refers to whether a test measures what it purports to measure.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 2**
- Base Form: *valid*
- Derived Form: *validly*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *valid* + *-ly*
- Natural English translation: in a valid manner; with logical or legal force
- Usage: Adverbial form; used in logical and legal discourse. Less common than the adjectival form. "The conclusion follows validly from the premises."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 3**
- Base Form: *valid*
- Derived Form: *validate*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *valid* + *-ate* (verbal suffix)
- Natural English translation: to confirm the validity of; to officially approve or confirm; to recognize the legitimacy of
- Usage: Used in administrative, scientific, psychological, and everyday contexts. In psychology, also used to mean affirming a person's feelings or experiences as legitimate. "The results were validated by an independent laboratory."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 4**
- Base Form: *valid* (via *validate*)
- Derived Form: *validation*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *validate* + *-ion*
- Natural English translation: the act or process of validating; official confirmation of validity
- Usage: Used in administrative, scientific, and psychological contexts. "Validation of the parking ticket is required." In psychology, refers to the affirmation of another's emotional experience.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 5**
- Base Form: *valid* (via *validate*)
- Derived Form: *validator*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *validate* + *-or*
- Natural English translation: a person or system that validates; a tool used to check validity
- Usage: Used in computing (HTML validator, XML validator) and administrative contexts. "Run the document through the validator before submission."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 6**
- Base Form: *valid*
- Derived Form: *invalid* (adjective)
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *in-* (negation) + *valid*
- Natural English translation: not valid; lacking legal force; logically unsound
- Usage: Applied to arguments, documents, contracts, and claims. Note: *invalid* (adjective, stress on second syllable: in-VAL-id) is distinct from *invalid* (noun, stress on first syllable: IN-va-lid, meaning a person with a disability or illness), though both derive from Latin *invalidus*.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 7**
- Base Form: *valid*
- Derived Form: *invalidity*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *in-* + *validity*
- Natural English translation: the quality of being invalid; lack of legal or logical force
- Usage: Used in legal and logical contexts. "The court ruled on the invalidity of the contract."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 8**
- Base Form: *valid* (via *validate*)
- Derived Form: *invalidate*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *in-* + *validate*
- Natural English translation: to make invalid; to deprive of legal force or logical soundness
- Usage: Used in legal, scientific, and logical contexts. "New evidence invalidated the earlier findings."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 9**
- Base Form: *valid* (via *invalidate*)
- Derived Form: *invalidation*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *invalidate* + *-ion*
- Natural English translation: the act of invalidating; the process of rendering something invalid
- Usage: Used in legal and psychological contexts. In psychology, *invalidation* refers to the dismissal or denial of another's emotional experience. "The invalidation of the election results was contested."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Note on productivity:** *Valid* is highly productive, generating both a verbal form (*validate*) and its negative counterpart (*invalidate*), each with further derivatives. The stress-differentiated homograph *invalid* (noun) represents a separate semantic branch.

## 7. Base Form: *correct*

**Derived Form 1**

---

**Derived Form 3**
- Base Form: *correct*

- Transliteration: N/A

- Natural English translation: not correct; containing errors; not in conformity with fact or standard
- Usage: Applied to answers, statements, and procedures. "The answer was incorrect."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 4**
- Base Form: *correct*
- Derived Form: *incorrectly*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *incorrect* + *-ly*
- Natural English translation: in an incorrect manner; erroneously
- Usage: Common adverbial form. "The data had been incorrectly entered."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 5**
- Base Form: *correct*
- Derived Form: *incorrectness*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *incorrect* + *-ness*
- Natural English translation: the quality of being incorrect
- Usage: Used in evaluative contexts; less common than *incorrectly* or *error*. "The incorrectness of the assumption was demonstrated."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 6**
- Base Form: *correct* (verbal use)
- Derived Form: *correction*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: Latin *correctio* → *corrigere* + *-ion*
- Natural English translation: the act of correcting; an alteration made to remove an error; a punishment intended to reform
- Usage: Used across editorial, educational, legal, and penal contexts. "The newspaper published a correction the following day." The penal sense (*corrections*, *correctional*) is a distinct semantic branch.

**Derived Form 7**

- Derived Form: *correctional*
- Transliteration: N/A

- Natural English translation: relating to the correction or punishment of offenders
- Usage: Used primarily in the penal and administrative sense ("correctional facility," "correctional officer"). This represents a semantic branch distinct from the epistemic sense of *correct*.
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 8**
- Base Form: *correct* (verbal use)
- Derived Form: *corrective*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: Latin *correctivus* → *corrigere* + *-ive*
- Natural English translation: intended to correct; serving to rectify an error or deficiency
- Usage: Used as both adjective and noun. "Corrective action was taken immediately." "The glasses served as a corrective for his astigmatism."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Derived Form 9**
- Base Form: *correct* (verbal use)
- Derived Form: *corrector*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *correct* + *-or* (agentive suffix)
- Natural English translation: a person or device that corrects
- Usage: Used in editorial and technical contexts. Less common than *editor* or *proofreader* in everyday usage. "The spell corrector flagged the error."
- Epistemic status: **[MARGINAL]**

---

**Derived Form 10**
- Base Form: *correct* (verbal use)
- Derived Form: *uncorrected*
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal Breakdown: *un-* + *corrected* (past participle of *correct*)
- Natural English translation: not corrected; containing errors that have not been addressed
- Usage: Used in editorial, scientific, and administrative contexts. "The uncorrected proof contained several errors."
- Epistemic status: **[CORE]**

---

**Note on productivity:** *Correct* functions as both adjective and verb in English, which expands its derivational range. The penal/administrative branch (*correctional*, *corrections*) represents a semantic extension documented here for completeness but distinct from the epistemic domain.

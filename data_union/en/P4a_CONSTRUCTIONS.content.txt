# Layer: P4a_CONSTRUCTIONS

## 1. ATTRIBUTION

### Pattern
Copular construction: **Subject + BE + truth-domain adjective**
The truth-domain adjective appears as a predicative complement of *be* (or related copulas *seem*, *prove*, *remain*, *hold*, *ring*). The subject is typically a proposition-denoting NP (*the claim*, *the statement*, *the account*) or a person.

### Examples
- *That is true.* → That is true.
- *The account is accurate.* → The account is accurate.
- *The statement is true.* → The statement corresponds to the facts.
- *The claim proved false.* → The claim was shown not to correspond to the facts.
- *His testimony was truthful.* → His testimony was truthful.

### Notes
This is the primary syntactic frame for propositional truth-evaluation in English. The copula slot accepts a range of verbs beyond *be*: *prove*, *hold*, *remain*, *seem*, *ring* (*ring true/false*). Subject NPs are typically proposition-denoting or person-denoting; the choice of adjective varies accordingly (propositions take *true/false/accurate/correct*; persons take *honest/sincere/truthful*).

All major truth-domain adjectives (*true*, *accurate*, *correct*, *genuine*, *authentic*, *valid*, *credible*, *reliable*, *sound*, *sincere*, *plausible*, *spurious*, *fraudulent*, *legitimate*) enter this frame freely. No morphological change occurs on the adjective.

English uses the copula *be* for all predicative attribution in this domain; no distinct evidential or assertive copula exists. The same pattern covers factual correspondence (*true*, *correct*, *accurate*), genuineness (*genuine*, *authentic*), and legal standing (*valid*, *legitimate*).

## 2. NEGATIVE ATTRIBUTION

### Pattern
Three competing strategies: **(a) prefixal negation** on the adjective (*un-*, *in-*, *im-*, *dis-*, *il-*) + copula; **(b) sentential negation** (*not*) + copula + adjective; **(c) antonym substitution** (replacing the positive adjective with a lexically distinct negative item). All three are productive; choice depends on the base item.

### Examples
- *The claim is false.* → The claim is false.
- *That is not accurate.* / *That is inaccurate.* → That is not accurate / That is inaccurate.
- *The account is inaccurate.* → The account does not correspond to the facts. [prefixal negation]
- *The argument is not valid.* → The argument's conclusion does not follow from its premises. [sentential negation]
- *The document is inauthentic.* → The document is inauthentic.
- *The claim is not genuine.* / *The claim is fake.* → The claim is not genuine. / The claim is fake.

### Notes
Both prefixal and sentential negation strategies coexist freely; prefixed forms (*untrue*, *inaccurate*, *invalid*, *illegitimate*) are available for most core items and tend toward more formal register, while *not* + adjective is neutral across registers. Some negative forms carry additional connotations beyond simple negation (*disingenuous* implies strategic concealment; *spurious* implies pretense of validity).

Prefixal negation is available for most Latinate items (*inaccurate*, *incorrect*, *invalid*, *insincere*, *dishonest*, *illegitimate*, *implausible*, *unfalsifiable*, *unverifiable*, *unsound*, *untruthful*, *inauthentic*, *unreliable*) but is absent or marginal for native Germanic items (*fake*, *ersatz*) and fixed expressions (*bona fide*), which rely on sentential negation or antonym substitution. Several items have no prefixed negative form in common use (*ungenuine*, *unauthentic* are not standard; *not genuine*, *inauthentic* serve instead).

English shows asymmetry across the domain. Some items have productive prefixed negatives; others lack them and require periphrasis (*not genuine*, *not bona fide*) or a lexically distinct antonym (*fake*, *bogus*, *counterfeit* as negatives of *genuine*/*authentic*). No single strategy is universal.

## 3. ADNOMINAL MODIFICATION

### Pattern
**Truth-domain adjective + Noun** (attributive position, prenominal); postnominal predicative construction also available.
The adjective modifies a noun directly, preceding it in the noun phrase.

### Examples
- *a genuine mistake* → a genuine mistake
- *a credible witness* → a credible witness
- *a spurious correlation* → a statistical association that is not due to a direct causal relationship
- *fraudulent misrepresentation* → fraudulent misrepresentation

### Notes
English adjectives in this domain are uniformly prenominal in attributive use and show no agreement morphology. Many collocations are strongly conventionalized (*credible threat*, *false alarm*, *valid argument*, *plausible deniability*) and are documented in P2b as near-fixed constructions.

Attributive position is available to all adjectival P1 items. Certain collocations are highly lexicalized and domain-specific: *counterfeit currency*, *false positive*, *plausible deniability*, *bona fide purchaser*, *honest broker*, *credible threat*. These fixed attributive collocations carry meanings that are not fully predictable from the adjective alone and are concentrated in legal, scientific, political, and journalistic registers.

English strongly favors prenominal adjective placement (*accurate account*, *valid argument*, *genuine concern*, *spurious correlation*, *plausible deniability*). Postnominal position is available only in predicative constructions (*the witness was credible*) or in fixed legal phrases (*attorney general*-type patterns, not productive here). Several truth-domain collocations are strongly lexicalized in prenominal position (*fake news*, *counterfeit currency*, *bona fide offer*).

## 4. BECOME / INCHOATIVE

### Pattern
No dedicated inchoative morphology exists in English for this domain. Inchoative meaning is expressed periphrastically via: **(a) become + ADJ** (*become true*, *become valid*); **(b) prove + ADJ** (*prove true*, *prove false*, *prove correct*, *prove genuine*, *prove authentic*) — the most frequent inchoative-like frame in this domain; **(c) come true** (lexicalized inchoative for *true* only); **(d) turn out + ADJ / to be ADJ** (*turn out to be false*, *turn out to be genuine*).

### Examples
- *His prediction proved true.* → His prediction came to be established as correct.
- *The story turned out to be false.* → The story turned out to be false.
- *His prediction proved correct.* → His prediction came to be established as correct.
- *Her dream came true.* → Her dream was realized / came to be the case.
- *The rumor proved true.* → The rumor proved true.
- *The document turned out to be fraudulent.* → The document turned out to be fraudulent.

### Notes
There is no morphological inchoative in English. *Come true* is a lexicalized inchoative restricted to the item *true* (wishes, predictions being realized) and carries a connotation of desired realization rather than epistemic confirmation. *Prove* and *turn out to be* are the general periphrastic strategies and apply across the domain.

*Prove + ADJ* is the most productive inchoative-like frame in the truth domain, covering *true*, *false*, *correct*, *genuine*, *authentic*, and *valid*. *Turn out* is more neutral and broadly applicable.

The construction *become true/valid/authentic* is grammatical but uncommon; *prove* and *turn out to be* are the preferred inchoative-evidential frames, encoding the discovery or confirmation of a truth-domain property.

## 5. CAUSATIVE

### Pattern
No productive morphological causative exists in English for this domain. Causative meaning is expressed via: **(a) make + NP + ADJ** (*make it true*, *make it valid*); **(b) render + NP + ADJ** (*render invalid*); **(c) dedicated causative verbs** derived from the domain: *verify*, *authenticate*, *validate*, *falsify*, *legitimize*, *invalidate*, *delegitimize*, *correct*.

### Examples
- *The new evidence invalidated the argument.* → The new evidence invalidated the argument.
- *They falsified the documents.* → They falsified the documents.
- *The laboratory verified the results.* → The laboratory caused the results to be established as true/accurate.
- *New evidence invalidated the earlier findings.* → New evidence caused the earlier findings to be rendered invalid.
- *The expert authenticated the painting.* → The expert established the painting as authentic.
- *New evidence validated the hypothesis.* → New evidence made the hypothesis valid / confirmed its validity.

### Notes
Dedicated causative verbs (*validate*, *authenticate*, *invalidate*, *falsify*, *legitimize*, *delegitimize*, *verify*) are the preferred strategy in formal registers. The periphrastic *make + adjective* construction (*make it true*, *make it legitimate*) is available but less common than the derived verbs for most items in this domain.

The causative function in this domain is primarily lexicalized in derived verbs (*verify*, *authenticate*, *validate*, *falsify*, *legitimize*, *invalidate*, *delegitimize*, *canonize*) rather than expressed by a productive morphological causative. These verbs are not formed by a single productive causative suffix; they enter English via Latin/French derivational history. The *-ize*/*-ise* suffix is the most productive causative-like suffix active in this domain (*legitimize*, *delegitimize*, *canonize*).

These verbal forms are not mechanically derived from all adjectives in the domain — *\*genuinize*, *\*accuratize*, *\*plausibilize* are unattested — producing a lexical gap pattern documented in P2a.

## 6. ABSTRACT NOMINALIZATION

### Pattern
**ADJ/VERB + nominalizing suffix → abstract noun**. Dominant suffixes in this domain: *-ity* / *-cy* (Latinate: *validity*, *accuracy*, *authenticity*, *credibility*, *plausibility*, *falsity*, *legitimacy*, *sincerity*, *veridicality*); *-ness* (Germanic/general: *truthfulness*, *soundness*, *exactness*, *genuineness*, *spuriousness*, *speciousness*, *correctness*); *-y* (short form: *honesty*); *-hood* (*falsehood*); *-tion* (*verification*, *authentication*, *validation*, *correction*); *-ude* (*exactitude*, *verisimilitude*); *-th* (*truth*).

### Examples
- *truth* (← *true* + *-th*); *The truth of the matter was never established.* → The truth of the matter was never established.
- *validity* (← *valid* + *-ity*); *The validity of the argument was questioned.* → The validity of the argument was questioned.
- *The validity of the argument was disputed.* → Nominalization of *valid*; names the property of being valid.
- *The truthfulness of the account was never questioned.* → Nominalization of *truthful*; names the property of being truthful.
- *The authenticity of the document was disputed.* → The authenticity of the document was disputed.
- *The verification of the claim took three days.* → The verification of the claim took three days.

### Notes
Multiple competing suffixes are attested for some bases (*falseness* / *falsity*; *trueness* / *truth*), with the Latinate suffix typically favored in formal registers. The suffix *-th* in *truth* is an archaic Old English formation; all other productive nominalizations in this domain use Latinate or Germanic suffixes. Abstract nominalizations are highly productive across the full P1 inventory.

Latinate items strongly prefer *-ity*/*-cy* (*accuracy*, *credibility*, *authenticity*, *validity*, *plausibility*, *falsity*, *legitimacy*, *sincerity*); Germanic and mixed items prefer *-ness* (*truthfulness*, *soundness*, *genuineness*) or *-y* (*honesty*). Several items have competing nominalizations with register differentiation: *exactness* (neutral/technical) vs. *exactitude* (formal/literary); *falsity* (formal) vs. *falsehood* (formal, with connotation of a specific false statement) vs. *falseness* (marginal). Fixed expressions (*bona fide*, *ersatz*) and the bound prefix *pseudo-* do not take English nominalizing suffixes.

The root-level nouns *truth* and *veracity* are not suffix-derived from their corresponding adjectives in synchronic English but function as the standard abstract nominals for *true* and *veracious* respectively.

Suffix selection is lexically determined and not predictable from the base form.

## 7. AGENTIVE NOMINALIZATION

### Pattern
**Verbal or nominal base + agentive suffix** (*-er*, *-or*, *-ster*) OR **compound noun** (*truth-teller*, *truth-seeker*, *con man*). Refers to a person characterized by a truth-domain role or practice.

### Examples
- *faker* (← *fake* + *-er*): *He was exposed as a faker.* → He was exposed as a faker.
- *truth-teller* (*truth* + *tell* + *-er*): *She was known as a truth-teller.* → She was known as a truth-teller.
- *She was known as a truth-teller who refused to soften her assessments.* → Agent noun: a person who tells the truth, especially under social pressure.
- *The counterfeiter was sentenced to five years in prison.* → Agent noun: a person who makes fraudulent imitations, especially of currency.
- *She is a truth-teller who refuses to flatter the powerful.* → She is a person who tells the truth.
- *The counterfeiter was convicted on three counts.* → The person who produces counterfeits was convicted.

### Notes
Agent nouns for the positive pole (*truth-teller*, *truth-seeker*) are formed as compounds rather than by direct suffixation on the adjective, since *\*truer* and *\*truther* are not standard agent forms. Agent nouns for the negative pole (*faker*, *counterfeiter*, *falsifier*, *fabricator*) are formed by suffixation on the corresponding verb. No single agentive suffix covers the full domain.

The agentive nominalization paradigm is asymmetric in this domain. The positive agent role (one who tells the truth) is expressed by the compound *truth-teller* rather than by suffixation on a truth-domain adjective. The negative agent roles are more lexically diverse: *faker*, *counterfeiter*, *falsifier* (rare), *liar* (independent lexeme). *Authenticator* and *validator* are used primarily for technical devices or processes rather than persons. No agentive nominal is derived directly from *true*, *honest*, *sincere*, *accurate*, *genuine*, or *authentic* by suffixation in standard English.

Agentive nominalization is productive for the deception/fraud cluster (*faker*, *counterfeiter*, *defrauder*, *fraudster*, *con man*) and for the verification cluster (*authenticator*, *validator*, *verifier*). The negative/deceptive agents have richer morphological expression than the positive/honest agents, an asymmetry preserved from P2a.

*Liar* is the dominant agentive nominal for the negative pole but is not derived from any P1 item; it is an independent lexical item.

## 8. DEGREE MODIFICATION

### Pattern
**Degree adverb + truth-domain adjective** (*completely*, *broadly*, *technically*, *barely*, *dead*, *only too*, *reasonably*)
English freely modifies truth-domain adjectives with degree adverbs, producing scalar readings that are prominent in this domain.

### Examples
- *broadly accurate* → correct in general outline though possibly imprecise in detail
- *barely plausible* → at the outer limit of believability

### Notes
Degree modification is particularly productive in this domain because many of the core adjectives (*accurate*, *plausible*, *credible*, *true*) are implicitly scalar in use. Intensifiers (*dead right*, *only too true*) and downtoners (*broadly*, *technically*, *reasonably*) both occur frequently. *Technically accurate* is notable in that it asserts narrow truth while implying broader misleadingness — a pattern with no dedicated lexical item.

## 9. CONCESSIVE CONSTRUCTION

### Pattern
**Concessive frame + truth-domain expression + contrastive connector**
Patterns: *it is true that … but*, *true enough, but*, *admittedly … however*, *granted … nevertheless*

### Examples
- *It is true that progress has been made, but serious problems remain.* → It is true that progress has been made, but serious problems remain.
- *True enough, but that does not settle the question.* → True enough, but that does not settle the question.

### Notes
Concessive use of truth-domain expressions is a distinct and frequent construction in English argumentation. The speaker asserts partial truth of an opposing claim in order to introduce a qualification or counterpoint. *True*, *true enough*, *admittedly*, and *granted* are the primary concessive markers in this domain; they function as discourse operators rather than simple predicates.

## 10. PARTIAL-TRUTH CONSTRUCTION

### Pattern
**Noun phrase denoting a portion of truth** (*a grain of truth*, *a kernel of truth*, *a half-truth*) OR **verb phrase denoting distortion** (*stretch the truth*, *bend the truth*, *be economical with the truth*)

### Examples
- *There is a grain of truth in what he says.* → There is a grain of truth in what he says.
- *She was being economical with the truth.* → She was being economical with the truth.

### Notes
English has a well-developed set of constructions for expressing that a claim is partially but not wholly accurate, or that truth is being distorted without outright falsehood. These constructions occupy the space between Group 1 (full factual correspondence) and Group 3 (deliberate falsification) identified in P3, and they have no single-word equivalents. *Economical with the truth* is distinctive in framing dishonesty as a resource-management decision.

## 11. DISCOURSE-MARKER CONSTRUCTIONS

### Pattern
Several truth-domain items are grammaticalized or semi-grammaticalized as **sentence-initial or parenthetical discourse markers** that signal a shift to candor, introduce a concession, or assert the speaker's commitment to accuracy. These are not predicative or attributive uses of the adjective but pragmatic framing devices.

### Examples
- *To be honest, I don't think the plan will work.* → Discourse marker signaling candor; does not predicate honesty of a proposition.
- *True enough, but that doesn't settle the question.* → Concessive acknowledgment; partially grants a claim before introducing a counter-point.
- *Truth be told, the plan was never viable.* → To tell the truth, the plan was never viable.
- *In truth, the situation was far more complex than reported.* → In reality, the situation was far more complex than reported.

### Notes
Attested discourse-marker constructions in this domain include: *to be honest*, *let's be honest*, *honestly* (sentence-initial), *truth be told*, *to tell the truth*, *in all sincerity*, *frankly* (adjacent domain), *true enough*, *it is true that … but*, *correct me if I'm wrong*, *in truth*, *if truth be told*. These constructions are syntactically distinct from attributive and predicative uses: the adjective or noun is not predicated of a subject but functions as a pragmatic hedge, concessive opener, or candor signal.

English has a productive set of sentence-adverbial and parenthetical constructions that frame the following proposition as honest or corrective. They signal that the speaker is departing from a polite or expected formulation to state what they actually believe. This construction type has no equivalent in the derivational or attributive patterns above and is documented here as an emergent category from the P2b data.

*Exactly* functions as a response particle (confirmation token) in a related but distinct pattern.

## 12. PREPOSITIONAL-PHRASE CONSTRUCTIONS EXPRESSING CONFORMITY OR DEVIATION

### Pattern
**ADJ + prepositional phrase** (*true to*, *faithful to*, *out of true*): the truth-domain adjective combines with a prepositional phrase to predicate conformity with or deviation from a standard, original, or commitment. This pattern is distinct from simple attribution (Section 1) in that the standard of comparison is made explicit by the prepositional phrase.

### Examples
- *The film adaptation is remarkably faithful to the original novel.* → The adaptation conforms closely to the source text.
- *The door frame is slightly out of true.* → The door frame deviates from perfect alignment.

### Notes
The *true to X* pattern (*true to one's word*, *true to form*, *true to type*, *true to life*, *true to the original*) is productive and covers a range of standards: commitments, expected behavior, biological type, reality, and source texts. *Faithful to X* is more restricted, appearing primarily in contexts of textual or artistic reproduction. *Out of true* is a fixed technical expression (carpentry, engineering) where *true* functions as a noun meaning "perfect alignment." These constructions extend the core truth-domain meaning into fidelity and conformity rather than propositional correspondence.

## 13. VERBAL IDIOMS WITH TRUTH-DOMAIN PREDICATES

### Pattern
**Intransitive or copular verb + truth-domain element** in a fixed or semi-fixed phrase (*ring true*, *hold true*, *come true*, *stand corrected*)

### Examples
- *His explanation didn't quite ring true.* → His explanation did not seem genuine or plausible.
- *The same principle holds true across all cases.* → The same principle remains valid across all cases.

### Notes
English has a set of fixed verbal idioms in which a truth-domain element (*true*, *corrected*) combines with a verb to produce a meaning not fully predictable from the parts: *ring true/false*, *hold true*, *come true*, *stand corrected*, *prove true*. These are distinct from the copular attribution pattern (§1) in that the verb is not *be* and the construction is idiomatic or semi-fixed. They are concentrated around the base form *true* and have no parallel idiom set for most other truth-domain adjectives.

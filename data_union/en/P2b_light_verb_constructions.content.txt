# CLCA Protocol v3.4 — P2b Compositional Constructions
# Language: English (en)
# Layer: P2b_light_verb_constructions

---

**Note on scope:** This step documents attested multi-word constructions involving the P1 base lexical items. Derivational forms already documented in P2a are excluded. Constructions documented in P1b (root expansion) are cross-referenced rather than re-described at length. The focus is on periphrastic, idiomatic, and syntactically-driven multi-word patterns not already fully treated. Transliteration is not applicable for English (Latin script native); the transliteration field is omitted throughout.

## Item 1: *true*

---

**C1.1**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *come true*
- **English translation:** (of a hope, dream, or prediction) to be realized or fulfilled
- **Usage notes:** Used with subjects that are wishes, dreams, predictions, or fears; *her dream came true*; *the worst fears came true*. A fixed verbal idiom; *true* is not substitutable here. Noted in P1b Step 3 as a peripheral extension where the truth-domain sense recedes.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.2**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *ring true*
- **English translation:** to seem genuine, accurate, or believable; to have the quality of truth
- **Usage notes:** Used of statements, explanations, and accounts; *his explanation didn't quite ring true*. Derived from the practice of testing coins by their sound. Contrasts with *ring false* and *ring hollow*.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.3**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *hold true*
- **English translation:** to remain valid or applicable; to continue to be the case
- **Usage notes:** Used of principles, rules, and generalizations; *this observation holds true across all cases*; *the same holds true for the second group*. A semi-fixed verbal phrase. Used in general and formal registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.4**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *prove true*
- **English translation:** to turn out to be correct or accurate; to be confirmed as accurate
- **Usage notes:** Used of predictions, suspicions, and claims; *the prediction proved true*; *his fears proved true when the results were announced*. Compositional but conventionally used.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.5**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *true to one's word*
- **English translation:** keeping one's promise; doing what one said one would do
- **Usage notes:** Used predicatively; *she was true to her word and returned the money*. A fixed prepositional phrase functioning as a predicate complement. Applied to persons who fulfill commitments they have made. Used across all registers to commend reliability and integrity.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.6**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *tried and true*
- **English translation:** tested and proven reliable through experience
- **Usage notes:** Used in general registers as an attributive phrase; *they relied on tried and true methods*. Noted in P1b Step 3 as a peripheral extension predicating reliability rather than propositional truth.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.7**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *true to one's nature*
- **English translation:** behaving in accordance with one's fundamental character
- **Usage notes:** Used in general and literary registers; *he was true to his nature, always choosing the harder path*. A variant of the *true to X* pattern; compare *true to form*, *true to type* (documented in P1b).
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.8**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *as true as*
- **English translation:** just as accurate or genuine as (something used as a standard of comparison); used in comparative constructions to assert the truth of something by analogy
- **Usage notes:** Used in general and literary registers, often in fixed similes such as *as true as steel*, *as true as I'm standing here*. Common in informal and rhetorical speech. The pattern is productive but individual instances vary in frequency.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.9**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *it is true that*
- **English translation:** it is a fact that; it is the case that; a concessive or assertive construction acknowledging the truth of a proposition
- **Usage notes:** Used in general and formal registers, often as a concessive before a contrasting claim; *it is true that the evidence is limited, but the conclusion still follows*. Functions both as a straightforward assertion and as a concessive opener before a contrasting point. A highly frequent syntactic frame.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.10**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *only too true*
- **English translation:** unfortunately or regrettably accurate; regrettably and entirely accurate
- **Usage notes:** Used in general registers to acknowledge an unwelcome truth with emphasis; *that description is only too true*. The construction carries a tone of resigned acknowledgment. More emphatic than *too true* alone. Common in informal and journalistic usage.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.11**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *be true of*
- **English translation:** to apply to or be the case for (a particular subject)
- **Usage notes:** Used in general and formal registers to predicate a property of a class or individual; *this is true of most languages*. A standard predicative construction.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.12**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *the same is true of*
- **English translation:** the same applies to; the same situation or property holds for
- **Usage notes:** Used in general and academic registers as a discourse connective extending a claim to a new subject; *the same is true of the other cases examined*.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.13**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *true enough*
- **English translation:** admittedly correct; fair enough; accurate as far as it goes; sufficiently true; used to acknowledge partial or qualified truth
- **Usage notes:** Already documented in P1b (1.17). Used as a concessive or qualifying expression; *true enough, the plan had its flaws*. Functions as a discourse marker of qualified agreement. Common in argumentation and debate across all registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.14**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *too good to be true*
- **English translation:** so favorable as to be implausible or likely to be deceptive
- **Usage notes:** Used in general registers to express skepticism about an apparently advantageous situation; *the offer seemed too good to be true*. A fixed evaluative phrase.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.15**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *dream come true*
- **English translation:** something desired that has been realized
- **Usage notes:** Used in general registers as a fixed nominal phrase; *winning the award was a dream come true*. A nominalized form of *come true* (C1.1 above).
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.16**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *out of true*
- **English translation:** not straight; not properly aligned; deviating from the correct position or shape
- **Usage notes:** Used in carpentry, engineering, and mechanics; *the wheel is out of true*; *the frame has gone out of true*. Applies the root to geometric alignment rather than propositional truth. Used in engineering, carpentry, and mechanical contexts to describe a surface, wheel, or component that is not correctly aligned or level.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.17**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *the true story*
- **English translation:** the actual account of events, as opposed to a fictional or distorted version
- **Usage notes:** A fixed attributive construction used in publishing, journalism, and everyday speech to signal that a narrative is factual rather than invented. Extremely common in book and film titles and promotional contexts.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.18**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *true to say*
- **English translation:** accurate to state; correct to assert
- **Usage notes:** Used as a parenthetical or introductory phrase to acknowledge the accuracy of a statement, often before introducing a qualification; *it is true to say that progress has been made, but...*. Common in formal and journalistic writing.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.19**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *that's true*
- **English translation:** that is correct; I agree with that; used to acknowledge or agree with a statement
- **Usage notes:** A fixed conversational response used to affirm the accuracy or correctness of a preceding statement; *"The deadline is tomorrow." "That's true."* One of the most frequent truth-affirming expressions in spoken English.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.20**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *too true*
- **English translation:** unfortunately very accurate; regrettably correct
- **Usage notes:** Already documented in P1b (1.18). Cross-reference only.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.21**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *true enough, but*
- **English translation:** that is correct as far as it goes; however
- **Usage notes:** A concessive construction used to acknowledge the partial validity of a claim before introducing a counterpoint or qualification. Common in argumentation and debate across all registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.22**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *true to life*
- **English translation:** accurately representing real life; realistic
- **Usage notes:** Documented in P1b Root 2, item 18. Used in aesthetic and literary evaluation; *a true-to-life portrayal*.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.23**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *true to form*
- **English translation:** behaving in the way that is expected or typical of someone
- **Usage notes:** Documented in P1b Root 2, item 19. Used in everyday speech; *true to form, he arrived late*.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.24**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *true to type*
- **English translation:** conforming to the expected characteristics of a category or kind
- **Usage notes:** Documented in P1b Root 2, item 20. Used in biological, agricultural, and everyday contexts.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.25**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *true north*
- **English translation:** geographic north; figuratively, a reliable guiding principle or orientation
- **Usage notes:** Documented in P1b Root 2, item 21. Used in navigation and figuratively in leadership discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.26**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *true copy*
- **English translation:** an exact, certified copy of a document
- **Usage notes:** Documented in P1b Root 2, item 22. Legal and administrative term.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.27**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *true bill*
- **English translation:** a grand jury's endorsement that sufficient evidence exists to bring an indictment
- **Usage notes:** Documented in P1b Root 2, item 23. Legal term, primarily US.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.28**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *true-blue*
- **English translation:** staunchly loyal; genuinely committed to a cause or party
- **Usage notes:** Documented in P1b Root 2, item 24. Used in everyday and political English.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.29**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *true to oneself*
- **English translation:** acting in accordance with one's own values and character; not compromising one's identity
- **Usage notes:** Used in philosophical, self-help, and everyday discourse to describe a person who acts in accordance with their authentic values rather than conforming to external pressure. Related to existentialist notions of authenticity.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C1.30**
- **Base Form:** *true*
- **Example:** *ring false*
- **English translation:** to seem implausible or insincere; to lack the quality of truth
- **Usage notes:** The negative counterpart of *ring true*; *her apology rang false*. Forms a minimal pair with *ring true* and is conventionally associated with the *true* base form.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

> **Note on *true*:** Many fixed expressions involving *true* were already documented in P1b (1.7–1.30), including *true-blue*, *true-life*, *true north*, *true to life*, *true to form*, *true to type*, *come true*, *ring true*, *hold true*, *prove true*, *truth be told*, *the truth of the matter*, *moment of truth*, *ground truth*, *home truth*, *half-truth*, and *post-truth*. Those are cross-referenced rather than repeated. The constructions above supplement that inventory with additional attested patterns.

## Item 2: *truth*

---

**C2.1**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *tell the truth*
- **English translation:** to state what is actually the case; to be honest
- **Usage notes:** The most basic verbal collocation with *truth*; used in legal (*tell the truth, the whole truth*), moral, and everyday contexts. Contrasts with *lie* and *conceal*. A fundamental fixed verb-object construction used across all registers to describe the act of honest disclosure. Appears in legal contexts (*tell the truth, the whole truth*), moral instruction, and everyday speech.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.2**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *the whole truth*
- **English translation:** the complete and unabridged facts; without omission
- **Usage notes:** Used in legal and moral contexts to emphasize that nothing relevant has been withheld. Appears in the courtroom oath (*the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth*) and in everyday emphasis on complete disclosure.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.3**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *nothing but the truth*
- **English translation:** only what is true; with no admixture of falsehood
- **Usage notes:** Used in legal oaths and in everyday emphasis on strict truthfulness. Most commonly encountered as part of the full courtroom formula (*the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth*).
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.4**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *speak the truth*
- **English translation:** to say what is true; to be honest
- **Usage notes:** A slightly more formal or literary variant of *tell the truth*, used in moral, religious, and rhetorical contexts. Common in biblical and ethical discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.5**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *speaking truth to power*
- **English translation:** honestly confronting those in authority with uncomfortable facts; to challenge authority by stating uncomfortable facts or moral truths
- **Usage notes:** A fixed phrase used in political and activist discourse to describe the act of honestly challenging authority with inconvenient facts. Originated in Quaker tradition and became widely used in political commentary; *she spoke truth to power in her testimony before Congress*.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.6**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *the truth will out*
- **English translation:** the truth will eventually become known; facts cannot be permanently concealed
- **Usage notes:** A proverbial expression asserting that concealed facts will eventually come to light; *out* is used in the archaic sense of "come out, emerge"; attested from Shakespeare (*The Merchant of Venice*). Used across all registers, often as a reassurance or warning.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.7**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *in truth*
- **English translation:** actually; in fact; to be honest; in reality; truly
- **Usage notes:** Documented in P1b Root 2, item 16. A prepositional phrase used as a discourse marker to introduce a frank or corrective statement, often contrasting with appearances or prior claims; *in truth, the situation was far worse than reported*. Common in formal and literary registers.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.8**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *to tell the truth*
- **English translation:** to be honest; frankly speaking
- **Usage notes:** Used as a parenthetical or introductory phrase to signal that the speaker is about to make a candid statement, sometimes one they might otherwise hesitate to make. Common in spoken and informal written English.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.9**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *the truth hurts*
- **English translation:** accurate information is sometimes painful to hear
- **Usage notes:** A proverbial expression used to acknowledge that honest statements can be uncomfortable or unwelcome. Common in everyday speech and moral discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.10**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *stretch the truth*
- **English translation:** to exaggerate or distort facts without outright lying
- **Usage notes:** Used to describe statements that are not entirely false but that exaggerate or distort the facts. Common in everyday and journalistic usage to describe mild dishonesty that falls short of outright lying.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.11**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *bend the truth*
- **English translation:** to distort or misrepresent facts without outright lying
- **Usage notes:** Used similarly to *stretch the truth* to describe statements that distort rather than directly contradict the facts. Implies deliberate but partial misrepresentation. Common in everyday and journalistic usage.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.12**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *economical with the truth*
- **English translation:** deliberately omitting relevant facts; misleading through selective disclosure; deliberately omitting or obscuring relevant facts; misleading without technically lying
- **Usage notes:** A fixed ironic phrase used in British English, particularly in political and journalistic discourse, to describe a person or statement that misleads through omission rather than outright falsehood. Popularized in British public life through the 1986 Spycatcher trial (attributed to Cabinet Secretary Sir Robert Armstrong). Often used ironically or euphemistically.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.13**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *a grain of truth*
- **English translation:** a small element of accuracy within a largely false or exaggerated claim
- **Usage notes:** Used to acknowledge that a claim, though largely inaccurate or exaggerated, contains some factual basis. Common in everyday and journalistic discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.14**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *kernel of truth*
- **English translation:** a small but genuine element of accuracy within a larger claim
- **Usage notes:** Used similarly to *a grain of truth*, with slightly more emphasis on the genuine core element. Common in analytical and journalistic discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.15**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *the truth of the matter*
- **English translation:** the actual facts of the situation; what is actually the case; the real facts of a situation
- **Usage notes:** Already documented in P1b (1.29). Used as a nominal phrase to introduce a frank or corrective statement; *the truth of the matter is that no one knew*. A semi-fixed frame.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.16**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *truth be told*
- **English translation:** to be honest; frankly speaking
- **Usage notes:** Already documented in P1b (1.28). Documented in P1b Root 2, item 15. Functions as a sentence-initial or parenthetical discourse marker.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.17**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *gospel truth*
- **English translation:** something regarded as absolutely and unquestionably true
- **Usage notes:** Documented in P1b Root 2, item 13. Used to assert that something is completely and certainly true, with the force of religious certainty. Common in informal and emphatic speech (*I'm telling you the gospel truth*); often used ironically.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.18**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *the plain truth*
- **English translation:** the simple, unadorned facts without embellishment
- **Usage notes:** Used to emphasize that a statement is straightforwardly factual, without rhetorical elaboration or softening. Common in direct and informal speech.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.19**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *the naked truth*
- **English translation:** the unvarnished, unembellished facts
- **Usage notes:** Used to emphasize that a statement presents the facts without softening, concealment, or embellishment. More emphatic than *the plain truth*. Common in rhetorical and journalistic usage.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.20**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *the uncomfortable truth*
- **English translation:** a fact that is accurate but unwelcome or difficult to accept
- **Usage notes:** Used in journalistic, political, and everyday discourse to describe accurate information that is difficult or unpleasant to acknowledge; *face the uncomfortable truth*; *an uncomfortable truth about inequality*. A productive collocation rather than a fixed phrase.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.21**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *the inconvenient truth*
- **English translation:** an accurate fact that is unwelcome because it requires difficult action or acknowledgment; a fact that is true but creates difficulty or embarrassment for those who would prefer to ignore it
- **Usage notes:** Used in political and environmental discourse, widely associated with Al Gore's 2006 documentary *An Inconvenient Truth*. Now used more broadly to describe any accurate but unwelcome fact that challenges comfortable assumptions. The phrase existed before the documentary but gained currency through it.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.22**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *truth drug* / *truth serum*
- **English translation:** a substance believed to compel honest disclosure; a drug supposedly causing a person to tell the truth under interrogation
- **Usage notes:** Used in espionage, legal, and popular culture contexts to refer to substances (such as sodium pentothal) believed to reduce a person's ability to withhold truthful information. *Truth serum* is more common in popular usage; *truth drug* is more common in British English. The term is widely used though the efficacy of such substances is disputed.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.23**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *there's truth in*
- **English translation:** there is some accuracy in; there is a valid element to
- **Usage notes:** A construction used to acknowledge partial validity in a claim or observation; *there's truth in what you say*. Common in conversational and analytical discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.24**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *truth-telling*
- **English translation:** the practice or act of telling the truth
- **Usage notes:** Documented in P1b Root 2, item 5. Used in moral philosophy and political discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.25**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *truth-teller*
- **English translation:** a person who tells the truth; one known for honesty
- **Usage notes:** Documented in P1b Root 2, item 6. Used in moral and political discourse.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.26**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *truth value*
- **English translation:** the logical status of a proposition as true or false
- **Usage notes:** Documented in P1b Root 2, item 7. Technical term in logic and philosophy of language.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.27**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *truth table*
- **English translation:** a table showing the truth values of a compound proposition for all combinations of truth values of its components
- **Usage notes:** Documented in P1b Root 2, item 8. Standard tool in propositional logic and digital circuit design.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.28**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *truth condition*
- **English translation:** the condition under which a statement is true
- **Usage notes:** Documented in P1b Root 2, item 9. Central concept in formal semantics and philosophy of language.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.29**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *post-truth*
- **English translation:** relating to circumstances in which objective facts are less influential than appeals to emotion and personal belief
- **Usage notes:** Documented in P1b Root 2, item 30. Used in political, journalistic, and academic discourse; *Oxford Dictionaries* Word of the Year 2016.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.30**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *truth commission*
- **English translation:** an official body established to investigate and document past human rights abuses or political crimes
- **Usage notes:** Used in political science, journalism, and international law; associated with post-conflict or post-authoritarian transitions; *the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission* is the paradigm case.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.31**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *ground truth*
- **English translation:** information obtained by direct observation or measurement rather than inference; the actual state of affairs used as a reference standard
- **Usage notes:** Used in remote sensing, machine learning, and scientific methodology; *ground truth data* is used to validate models or satellite imagery. Extended to mean "the actual facts" in journalistic and everyday use.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C2.32**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *truth squad*
- **English translation:** a group assigned to monitor and rebut false or misleading claims, especially in political campaigns
- **Usage notes:** Used in US political journalism; a *truth squad* follows an opposing candidate to counter their claims in real time.

**C2.33**

- **Usage notes:** Used in philosophical and political discourse; associated with the US Declaration of Independence (*We hold these truths to be self-evident*); used more broadly for any proposition taken as obviously true.

**C2.34**

**C2.35**

- **Usage notes:** Documented in P1b Root 2, item 11. Used in everyday, journalistic, and political discourse.

**C2.36**

---

**C2.38**
- **Base Form:** *truth*
- **Example:** *speak truth to power*
- **English translation:** to challenge authority by stating uncomfortable facts or moral truths
- **Usage notes:** A fixed phrase used in political and activist discourse; associated with Quaker tradition and popularized in the 20th century; *she spoke truth to power in her testimony before Congress*.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

## Item 3: *accurate*

---

**C3.1**
- **Base Form:** *accurate*
- **Example:** *accurate to within*
- **English translation:** correct within a specified margin of error
- **Usage notes:** Used in scientific, technical, and journalistic contexts to specify the degree of precision of a measurement or instrument; *accurate to within one millimeter*; *accurate to within 0.1%*; *accurate to within three percentage points*. A standard technical construction. Common in technical and scientific writing.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C3.2**
- **Base Form:** *accurate*
- **Example:** *broadly accurate*
- **English translation:** correct in its general outlines; correct in its main features, though not in every detail
- **Usage notes:** Used to acknowledge that a description or account is substantially correct without asserting complete precision; *the description is broadly accurate*; *broadly accurate but oversimplified*. Common in journalistic and analytical writing when assessing the overall correctness of a claim.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C3.3**
- **Base Form:** *accurate*
- **Example:** *reasonably accurate*
- **English translation:** sufficiently correct for practical purposes; approximately right
- **Usage notes:** Used to indicate that something is correct within acceptable limits, without claiming complete precision; *the model provides a reasonably accurate prediction of outcomes*. Common in scientific, journalistic, and everyday contexts.
- **Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**C3.4**

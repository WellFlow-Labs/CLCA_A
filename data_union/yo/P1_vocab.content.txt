# CLCA Protocol v3.2 — P1 CORE VOCABULARY COLLECTION
# Language: Yoruba (yo)

---

**Note on script and transliteration:** Yoruba is written in a Latin-based orthography with diacritics marking tone (acute = high, grave = low, unmarked or macron = mid) and vowel quality (subdot for open vowels). The standard orthography used here follows the Yoruba Studies Association / Akin Oyètádé convention as codified in academic Yoruba linguistics and as reflected in major Yoruba dictionaries (e.g., Abraham 1958; Bamgboṣe 1966). Tones are marked on the native orthographic forms; no separate transliteration column is added where the orthography is self-representing.

## Lexical Items

---

**1.**
**Form:** òtítọ́
**Literal meaning/morphology:** Nominalized form related to the root *tọ* (to be straight, correct, proper); the *ò-* prefix nominalizes; *-tí-* may be an intensifying or aspectual element (analysis debated)
**Typical English translations:** truth, truthfulness, accuracy, correctness, fact
**Usage:** The most general and widely used term for truth in Yoruba. Used in moral, legal, religious, and everyday contexts to evaluate whether a statement corresponds to reality. Appears in proverbs, court contexts, and Christian/Islamic religious language. Participates in productive constructions such as *sọ òtítọ́* ("speak the truth"), *ọ̀nà òtítọ́* ("the way of truth"), and *ènìyàn òtítọ́* ("a truthful person"). Contrasts with *irọ́* (falsehood).
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**2.**
**Form:** irọ́
**Literal meaning/morphology:** Noun; morphological decomposition uncertain; sometimes analyzed as related to *rọ* (to be soft, yielding, to flow), but the semantic connection is not transparent; conventionally understood as the antonym of *òtítọ́*
**Typical English translations:** lie, falsehood, untruth, deception, fabrication
**Usage:** The direct antonym of *òtítọ́*; used across all registers to denote a false statement or act of deception. Used in moral condemnation of dishonest speech and in legal contexts of perjury or fraud. Appears in fixed expressions such as *sọ irọ́* ("tell a lie") and in proverbs contrasting truth and falsehood.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**3.**
**Form:** òdodo
**Literal meaning/morphology:** Nominalized form; reduplication of *do* or related to *dọ* (to be right, fitting); the reduplication may intensify; semantically associated with uprightness and moral correctness; also homophonous with *òdodo* meaning "flower/hibiscus" — context disambiguates
**Typical English translations:** justice, righteousness, fairness, rightness, rectitude
**Usage:** Used in moral, legal, and religious discourse to describe conduct or outcomes that conform to right standards. Appears in political discourse and in Yoruba translations of religious texts, including Yoruba traditional religion and Christian/Islamic usage. Distinct from *òtítọ́* in that it foregrounds normative rightness rather than factual accuracy. Contrasts with *àìdọ́gba* (inequality) and *ẹ̀tọ́* in some contexts.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**4.**
**Form:** ìmọ̀
**Literal meaning/morphology:** Nominalization of *mọ̀* (to know); *ì-* is a nominalizing prefix
**Typical English translations:** knowledge, understanding, awareness
**Usage:** Refers to the state of knowing or being informed about something. Used in epistemological and practical contexts to evaluate whether a person has accurate information. Relevant to assessments of reliability and credibility of testimony. Contrasts with *àìmọ̀* (ignorance). Appears in compounds such as *ìmọ̀ ènìyàn* ("human knowledge/understanding").
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**5.**
**Form:** àìmọ̀
**Literal meaning/morphology:** *àì-* (negation prefix) + *mọ̀* ("to know"); "not-knowing"
**Typical English translations:** ignorance, lack of knowledge, unawareness
**Usage:** Used to evaluate a person's claim or conduct as unreliable or mistaken due to lack of knowledge. Functions as a mitigating or explanatory term in moral and epistemic assessments.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**6.**
**Form:** gbàgbọ́
**Literal meaning/morphology:** Verb; *gbà* (to accept, receive) + *gbọ́* (to hear, to believe); compound meaning "to accept as heard/true"
**Typical English translations:** to believe, to trust, to have faith in
**Usage:** Describes the cognitive act of accepting a proposition or person as reliable or true. Used in both religious contexts (faith) and everyday contexts of trusting testimony. The object can be a person, statement, or claim.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**7.**
**Form:** ìgbàgbọ́
**Literal meaning/morphology:** Nominalization of *gbàgbọ́*; *ì-* nominalizing prefix; *gbà* (to receive, accept) + *gbọ́* (to hear, believe); compound nominalization
**Typical English translations:** belief, faith, trust, conviction
**Usage:** The nominal form of *gbàgbọ́*, referring to the state or act of believing. Used in religious, moral, and epistemic contexts to describe acceptance of something as true or reliable. Widely used in religious discourse (particularly Christian and Islamic) but also in secular contexts of epistemic commitment. Evaluates the reliability or credibility of what is believed. Common in Christian and Islamic Yoruba discourse as well as everyday speech.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**8.**
**Form:** ìgbẹ́kẹ̀lé
**Literal meaning/morphology:** Nominalization of *gbẹ́kẹ̀lé* (to rely on, to trust); *ì-* nominalizing prefix; compound involving *gbé* (carry/place) + *kẹ̀lé* (lean on); the verbal base may decompose further but analysis is not fully settled
**Typical English translations:** trust, reliance, dependability, reliability, confidence in
**Usage:** Refers specifically to reliance on a person or thing as dependable or trustworthy. Used in interpersonal, commercial, and moral contexts to evaluate whether a source, person, or institution is reliable. Distinct from *ìgbàgbọ́* in emphasizing practical dependability over propositional belief. A person described as having *ìgbẹ́kẹ̀lé* is considered dependable and honest. Appears in evaluative contexts such as *ènìyàn tí a lè gbẹ́kẹ̀lé* ("a person one can rely on / a reliable person").
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**9.**
**Form:** ẹ̀rí
**Literal meaning/morphology:** Noun; related to *rí* (to see); *ẹ̀-* nominalizes; thus roughly "that which is seen / evidence"
**Typical English translations:** evidence, proof, testimony, witness, sign
**Usage:** Used in legal, moral, and everyday contexts to refer to evidence that supports or establishes the truth of a claim. *Ẹ̀rí ojú* ("eye-witness evidence") is a common compound. Central to assessments of factual accuracy in judicial settings. Closely related in usage to *ìjẹ́rìísí* but more general.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**10.**
**Form:** ẹ̀rí ojú
**Literal meaning/morphology:** *ẹ̀rí* (evidence, witness) + *ojú* (eye, face); literally "eye-evidence" or "face-witness"
**Typical English translations:** eyewitness testimony, direct evidence, firsthand witness
**Usage:** A fixed expression referring to evidence based on direct visual observation. Used in legal and everyday contexts to distinguish firsthand from hearsay evidence. Considered the most reliable form of testimony in Yoruba legal and moral discourse. Carries strong epistemic weight as a marker of reliable testimony.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**11.**
**Form:** ìjẹ́rìísí
**Literal meaning/morphology:** Nominalization of *jẹ́rìí* + causative/completive suffix *-sí*; *ì-* nominalizing prefix; the verbal base relates to witnessing or attesting; possibly influenced by Arabic *shahāda* via Islamic contact, though the Yoruba form is nativized
**Typical English translations:** testimony, attestation, certification, verification, confirmation, witness, evidence, proof
**Usage:** Used in legal, religious, and administrative contexts to refer to formal attestation of truth or authenticity, and also more broadly for any form of corroborating evidence. In Islamic Yoruba usage, refers specifically to the declaration of faith. Appears in court language and in Islamic religious discourse. More formal than *ẹ̀rí*.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**12.**
**Form:** jẹ́rìí
**Literal meaning/morphology:** Verb; to bear witness, to testify, to attest; related to *ẹ̀rí* (evidence/witness); possibly from Arabic *shahida* via Islamic influence, or native derivation
**Typical English translations:** to testify, to bear witness, to attest, to certify
**Usage:** The verbal base from which *ìjẹ́rìísí* is derived. Used in legal and religious contexts to describe the act of attesting to the truth of something. Appears in Islamic religious usage (*jẹ́rìí* to the oneness of God) and in court contexts. Evaluates the speaker as a reliable source of confirmation.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**13.**
**Form:** gidi
**Literal meaning/morphology:** Adjective/adverb; morphological origin unclear; possibly a loanword or early borrowing, though this is debated; possibly related to *gidi* (solid, firm, real)
**Typical English translations:** genuine, real, authentic, actual, true (intensifying)
**Usage:** Functions as an intensifier or qualifier meaning "real" or "genuine," used to evaluate objects, persons, or states as authentic rather than imitation or pretense. Appears in constructions such as *ọrẹ gidi* ("a genuine friend"), *ìfẹ́ gidi* ("real love"), *ènìyàn gidi* ("a real/genuine person"), and *Nǹkan gidi* ("a real/genuine thing"). Widely used in colloquial and contemporary Yoruba, including urban Yoruba and Yoruba Pidgin-influenced speech.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**14.**
**Form:** òtọ́
**Literal meaning/morphology:** Adjective/noun; correctness, straightness, accuracy
**Typical English translations:** correct, right, accurate, proper
**Usage:** Used to evaluate statements, measurements, or conduct as correct or accurate. Related morphologically to *òtítọ́* but used more commonly as a predicative adjective. Appears in *ó tọ́* ("it is correct/right").
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**15.**
**Form:** tọ́
**Literal meaning/morphology:** Verb/adjective root; to be straight, correct, proper, right
**Typical English translations:** to be correct, to be right, to be proper, to be straight
**Usage:** A core evaluative root used to assess whether something conforms to a standard of correctness or propriety. Appears in numerous compounds and collocations. Used for both factual correctness (*ó tọ́* — "it is correct") and moral rightness.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**16.**
**Form:** ìtọ́ni
**Literal meaning/morphology:** *ì-* (nominalizer) + *tọ́* (to be correct, straight) + *ni* (copula/linker, or part of verbal complex); analysis of the full form is not fully settled; "directing toward correctness"
**Typical English translations:** guidance, direction, correction, instruction, correction toward what is right
**Usage:** Used in educational, moral, and advisory contexts to describe the act of directing someone toward what is correct or right, or correcting someone toward accuracy. Less frequent than *àṣìṣe* in general discourse.
**Epistemic status:** [UNCERTAIN]

---

**17.**
**Form:** àṣìṣe
**Literal meaning/morphology:** *à-* (nominalizer, often for negative or resultant states) + *ṣìṣe* (doing, action from *ṣe*, to do); thus roughly "wrong-doing" or "mis-doing"
**Typical English translations:** error, mistake, fault, wrongdoing, inaccuracy
**Usage:** Used to evaluate actions or statements as incorrect, mistaken, or morally wrong. Applies both to factual errors (inaccuracy) and to moral transgressions (fault, transgression). Contrasts with *òtọ́* and *òdodo*. Distinct from *ẹ̀ṣẹ̀* in that it does not as strongly imply moral culpability. Appears in expressions such as *ṣe àṣìṣe* ("to make a mistake / to err").
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**18.**
**Form:** ẹ̀ṣẹ̀
**Literal meaning/morphology:** Noun; sin, offense, guilt, fault
**Typical English translations:** sin, guilt, offense, wrongdoing, fault
**Usage:** Used in moral, religious, and legal contexts to evaluate conduct as a violation of a norm or law. Distinct from *àṣìṣe* in that it more strongly implies moral culpability rather than mere error. Central to Yoruba religious and ethical discourse.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**19.**
**Form:** ìwà
**Literal meaning/morphology:** *ì-* (nominalizer) + *wà* (to exist, to be); literally "the manner of being" or "existence-quality"; thus roughly "being, existence, character"
**Typical English translations:** character, conduct, nature, behavior, essence, being
**Usage:** A central term in Yoruba moral and philosophical discourse referring to a person's fundamental character or nature, which is evaluated as good (*ìwà rere*) or bad (*ìwà burúkú*). Used to assess what a person fundamentally is, as expressed through their conduct, not merely what they do on the surface. Widely attested across registers, proverbs, and genres.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**20.**
**Form:** ìwà rere
**Literal meaning/morphology:** *ìwà* (character, nature) + *rere* (good, fine, excellent); fixed attributive expression
**Typical English translations:** good character, virtue, moral goodness, integrity, moral excellence
**Usage:** A fixed and highly frequent expression that is the central concept of Yoruba ethics, referring to the possession of genuine moral goodness as a fundamental quality of a person. *Ìwà rere* is treated in Yoruba ethical thought as the foundational virtue, the basis of all other moral evaluations. Used to evaluate persons against the highest moral standard. Widely attested in proverbs, philosophical discourse, religious texts, and everyday moral instruction.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**21.**
**Form:** ìwà burúkú
**Literal meaning/morphology:** *ìwà* (character) + *burúkú* (bad, evil, wicked); fixed attributive expression
**Typical English translations:** bad character, wickedness, moral corruption, vice
**Usage:** The standard antonym of *ìwà rere*; used to evaluate a person as fundamentally morally deficient or harmful. Appears in moral condemnation and in proverbs contrasting good and bad conduct. Evaluates the fundamental nature of a person rather than isolated acts. Widely attested across all registers and genres.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**22.**
**Form:** ìwà búburú
**Literal meaning/morphology:** *ìwà* (character, nature) + *búburú* (bad, evil, wicked); "bad character"
**Typical English translations:** bad character, wickedness, moral corruption, vice
**Usage:** The antonym of *ìwà rere*, used to evaluate a person as fundamentally morally deficient. Appears in moral condemnation and in proverbs contrasting good and bad conduct. Evaluates the fundamental nature of a person rather than isolated acts.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**23.**
**Form:** ojúlówó
**Literal meaning/morphology:** *ojú* (face, surface, eye) + *lówó* (in the hand of / possessing); compound; roughly "having a face" or "face-possessing," by extension "genuine, having real substance"
**Typical English translations:** genuine, authentic, real, legitimate
**Usage:** Used to evaluate objects, persons, or relationships as authentic and not counterfeit or superficial. Contrasts with imitation or pretense. Appears in commercial, social, and moral contexts: *ènìyàn ojúlówó* ("a genuine/authentic person"), *ohun ojúlówó* ("a genuine/real thing").
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**24.**
**Form:** ìtànjẹ́
**Literal meaning/morphology:** *ì-* (nominalizer) + *tànjẹ́* (to deceive, to defraud); verbal base *tàn* (to deceive) + *jẹ* (to eat/consume, here in a serial verb construction implying harm or gain)
**Typical English translations:** deception, fraud, deceit, trickery, misrepresentation
**Usage:** Refers to deliberate deception or misrepresentation, often with an implication of material or moral harm to the deceived party. Applied in legal, moral, and commercial contexts. Implies intentional misrepresentation, distinguishing it from mere error (*àṣìṣe*). Closely related to *irọ́* but carries a stronger connotation of intentional manipulation.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**25.**
**Form:** àgàbàgebè
**Literal meaning/morphology:** Morphological decomposition uncertain; the form may be reduplicated or compounded; origin debated
**Typical English translations:** pretense, hypocrisy, sham, insincerity, fake behavior
**Usage:** Used to evaluate a person's conduct or presentation as insincere or performatively false — the opposite of genuine behavior. Implies that the outward appearance does not correspond to inner reality. Used in moral criticism of hypocritical behavior. Appears in social criticism, proverb-like expressions, and literary and colloquial Yoruba.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**26.**
**Form:** ìfarahàn
**Literal meaning/morphology:** *ì-* (nominalizer) + *farahan* (to appear, to manifest, to reveal oneself); *fara* (body) + *hàn* (to show/appear)
**Typical English translations:** appearance, manifestation, revelation, showing; what something shows itself to be
**Usage:** Used in contexts evaluating whether something has been genuinely revealed or made apparent. Can carry the sense of authentic self-disclosure as opposed to concealment. Used in religious and philosophical contexts. The gap between *ìfarahàn* and underlying reality is a recurrent theme in Yoruba proverbial thought.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**27.**
**Form:** ìmúrasílẹ̀
**Literal meaning/morphology:** Nominalization of *múrasílẹ̀* ("to prepare oneself, to make ready"); compound of *mú* (take/make) + *ara* (body/self) + *sílẹ̀* (down/ready)
**Typical English translations:** preparation, readiness, making ready
**Usage:** Used in evaluative contexts where readiness or proper preparation is a criterion of adequacy or correctness. Less central to truth-evaluation than other items here; included as it appears in assessments of whether something is properly constituted.
**Epistemic status:** [UNCERTAIN]

---

**28.**
**Form:** ìdánilójú
**Literal meaning/morphology:** *ì-* (nominalizer) + *dá* (make/establish) + *ni* (person/one) + *ojú* (eye/certainty); "making one certain / establishing certainty in the eye"; alternatively analyzed as *dán* (to test/prove) + *ilé* (contracted) + *ojú* (eye/face); "establishing in someone's eyes"
**Typical English translations:** assurance, confirmation, verification, convincing, proof, convincing evidence
**Usage:** Refers to the act of convincing or providing proof to someone, establishing something as true or reliable in their perception. Used in persuasive, legal, commercial, and interpersonal contexts where certainty about truth or reliability is at issue. Evaluates the sufficiency of evidence to produce conviction.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**29.**
**Form:** ìdánwò
**Literal meaning/morphology:** *ì-* (nominalizer) + *dán* (to test, to try) + *wò* (to look at, examine); compound nominalization meaning roughly "testing and examining"
**Typical English translations:** test, trial, examination, proof (by testing), verification
**Usage:** Used in contexts where the truth, quality, or reliability of something is established through testing or examination. Applies to persons, objects, and claims. Appears in academic, scientific, educational, legal, religious, and evaluative contexts. Evaluates whether something meets a standard through systematic examination.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

**30.**
**Form:** ìfọ̀rọ̀wánilẹ́nu
**Literal meaning/morphology:** Nominalization; *fọ̀rọ̀* (question/inquiry) + *wá* (seek) + *ni* (person) + *lẹ́nu* (in mouth); "seeking inquiry from a person's mouth"
**Typical English translations:** interrogation, questioning, interview, inquiry
**Usage:** Used in legal and investigative contexts to describe formal questioning aimed at establishing the truth of a matter. Evaluates the process by which testimony or information is extracted and assessed.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**31.**
**Form:** ìfọ̀rọ̀wánilẹ́nuwò
**Literal meaning/morphology:** *ì-* + *fọ̀rọ̀* (to question/inquire) + *wá* (to come/seek) + *ni* (person) + *lẹ́nu* (in the mouth) + *wò* (to look/examine); "examining what comes from a person's mouth"
**Typical English translations:** interrogation, cross-examination, inquiry, interview
**Usage:** Refers to the formal act of questioning someone to elicit truthful information or to test the accuracy of their account. Used in legal and investigative contexts. Evaluates the reliability of testimony through systematic questioning.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**32.**
**Form:** ìdájọ́
**Literal meaning/morphology:** *ì-* (nominalizer) + *dá* (to create, establish, judge, pronounce) + *jọ́* (judgment, verdict, resemblance, or verbal extension); "establishing judgment" or "pronouncing judgment"
**Typical English translations:** judgment, verdict, ruling, adjudication
**Usage:** Used in legal and moral contexts to refer to the formal evaluation and determination of right and wrong, guilt and innocence, or the truth of a matter. Central to Yoruba legal discourse. Participates in compounds such as *ilé ìdájọ́* ("court of law"). Also appears in religious discourse about divine judgment.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**33.**
**Form:** dọ̀gba
**Literal meaning/morphology:** Verb/adjective; to be equal, to match, to correspond
**Typical English translations:** equal, equivalent, matching, corresponding
**Usage:** Used in evaluative contexts where the criterion is correspondence or equivalence between two things — including resemblance, proportionality, or fair equivalence. Appears in assessments of fairness and in comparisons of representations to originals.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**34.**
**Form:** ìjọra
**Literal meaning/morphology:** *ì-* (nominalizer) + *jọra* (to resemble one another); *jọ* (to resemble) + *ra* (reflexive/reciprocal element)
**Typical English translations:** resemblance, similarity, likeness
**Usage:** Used to evaluate the degree to which one thing resembles another — relevant in aesthetic, legal (e.g., identification), representational, and evaluative contexts where resemblance to an original or standard is at issue. Distinct from *dọ̀gba* in that it concerns likeness rather than strict equality. Appears in discussions of portraiture, imitation, and comparison.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**35.**
**Form:** tọ̀nà
**Literal meaning/morphology:** Possibly *tọ* (straight, correct) + *nà* (path/way); compound analysis uncertain; literally "straight path" or "correct way"
**Typical English translations:** right path, correct way, proper conduct, correct path
**Usage:** Used in moral, advisory, and religious contexts to describe adherence to the correct or proper course of action or belief. Evaluates conduct or orientation against a standard of what is right or proper. Common in Islamic and Christian religious discourse in Yoruba. Attested in proverb literature and elder speech. Less common in younger urban registers.
**Epistemic status:** [UNCERTAIN]

---

**36.**
**Form:** àṣà
**Literal meaning/morphology:** Noun; morphological decomposition uncertain; sometimes analyzed as related to *ṣe* (to do) with a nominalizing prefix, or as *à-* prefix + *ṣà* (to choose, select), but neither analysis is fully settled
**Typical English translations:** custom, tradition, cultural norm, established practice
**Usage:** Refers to established cultural practices or norms against which conduct is evaluated for propriety or correctness. Used in assessments of whether behavior conforms to recognized standards. Relevant to evaluations of rightness in cultural, social, aesthetic, and moral contexts.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**37.**
**Form:** ẹ̀tọ́
**Literal meaning/morphology:** Nominalized form of *tọ* (to be straight, right, correct); *ẹ̀-* is a nominalizing prefix; "that which is right/correct"
**Typical English translations:** right, entitlement, correctness, what is due
**Usage:** Used in legal, moral, and social contexts to refer to what is rightfully owed or correct, and also to what is proper. *Ẹ̀tọ́ ènìyàn* ("human rights") is a common modern collocation. Appears in expressions such as *ẹ̀tọ́ rẹ ni* ("it is your right/it is correct for you"). Evaluates whether a claim, action, or allocation conforms to a standard of rightness or entitlement. Participates in productive nominal constructions.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

**38.**

**Typical English translations:** satisfaction, sufficiency, adequacy, fulfillment
**Usage:** Used to evaluate whether something meets a required standard or is sufficient for a purpose. Relevant in assessments of whether evidence, conduct, or an object is adequate or genuinely fulfills what is expected. Less directly epistemic than some other items but used in evaluative contexts.

**Typical English translations:** consistency, conformity, correspondence, agreement
**Usage:** Refers to the quality of being consistent or conforming to a standard or to itself. Used to evaluate whether a statement, account, or object is internally consistent or corresponds to what it should be. Relevant in logical and legal assessments of coherence.

**40.**

**Typical English translations:** guidance, direction, correct instruction, proper guidance
**Usage:** Refers to the act of directing someone correctly or providing accurate guidance. Used in religious, educational, and moral contexts to evaluate whether instruction or direction conforms to what is right or true. Relevant to assessments of reliable guidance.

**43.**

**Typical English translations:** authenticity, genuineness (in some dialects and registers); also "thought," "device"
**Usage:** This form is polysemous and context-dependent; its use specifically in the sense of genuineness or authenticity is attested but not universal across dialects. Caution is warranted in assigning a stable "authenticity" reading.

**44.**
**Form:** ìpìlẹ̀
**Literal meaning/morphology:** *ì-* (nominalizer) + *pilẹ̀* (to found, establish at the base); *pí* + *ilẹ̀* (ground/earth); roughly "the founding/grounding"
**Typical English translations:** foundation, basis, origin, fundamental nature
**Usage:** Used to refer to the foundational or essential nature of something — what something

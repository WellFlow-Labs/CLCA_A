# CLCA Protocol v3.2 — P3 NATURAL SEMANTIC GROUPING
# Language: Yoruba (yo)

---

## Preliminary Note on Method

Groupings below are derived from patterns of co-occurrence, contrast, and contextual restriction observed across the P1 and P1b data and the constructions documented in P2b. No external semantic framework has been imposed. Where the data supports overlap, this is noted. Where items resist grouping, this is stated explicitly. The number of groups and their sizes reflect the data, not a desire for symmetry.

---

## Group 1: Expressions used in asserting or denying that a statement is true

**Definition:** These expressions appear in contexts where a speaker is making, supporting, or challenging a claim about whether something said or reported corresponds to what actually happened or is the case. They are used in legal testimony, everyday dispute, and moral instruction, and they contrast with one another along the dimension of affirmation versus denial. The core contrast in this group is between *sọ òtítọ́* and *sọ irọ́*.

**Representative expressions:**

- *òtítọ́* — "truth, truthfulness"; the primary term for what a statement is when it corresponds to reality; appears in *sọ òtítọ́* ("speak the truth"), *òtítọ́ ni* ("it is the truth"), *kò sí òtítọ́ nínú ọ̀rọ̀ rẹ̀* ("there is no truth in his/her words")
- *irọ́* — "lie, falsehood"; the standard antonym of *òtítọ́*; appears in *sọ irọ́* ("tell a lie"), *kò sí irọ́ nínú rẹ̀* ("there is no lie in it"), *irọ́ ní ẹsẹ̀ kúkúrú* ("a lie has short legs")
- *dájúdájú* — "certainly, definitely, without doubt"; used as an epistemic intensifier to assert that a claim is definitely true; appears in *dájúdájú, òtítọ́ ni* ("certainly, it is the truth"), *mo mọ̀ dájúdájú pé...* ("I know for certain that...")
- *ìjẹ́ òtítọ́* — "acknowledgment of the truth, admission of truth"; used when a party formally accepts that a statement is true, particularly in legal and confessional contexts
- *ìwádìí òtítọ́* — "investigation of the truth, fact-finding"; used in legal and journalistic contexts for the process of establishing what is true

**Observations:** *dájúdájú* functions specifically as an intensifier within this group — it does not name truth or falsehood but marks the speaker's certainty about a claim. *ìjẹ́ òtítọ́* and *ìwádìí òtítọ́* are compound expressions that presuppose the existence of truth as something that can be admitted or sought; they appear at the procedural end of truth-assertion contexts rather than in simple first-person assertion.

## Group 2: Expressions used in legal and formal proceedings to establish what happened

**Definition:** These expressions cluster in court language, formal testimony, and administrative verification contexts. They are used when a party is required to produce, evaluate, or formally accept evidence about events or facts. They are distinguished from Group 1 in that they focus on the institutional process of establishing truth rather than on the act of asserting it in everyday speech.

**Representative expressions:**

- *ẹ̀rí* — "evidence, proof, sign"; the primary term for anything that supports a claim in legal or evaluative contexts; appears in *mú ẹ̀rí wá* ("bring evidence"), *kò sí ẹ̀rí tó tó* ("there is no sufficient evidence"), *ẹ̀rí wà tó fi hàn pé...* ("there is evidence that shows that...")
- *ẹ̀rí ojú* — "eyewitness evidence, direct visual evidence"; a specific type of evidence considered particularly strong; appears in *ẹ̀rí ojú ló pọ̀ jù* ("eyewitness evidence is the strongest")
- *ẹ̀rí ìgbọ́* — "hearsay evidence, evidence based on hearing"; distinguished from *ẹ̀rí ojú* in legal contexts
- *ẹ̀rí àkọsílẹ̀* — "documentary evidence, written evidence"; evidence in the form of written records
- *ẹlẹ́rìí* — "witness, one who bears evidence"; the person who provides *ẹ̀rí*; appears in *ẹlẹ́rìí ojú* ("eyewitness")
- *ìjẹ́rìísí* — "testimony, attestation, witness"; used for formal attestation in court and religious contexts; appears in *wọ́n béèrè ìjẹ́rìísí lọ́wọ́ rẹ̀* ("they requested testimony from him/her"), *kò sí ìjẹ́rìísí tó tó* ("there is no sufficient testimony")
- *jẹ́rìísí* — verbal base: "to testify, to attest"; appears in *ó jẹ́rìísí pé ó wà níbẹ̀* ("he/she testified that he/she was there")
- *ìdánwò* — "test, trial, examination, verification"; used when something or someone is subjected to a process of checking; appears in *wọ́n dán an wò* ("they tested him/her/it"), *ó kọjá ìdánwò náà* ("he/she passed the test")
- *ìdájọ́* — "judgment, verdict, ruling"; the outcome of a formal evaluative process; appears in *ìdájọ́ náà tọ́* ("the judgment is correct"), *ó dúró dè ìdájọ́* ("he/she is awaiting judgment")
- *ìfọwọ́sí* — "endorsement, confirmation, ratification, authentication"; the formal act of officially confirming something; appears in *wọ́n fọwọ́ sí àdéhùn náà* ("they ratified the agreement"), *kò tíì gba ìfọwọ́sí* ("it has not yet received endorsement")
- *ẹ̀rí ìdánimọ̀* — "proof of identity, identification evidence"; used in administrative and security contexts

**Observations:** *ẹ̀rí* and *ìjẹ́rìísí* overlap considerably in this group but are distinguishable: *ẹ̀rí* refers to any evidence or sign (including physical traces), while *ìjẹ́rìísí* refers specifically to personal attestation or formal certification. *ìdánwò* and *ìdájọ́* represent sequential stages in a formal process — testing precedes judgment — and both appear in the same institutional contexts. *ìfọwọ́sí* marks the endpoint of formal confirmation and is more administrative than evidential.

## Group 3: Expressions used to evaluate whether a person or thing is what it claims or appears to be

**Definition:** These expressions are used when the question is not whether a statement is true but whether a person, object, or entity is genuine, authentic, or what it presents itself as. They appear in commercial, social, and moral evaluation contexts, and they contrast with expressions for pretense or imitation. The central contrast in this group is between *ojúlówó* / *gidi* on one side and *àgàbàgebè* / *àdàwọ̀* on the other.

**Representative expressions:**

- *ojúlówó* — "genuine, authentic, real, legitimate"; used of persons and objects; appears in *ènìyàn ojúlówó* ("a genuine person"), *ohun ojúlówó* ("a genuine thing"), *èyí ni ohun ojúlówó, kì í ṣe àdàwọ̀* ("this is the genuine article, it is not a counterfeit")
- *gidi* — "real, genuine, actual"; used as a post-nominal intensifier; appears in *ọ̀rẹ́ gidi* ("a true friend"), *ènìyàn gidi* ("a real person"), *èyí ni ohun gidi* ("this is the real thing")
- *àgàbàgebè* — "pretense, sham, fake behavior, hypocrisy"; used when conduct or presentation is judged to be false or performative; appears in *ìwà rẹ̀ jẹ́ àgàbàgebè* ("his/her conduct is pretense"), *ó ń ṣe àgàbàgebè* ("he/she is acting hypocritically")
- *ìfarahàn* — "appearance, manifestation, how something presents itself"; used in contexts where surface presentation is evaluated against underlying reality; appears in *ìfarahàn rẹ̀ kò dára, ṣùgbọ́n ìwà rẹ̀ dára* ("his/her appearance is not good, but his/her character is good")
- *ìjọra* — "resemblance, similarity, likeness"; used when evaluating how closely something resembles an original or standard; appears in *ìjọra wà láàárín àwọn méjì* ("there is resemblance between the two"), *ó jọ rẹ̀ gan-an* ("it resembles it exactly"), *kò sí ìjọra kankan* ("there is no resemblance at all")

**Observations:** *ojúlówó* and *gidi* are near-synonyms in this group but differ in register: *gidi* is more colloquial and informal, while *ojúlówó* appears across formal and informal registers. *ìfarahàn* occupies a distinctive position — it names the surface presentation itself rather than evaluating it as genuine or false; it is the thing that *àgàbàgebè* manipulates and that *ojúlówó* transcends. *ìjọra* is used specifically when resemblance to an original is the evaluative criterion, as in disputes about counterfeit goods or imitation.

## Group 4: Expressions used in evaluating a person's fundamental character or conduct

**Definition:** These expressions are used when the evaluation concerns not a specific statement or object but a person's underlying nature, habitual conduct, or moral quality. They are central to Yoruba moral discourse and appear in proverbs, parenting, religious instruction, and social assessment. The primary axis in this group is between *ìwà rere* and *ìwà burúkú*, with several more specific evaluative expressions clustering around each pole.

**Representative expressions:**

- *ìwà* — "character, nature, being, conduct, essence"; the base term for a person's fundamental nature; appears in *ìwà rẹ̀ ti hàn* ("his/her character has been revealed"), *ìwà ẹni ló ń ṣe é* ("it is a person's character that defines them")
- *ìwà rere* — "good character, virtue, moral goodness"; the foundational positive evaluation; appears in *ìwà rere ló ń gbani lọ́wọ́* ("it is good character that saves one")
- *ìwà burúkú* — "bad character, wickedness, moral corruption"; the standard negative evaluation; appears in *ìwà burúkú ló pa á* ("it is bad character that destroyed him/her")
- *ìwà pẹ̀lẹ́* — "gentle character, gentleness, mild and careful conduct"; a specific positive quality within the *ìwà rere* domain; appears in *ìwà pẹ̀lẹ́ ló yẹ ọmọlúwàbí* ("gentle character befits a person of good breeding")
- *ọmọlúwàbí* — "a person of good character and proper upbringing"; the term for the ideal person who embodies good character; appears in *ìwà ọmọlúwàbí* ("the character of a well-bred, virtuous person")
- *ìwà ìdúróṣinṣin* — "steadfast character, integrity, firmness of conduct"; used in praise of leaders and in religious discourse about faithfulness
- *ìwà ìbàjẹ́* — "corrupt conduct, moral corruption, depraved character"; used in political and moral criticism
- *ìwà àìtọ́* — "incorrect conduct, improper character, wrongful behavior"; used in moral and legal evaluation; notable as a compound that brings together the *ìwà* and *tọ* roots
- *ìwà àgàbàgebè* — "hypocritical conduct, pretentious character"; used in social and moral criticism
- *òdodo* — "justice, righteousness, fairness"; used in moral, legal, and religious contexts to describe conduct or outcomes that conform to what is right; appears in *ṣe òdodo fún gbogbo ènìyàn* ("do justice to all people"), *ó ń wá òdodo* ("he/she is seeking justice")
- *àṣìṣe* — "error, mistake, fault, wrongdoing"; used when conduct or a statement deviates from what is correct; appears in *ṣe àṣìṣe* ("to make a mistake"), *jẹ́wọ́ àṣìṣe rẹ* ("admit your mistake"), *kò ṣe àṣìṣe kankan* ("he/she made no mistake at all")

**Observations:** *ìwà* is the organizing term of this group — nearly all other expressions in it are either compounds of *ìwà* or terms that appear in the same evaluative contexts. *òdodo* participates in this group but also has a distinct legal-procedural use (see Group 2 overlap below). *àṣìṣe* is used both for moral fault and for epistemic error (incorrect knowledge or judgment), placing it at the boundary between this group and Group 1. *ìwà àìtọ́* is notable for explicitly combining the character domain (*ìwà*) with the correctness domain (*tọ*), suggesting that in Yoruba usage these two domains are not fully separate.

## Group 5: Expressions used in asserting or evaluating entitlement and what is due

**Definition:** These expressions are used when the question is whether someone is entitled to something, whether a claim or allocation is correct or proper, or whether a right has been granted, denied, or violated. They appear in legal, civic, and political discourse and are built primarily on the *ẹ̀tọ́* + [domain noun] pattern. They are distinguished from Group 1 (truth of statements) and Group 4 (character evaluation) by their focus on normative entitlement rather than factual accuracy or personal virtue.

**Representative expressions:**

- *ẹ̀tọ́* — "right, entitlement, correctness, what is due"; the base term; appears in *ẹ̀tọ́ rẹ ni* ("it is your right"), *wọ́n kọ ẹ̀tọ́ rẹ̀* ("they denied his/her right"), *gbà ẹ̀tọ́ rẹ* ("claim your right"), *kò sí ẹ̀tọ́ rẹ̀ nínú rẹ̀* ("he/she has no right to it")
- *ẹ̀tọ́ ènìyàn* — "human rights"; the standard expression in legal and civic discourse
- *ẹ̀tọ́ òfin* — "legal right, right under the law"
- *ẹ̀tọ́ ìdìbò* — "right to vote, voting rights"
- *ẹ̀tọ́ ìkẹ́kọ̀ọ́* — "right to education"
- *ẹ̀tọ́ ìgbésí ayé* — "right to life"

**Observations:** This group is morphologically and syntactically coherent — it is generated by a single productive pattern (*ẹ̀tọ́* + domain noun). The base term *ẹ̀tọ́* shares its root (*tọ*) with *òtítọ́* (truth) and *ìtọ́ni* (correction/guidance), but its usage in this group is distinct: it concerns normative entitlement rather than factual accuracy. The constructions in this group (*wọ́n kọ ẹ̀tọ́ rẹ̀*, *gbà ẹ̀tọ́ rẹ*) are specifically about the granting, denial, and claiming of rights, not about the truth of propositions.

## Group 6: Expressions used in describing the basis or grounds for knowing something

**Definition:** These expressions appear in contexts where the question is what justifies a claim to knowledge — what someone has seen, heard, experienced, or been told. They are used in testimony, philosophical discourse, and everyday assertions of knowledge. They are distinguished from Group 2 (formal legal evidence) by their broader application to everyday epistemic contexts and from Group 1 (truth-assertion) by their focus on the source or basis of knowledge rather than its content.

**Representative expressions:**

- *ìmọ̀* — "knowledge, understanding, awareness"; the primary term for the state of knowing; appears in *ó ní ìmọ̀ nípa ọ̀rọ̀ náà* ("he/she has knowledge of the matter"), *kò ní ìmọ̀ nípa rẹ̀* ("he/she has no knowledge of it"), *ìmọ̀ kò ní òpin* ("knowledge has no end")
- *àìmọ̀* — "ignorance, lack of knowledge"; the negated form; the standard antonym of *ìmọ̀* in epistemic contexts
- *ìgbàgbọ́* — "belief, faith, trust, conviction"; used when the basis for a claim is acceptance or trust rather than direct knowledge; appears in *ó ní ìgbàgbọ́ pé òtítọ́ ni* ("he/she believes that it is the truth"), *kò gbàgbọ́ pé òtítọ́ ni* ("he/she does not believe that it is true")
- *ìrírí* — "experience, direct observation, what one has seen/lived through"; used as a basis for knowledge claims; appears in *ìrírí mi ni* ("it is my experience / I have seen it myself"), *ìrírí ìgbésí ayé* ("life experience")
- *ẹ̀rí inú* — "inner evidence, evidence of the heart, conscience as witness"; used in moral and religious discourse for the inner sense that bears witness to one's own conduct
- *rí i gbangba* — "to see it clearly, to witness it openly"; used to assert direct and unambiguous observation as the basis for a claim

**Observations:** *ìmọ̀* and *ìgbàgbọ́* are distinguished in usage: *ìmọ̀* is used for knowledge that is or could be verified, while *ìgbàgbọ́* is used for conviction that rests on acceptance or faith, including religious faith. *ìrírí* is used specifically when direct personal experience is the basis for a claim, and it is treated in the data as a particularly strong epistemic ground. *ẹ̀rí inú* is a specialized expression that extends the evidential vocabulary of Group 2 into the domain of inner moral awareness; it overlaps with Group 2 in form but with Group 4 in context.

---

## Group 7: Expressions used in describing trust and reliability between persons

**Definition:** These expressions are used when evaluating whether a person can be relied upon, whether trust has been established or broken, and whether confidence in a person is warranted. They appear in social, moral, and institutional evaluation contexts. They are distinguished from Group 4 (character evaluation generally) by their specific focus on the relational dimension of reliability — whether one person can depend on another.

**Representative expressions:**

- *ìgbẹ́kẹ̀lé* — "trust, reliability, dependability, confidence in"; the primary term; appears in *mo gbẹ́kẹ̀lé rẹ* ("I trust you"), *ìgbẹ́kẹ̀lé rẹ̀ ti fọ́* ("trust in him/her has broken"), *kò tọ́ láti gbẹ́kẹ̀lé rẹ̀* ("it is not right to trust him/her"), *ó jẹ́ ènìyàn tí a lè gbẹ́kẹ̀lé* ("he/she is a person one can trust")
- *àìgbẹ́kẹ̀lé* — "unreliability, untrustworthiness"; the negated form
- *ìgbàgbọ́* — "belief, faith, trust, conviction"; overlaps with this group when used in interpersonal trust contexts rather than propositional belief contexts; appears in *ìgbàgbọ́ rẹ̀ lágbára* ("his/her faith/belief is strong"), *ìgbàgbọ́ rẹ̀ ti wó* ("his/her faith has collapsed")
- *ènìyàn tí a lè gbẹ́kẹ̀lé* — "a person one can rely on, a reliable person"; the standard relative-clause construction for describing trustworthiness

**Observations:** *ìgbẹ́kẹ̀lé* and *ìgbàgbọ́* overlap in this group when both are used in interpersonal trust contexts, but they are distinguishable: *ìgbẹ́kẹ̀lé* focuses on the reliability of a person or institution as something that can be depended upon, while *ìgbàgbọ́* focuses on the internal state of the person who trusts or believes. The construction *ìgbẹ́kẹ̀lé rẹ̀ ti fọ́* ("trust has broken") and *ìgbàgbọ́ rẹ̀ ti wó* ("faith has collapsed") are structurally parallel and appear in similar contexts of betrayal or disillusionment.

## Group 8: Expressions used in describing deliberate deception or misrepresentation

**Definition:** These expressions are used when the evaluation concerns not merely that something is false but that someone has intentionally created a false impression, often with harmful intent. They appear in legal, moral, and social criticism contexts and are distinguished from *irọ́* (simple falsehood) by their emphasis on intentionality and manipulation.

**Representative expressions:**

- *ìtànjẹ́* — "deception, fraud, trickery, misrepresentation"; the primary term for deliberate deception; appears in *ó tànjẹ́ mi* ("he/she deceived me"), *wọ́n fi ìtànjẹ́ gbà owó rẹ̀* ("they used deception to take his/her money"), *ìtànjẹ́ kò ní gbádùn* ("deception will not prosper"), *ó ń tàn wọ́n jẹ* ("he/she is deceiving them")
- *àgàbàgebè* — "pretense, sham, fake behavior, hypocrisy"; used when the deception is social and performative rather than material; appears in *ó ń ṣe àgàbàgebè* ("he/she is acting hypocritically"), *ìwà rẹ̀ jẹ́ àgàbàgebè* ("his/her conduct is pretense")
- *irọ́* — "lie, falsehood"; participates in this group when used in contexts of deliberate deception, though it also appears in Group 1 for simple false statements

**Observations:** *ìtànjẹ́* and *àgàbàgebè* are distinguished in usage: *ìtànjẹ́* typically involves material harm or gain (fraud, swindling), while *àgàbàgebè* describes social performance — presenting oneself as virtuous or sincere when one is not. *irọ́* is the most general term and can appear in both groups; *ìtànjẹ́* and *àgàbàgebè* are more specific. The construction *wọ́n fi ìtànjẹ́ gbà owó rẹ̀* ("they used deception to take his/her money") is not attested with *irọ́* or *àgàbàgebè*, suggesting that *ìtànjẹ́* is specifically associated with instrumental deception for material ends.

## Ambiguous and Overlapping Items

The following items appear in multiple groups or resist clean assignment:

**ìwà àìtọ́** ("incorrect conduct, improper character"): This compound appears in Group 4 (character evaluation) but explicitly combines the *ìwà* root (character) with the *tọ* root (correctness), suggesting that in Yoruba usage the evaluation of character and the evaluation of correctness are not fully separate domains. It could also be placed in a boundary zone between Group 4 and Group 1.

**àṣìṣe** ("error, mistake, fault, wrongdoing"): This term appears in Group 4 (character/conduct evaluation) but is also used in epistemic contexts (incorrect knowledge, factual error) that align with Group 1. The construction *àṣìṣe ìmọ̀* ("error of knowledge") explicitly places it in the epistemic domain. It is not clear from the data whether Yoruba speakers treat these as the same concept or as two uses of the same word.

**òdodo** ("justice, righteousness, fairness"): This term appears in Group 4 (moral character evaluation) and also in Group 2 (formal proceedings), where it describes outcomes that conform to what is right. The constructions *ṣe òdodo fún gbogbo ènìyàn* ("do justice to all people") and *ó ń wá òdodo* ("he/she is seeking justice") suggest both a personal virtue dimension and a procedural-outcome dimension.

**ìgbàgbọ́** ("belief, faith, trust, conviction"): This term appears in Group 6 (basis for knowing) when used for propositional belief and in Group 7 (trust and reliability) when used for interpersonal trust or religious faith. The data does not provide a clear basis for treating these as distinct senses versus a single unified concept.

**ẹ̀rí inú** ("inner evidence, evidence of the heart, conscience as witness"): This compound uses the vocabulary of Group 2 (legal evidence) but appears in moral and religious contexts that align with Group 4 (character evaluation) and Group 6 (basis for knowing). It is not clearly assignable to any single group.

**ìpìlẹ̀** ("foundation, basis, origin, fundamental nature"): This term appears in the P1 data as relevant to questions about the true or essential nature of things, but its usage in the P2 data is limited to compound expressions (*ìpìlẹ̀ òtítọ́*, *ìpìlẹ̀ ìwà*) that are marked [UNCERTAIN]. It is not clearly assignable to any group on the basis of the available data.

**ìtọ́ni** ("correction, guidance, direction toward what is right") and **tọ̀nà** ("correct path, right way"): Both are marked [UNCERTAIN] in the P1 data and appear in moral and advisory contexts. They share the *tọ* root with Group 5 (*ẹ̀tọ́*) and Group 4 (*ìwà àìtọ́*) but do not appear in the same constructions as either. They are not assigned to any group.

**ẹ̀rọ̀** ("authenticity, genuineness" — [UNCERTAIN]): This item is marked [UNCERTAIN] in P1 and its usage in the authenticity sense is not confirmed across dialects. It is not assigned to any group.

---

## Items That Do Not Fit Cleanly into Any Group

**àṣà** ("custom, tradition, established practice, norm"): This term appears in the P1 data as a standard against which conduct and objects are evaluated, and in P2b in constructions such as *àṣà wa ni bẹ́ẹ̀* ("that is our custom") and *ó lòdì sí àṣà wa* ("it goes against our custom"). Its role in the data is as a reference point or criterion for evaluation rather than as an evaluative term itself. It does not cluster naturally with any of the groups above, though it is invoked in contexts relevant to Groups 3 and 4.

**ìjọra** ("resemblance, similarity, likeness"): This term is assigned to Group 3 above, but it is worth noting that its usage is more specific than the other Group 3 terms — it is used when the question is degree of resemblance to an original, not simply whether something is genuine or fake. It could be treated as a peripheral member of Group 3 or as a standalone term.

**ìtọ́jú** and its compounds (*ìtọ́jú àrùn*, *ìtọ́jú ọmọ*): These terms share the *tọ* root with several items in Groups 1, 4, and 5, but their usage is in the domain of physical care and maintenance rather than truth, character, or entitlement. They are noted in P1b as peripheral extensions of the *tọ* root. They are not assigned to any group.

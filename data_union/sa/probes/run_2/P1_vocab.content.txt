# CLCA Protocol v3.2 — P1 CORE VOCABULARY COLLECTION
# Language: Sanskrit (sa)

Transliteration standard: IAST (International Alphabet of Sanskrit Transliteration), applied consistently throughout.

---

**1.**
**Form:** सत्य
**Transliteration:** satya
**Literal meaning:** from *sat* (being, existing, real) + adjectival suffix *-ya*; "that which is"
**Typical English translations:** truth, truthfulness, reality, the true
**Usage:** Used across Vedic, epic, and philosophical literature to denote both the property of a statement's correspondence with reality and the moral virtue of speaking truthfully. Appears in legal and ritual contexts as an oath-formula and in philosophical contexts as a metaphysical category (e.g., *satyam brahma*). Participates in extensive derivation: *satyatā*, *satyavādin*, *satyāgraha*.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**2.**
**Form:** ऋत
**Transliteration:** ṛta
**Literal meaning:** from root *ṛ-* (to go, to fit, to be in order); "that which has gone rightly," cosmic order
**Typical English translations:** cosmic order, right, truth, the correct
**Usage:** A Vedic term denoting the cosmic and moral order that governs both natural phenomena and ritual correctness; contrasted with *anṛta* (disorder, falsehood). In the Ṛgveda it functions as a standard against which actions and utterances are evaluated for rightness. Its usage is largely confined to Vedic and archaic contexts.
**Epistemic status:** [MARGINAL]

---

**3.**
**Form:** यथार्थ
**Transliteration:** yathārtha
**Literal meaning:** *yathā* (as, according to) + *artha* (meaning, object, purpose); "according to the object/meaning"
**Typical English translations:** accurate, true to the object, literal, real, correct
**Usage:** Used to describe statements, representations, or names that correspond accurately to their referent or intended meaning. Common in philosophical and grammatical literature to evaluate whether a word or description matches what it denotes. Also used in aesthetic and practical contexts to mean "appropriate" or "fitting."
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**4.**
**Form:** प्रमाण
**Transliteration:** pramāṇa
**Literal meaning:** *pra-* (forth, correctly) + *māṇa* (measuring, from root *mā-*, to measure); "correct measure"
**Typical English translations:** valid cognition, proof, authority, standard, criterion
**Usage:** A central epistemological term across all major Sanskrit philosophical schools, denoting a means of valid knowledge (perception, inference, testimony, etc.). Also used in legal and practical contexts to mean an authoritative standard or evidence. Participates in derivation: *pramātṛ* (knower), *prameya* (object of valid cognition), *aprāmāṇika* (lacking authority).
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**5.**
**Form:** प्रमा
**Transliteration:** pramā
**Literal meaning:** *pra-* + *mā-* (to measure); "correct measuring/cognition"
**Typical English translations:** valid knowledge, true cognition, correct apprehension
**Usage:** In epistemological literature, *pramā* denotes the result of a *pramāṇa*—a cognition that accurately apprehends its object. Distinguished from *apramā* (erroneous cognition) and *saṃśaya* (doubt). Primarily a technical philosophical term.
**Epistemic status:** [MARGINAL]

---

**6.**
**Form:** सत्
**Transliteration:** sat
**Literal meaning:** present participle of root *as-* (to be); "being, existing, real"
**Typical English translations:** real, existent, true, good, genuine
**Usage:** Used both ontologically (that which truly exists) and evaluatively (a good or genuine person or thing). In compounds such as *sat-puruṣa* (a genuine/good man) and *sat-karman* (right action), it marks authenticity or moral worth. Foundational in Vedāntic metaphysics as a name for ultimate reality.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**7.**
**Form:** असत्
**Transliteration:** asat
**Literal meaning:** *a-* (negation) + *sat*; "non-being, unreal"
**Typical English translations:** unreal, false, non-existent, bad, spurious
**Usage:** The negation of *sat*, used both metaphysically (that which lacks true existence) and evaluatively (a false statement, a bad or ignoble person). Appears in philosophical debate about the ontological status of the world and in moral contexts to characterize dishonest conduct.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**8.**
**Form:** तथ्य
**Transliteration:** tathya
**Literal meaning:** from *tathā* (thus, in that way) + *-ya*; "that which is thus/as it is"
**Typical English translations:** true, factual, real, a fact
**Usage:** Used to describe statements or accounts that correspond to how things actually are. Appears in legal and narrative contexts to mean "factual" or "the truth of the matter." Somewhat less philosophically loaded than *satya* and more oriented toward factual accuracy in specific cases.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**9.**
**Form:** अनृत
**Transliteration:** anṛta
**Literal meaning:** *an-* (negation) + *ṛta*; "contrary to cosmic order"
**Typical English translations:** falsehood, untruth, wrong, disorder
**Usage:** In Vedic literature, the opposite of *ṛta*, denoting both cosmic disorder and false or deceptive speech. In later literature it is used more narrowly for falsehood or lying, particularly in legal and moral contexts. Contrasted with *satya* in many texts.
**Epistemic status:** [MARGINAL]

---

**10.**
**Form:** मिथ्या
**Transliteration:** mithyā
**Literal meaning:** from *mithā* (wrongly, alternately, mutually) + adverbial/adjectival use; "wrongly, in a contrary manner"
**Typical English translations:** false, erroneous, wrong, illusory, in vain
**Usage:** Widely used in philosophical literature (especially Advaita Vedānta) to denote that which is neither absolutely real nor absolutely unreal—illusory or phenomenally apparent. Also used in everyday and logical contexts to mean simply "false" or "mistaken." Appears in compounds: *mithyājñāna* (false knowledge), *mithyāvāda* (false doctrine).
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**11.**
**Form:** भ्रम
**Transliteration:** bhrama
**Literal meaning:** from root *bhram-* (to wander, to be confused); "wandering, confusion"
**Typical English translations:** error, illusion, mistaken cognition, delusion
**Usage:** Denotes a cognitive error in which something is apprehended as other than it is (e.g., mistaking a rope for a snake). A standard example in epistemological and Vedāntic discussions of erroneous cognition. Contrasted with *pramā* (valid cognition).
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**12.**
**Form:** विपर्यय
**Transliteration:** viparyaya
**Literal meaning:** *vi-* (apart, wrongly) + *paryaya* (going around, reversal); "reversal, inversion"
**Typical English translations:** erroneous cognition, misapprehension, contrary knowledge, error
**Usage:** A technical epistemological term for cognition that apprehends an object as the opposite of what it is. Used in Sāṃkhya, Yoga, and other philosophical systems to classify a type of false cognition. Distinguished from *saṃśaya* (doubt) and *vikalpa* (conceptual construction).
**Epistemic status:** [MARGINAL]

---

**13.**
**Form:** धर्म
**Transliteration:** dharma
**Literal meaning:** from root *dhṛ-* (to hold, to sustain) + *-man* suffix; "that which holds/sustains"
**Typical English translations:** duty, right conduct, law, righteousness, moral order
**Usage:** One of the most semantically broad terms in Sanskrit, used to evaluate conduct, social roles, legal norms, and cosmic order. In legal texts (*dharmaśāstra*) it functions as the standard against which actions are judged right or wrong. In philosophical contexts it also denotes the defining property or characteristic of a thing.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**14.**
**Form:** न्याय
**Transliteration:** nyāya
**Literal meaning:** *ni-* (down, into) + root *i-* (to go); "going into, proper procedure, rule"
**Typical English translations:** rule, method, right reasoning, justice, logical argument
**Usage:** Used in legal contexts to mean a rule or principle of justice, and in philosophical contexts as the name of the school of logic and epistemology (*Nyāya*). Evaluates whether reasoning or procedure is correct and valid. Also used in the sense of a maxim or illustrative rule (*nyāya* as a standard example).
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**15.**
**Form:** युक्त
**Transliteration:** yukta
**Literal meaning:** past passive participle of root *yuj-* (to join, to yoke); "joined, fitted, appropriate"
**Typical English translations:** appropriate, fitting, right, proper, reasonable, correct
**Usage:** Used to evaluate whether an action, statement, or object is appropriate or fitting to its context, purpose, or standard. Common in philosophical, legal, and literary texts to express that something is well-reasoned or proper. Contrasted with *ayukta* (inappropriate, unfitting).
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**16.**
**Form:** उचित
**Transliteration:** ucita
**Literal meaning:** past passive participle of root *vac-/uc-* (to be accustomed, to be fit); "that which is fitting, customary"
**Typical English translations:** appropriate, fitting, proper, right, suitable
**Usage:** Evaluates conduct, speech, or objects as fitting or appropriate to a given context, person, or standard. Frequently used in literary and ethical texts to assess whether behavior conforms to expected norms. Overlaps with *yukta* but tends more toward social and contextual appropriateness.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**17.**
**Form:** साधु
**Transliteration:** sādhu
**Literal meaning:** from root *sādh-* (to accomplish, to go straight to a goal); "one who goes straight, accomplished"
**Typical English translations:** good, correct, virtuous, proper, well done; also a noun: a holy person
**Usage:** Used in grammatical literature to evaluate whether a word-form is correct (*sādhu* = grammatically well-formed), and in moral contexts to evaluate persons or actions as virtuous or good. In Pāṇinian grammar, *sādhu* is the standard term for a correct linguistic form. Also used as an exclamation of approval.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**18.**
**Form:** अपशब्द
**Transliteration:** apaśabda
**Literal meaning:** *apa-* (away from, deviant) + *śabda* (word, sound); "deviant word"
**Typical English translations:** incorrect word-form, solecism, corrupt form, barbarism
**Usage:** In grammatical literature, denotes a word-form that deviates from the standard (*sādhu*) and is therefore incorrect or corrupt. Used to evaluate linguistic forms against the norm established by grammar. The opposite of *sādhu* in the grammatical sense.
**Epistemic status:** [MARGINAL]

---

**19.**
**Form:** प्रतिरूप
**Transliteration:** pratirūpa
**Literal meaning:** *prati-* (against, corresponding to) + *rūpa* (form, appearance); "corresponding form, counterpart"
**Typical English translations:** resemblance, likeness, counterpart, imitation, semblance
**Usage:** Used to describe something that resembles or corresponds to an original form, sometimes with the implication of being a copy or imitation rather than the genuine article. Appears in philosophical and legal contexts to evaluate whether something is the real thing or merely a semblance. Can carry a slightly negative connotation of being a substitute or counterfeit.
**Epistemic status:** [MARGINAL]

---

**20.**
**Form:** आभास
**Transliteration:** ābhāsa
**Literal meaning:** *ā-* (toward, resembling) + *bhāsa* (shining, appearance, from root *bhās-*); "semblance, apparent shining"
**Typical English translations:** semblance, appearance, fallacy, pseudo-, apparent (but not real)
**Usage:** Used in philosophical and logical literature to denote something that has the appearance of a thing without being that thing—e.g., *hetvābhāsa* (fallacy, literally "semblance of a reason"). Evaluates whether something genuinely is what it appears to be. Common in Nyāya and Vedāntic literature.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**21.**
**Form:** वास्तव
**Transliteration:** vāstava
**Literal meaning:** from *vastu* (thing, real object, substance) + *-va* adjectival suffix; "pertaining to the real object"
**Typical English translations:** real, actual, genuine, factual
**Usage:** Used to evaluate whether something corresponds to or pertains to actual reality (*vastu*) as opposed to appearance or convention. Common in philosophical literature to distinguish what is genuinely the case from what is merely apparent or conventional. Also used in ordinary language to mean "actual" or "real."
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**22.**
**Form:** वस्तुतः
**Transliteration:** vastutaḥ
**Literal meaning:** *vastu* (real thing) + ablative/adverbial suffix *-taḥ*; "from the standpoint of the real thing"
**Typical English translations:** in reality, actually, truly, in fact
**Usage:** An adverbial expression used to signal that what follows is an assessment of how things actually are, as opposed to how they appear or are conventionally described. Common in philosophical commentary and argumentation to introduce a corrective or deeper evaluation.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**23.**
**Form:** परमार्थ
**Transliteration:** paramārtha
**Literal meaning:** *parama* (highest, ultimate) + *artha* (meaning, object, purpose); "ultimate object/meaning"
**Typical English translations:** ultimate truth, highest reality, absolute sense, true meaning
**Usage:** Used in Buddhist and Hindu philosophical literature to distinguish the ultimate or absolute level of truth from the conventional (*saṃvṛti* or *vyavahāra*) level. Evaluates whether a statement or claim holds at the highest level of analysis. Central to Madhyamaka Buddhist epistemology and Advaita Vedānta.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**24.**
**Form:** व्यावहारिक
**Transliteration:** vyāvahārika
**Literal meaning:** from *vyavahāra* (transaction, conventional practice, usage) + *-ika*; "pertaining to conventional practice"
**Typical English translations:** conventional, practical, empirical, transactional
**Usage:** Denotes the level of truth or validity that holds within conventional or practical usage, as opposed to the ultimate (*pāramārthika*) level. Used in philosophical literature to evaluate claims as conventionally valid without being ultimately real. Also used in legal literature to pertain to transactions and practical affairs.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**25.**
**Form:** शुद्ध
**Transliteration:** śuddha
**Literal meaning:** past passive participle of root *śudh-* (to be pure, to be cleansed); "purified, clean"
**Typical English translations:** pure, correct, unadulterated, genuine, unmixed
**Usage:** Used to evaluate objects, texts, persons, or substances as free from contamination, error, or admixture. In textual criticism and grammar, *śuddha pāṭha* denotes a correct or uncorrupted reading. In ritual and moral contexts, evaluates purity of conduct or substance.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**26.**
**Form:** दोष
**Transliteration:** doṣa
**Literal meaning:** from root *duṣ-* (to be spoiled, to be corrupted); "fault, corruption"
**Typical English translations:** fault, defect, flaw, error, blemish
**Usage:** Used across legal, medical, aesthetic, and philosophical literature to evaluate the presence of a defect or flaw in a person, object, argument, or action. In *Nyāya*, *doṣa* denotes a logical or epistemic fault. In *Āyurveda*, it refers to bodily humors when imbalanced. In aesthetics, it marks a flaw in a literary work.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**27.**
**Form:** गुण
**Transliteration:** guṇa
**Literal meaning:** from root *guṇ-* (to multiply, to count); "strand, quality, merit"
**Typical English translations:** quality, merit, virtue, property, attribute
**Usage:** Used to evaluate positive properties of persons, objects, arguments, or literary works. In aesthetics (*alaṃkāraśāstra*), *guṇa* denotes a positive quality of style. In Sāṃkhya philosophy, the three *guṇas* are the fundamental constituents of matter. Contrasted with *doṣa* in evaluative contexts.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**28.**
**Form:** तत्त्व
**Transliteration:** tattva
**Literal meaning:** *tat* (that) + *-tva* (abstract suffix); "that-ness, the being of that"
**Typical English translations:** truth, reality, essence, true nature, principle
**Usage:** Used to denote the true or essential nature of something, what something fundamentally is. Common in philosophical literature as a term for a fundamental category or principle of reality. The expression *tattvataḥ* ("in truth, in essence") is used to signal an assessment of what something really is as opposed to how it appears.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**29.**
**Form:** स्वरूप
**Transliteration:** svarūpa
**Literal meaning:** *sva* (own, self) + *rūpa* (form, appearance); "own form, intrinsic form"
**Typical English translations:** own nature, intrinsic form, true character, essential nature
**Usage:** Used to evaluate what something is in its own right, as opposed to what it appears to be or how it is described by others. Common in philosophical and grammatical literature to identify the intrinsic or defining character of a thing. Appears in expressions evaluating whether a description captures the genuine nature of its subject.
**Epistemic status:** [CORE]

---

**30.**
**Form:** अविसंवादिन्
**Transliteration:** avisaṃvādin
**Literal meaning:** *a-* (negation) + *vi-* (apart) + *saṃvāda* (agreement, correspondence) + *-in* (possessive suffix); "not contradicting, not discordant"
**Typical English translations:** non-contradictory, reliable, consistent, not misleading
**Usage:** Used in epistemological literature, particularly Buddhist *pramāṇa* theory (Dignāga, Dharmakīrti), to characterize a cognition or statement that does not contradict or mislead with respect to its object—a criterion of reliability or validity. A cognition is *avisaṃvādin* if acting on it leads to successful engagement with its object.
**Epistemic status:** [MARGINAL]

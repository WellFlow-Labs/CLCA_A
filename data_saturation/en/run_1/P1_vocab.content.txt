# CLCA Protocol v3.2 — P1 CORE VOCABULARY COLLECTION
# Language: English (en)

---

**Note on form fields:** English uses the Latin script natively; no transliteration column is applicable. The "literal meaning or morphological breakdown" field is provided where etymology or morphology is informative.

---

**1. true**
- Form: *true*
- Morphological breakdown: Old English *trēowe* (faithful, trustworthy)
- Typical translations: true, correct, genuine, real
- Usage: Used across propositional, moral, and ontological contexts to assert that something conforms to fact, is genuine, or is faithful to an original. Participates in productive derivation: *truth*, *truly*, *untrue*, *trueness*.
- [CORE]

---

**2. truth**
- Form: *truth*
- Morphological breakdown: Old English *trēowth* (faithfulness); nominalization of *true*
- Typical translations: truth, verity, fact
- Usage: Refers to the property of being true, or to a true statement or fact. Used in philosophical, legal, moral, and everyday contexts. Appears in fixed expressions such as *tell the truth*, *truth of the matter*.
- [CORE]

---

**3. accurate**
- Form: *accurate*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *accuratus* (done with care), from *ad-* + *cura* (care)
- Typical translations: accurate, precise, correct, exact
- Usage: Applied to descriptions, measurements, representations, and reports to indicate close correspondence with fact or reality. Commonly contrasted with *approximate* or *inaccurate*. Productive: *accuracy*, *inaccurately*, *inaccuracy*.
- [CORE]

---

**4. genuine**
- Form: *genuine*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *genuinus* (native, natural), possibly from *genu* (knee, in the sense of placing a child on the knee to acknowledge paternity)
- Typical translations: genuine, authentic, real, sincere
- Usage: Applied to objects, persons, emotions, and documents to indicate that they are what they purport to be, not counterfeit or feigned. Contrasts with *fake*, *spurious*, *counterfeit*. Productive: *genuinely*, *genuineness*.
- [CORE]

---

**5. authentic**
- Form: *authentic*
- Morphological breakdown: Greek *authentikos* (original, genuine), from *authentes* (one acting on one's own authority)
- Typical translations: authentic, genuine, original, authoritative
- Usage: Used of documents, artworks, persons, and experiences to indicate that they are original, not copied or falsified, or that they express a person's true character. Appears in legal, aesthetic, and philosophical contexts. Productive: *authenticity*, *authentically*, *authenticate*, *authentication*.
- [CORE]

---

**6. valid**
- Form: *valid*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *validus* (strong, effective), from *valere* (to be strong)
- Typical translations: valid, sound, legally effective, well-grounded
- Usage: Used in logical contexts to describe arguments whose conclusions follow necessarily from their premises, and in legal contexts to describe instruments or acts that have binding force. Also used informally to mean well-founded or acceptable. Productive: *validity*, *validate*, *invalid*, *invalidate*.
- [CORE]

---

**7. correct**
- Form: *correct*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *correctus*, past participle of *corrigere* (to set right), from *com-* + *regere* (to rule, guide)
- Typical translations: correct, right, accurate, proper
- Usage: Applied to answers, behavior, procedures, and statements to indicate conformity with a standard, rule, or fact. Used in both descriptive and normative senses. Productive: *correctly*, *correctness*, *incorrect*, *correction*, *corrective*.
- [CORE]

---

**8. right**
- Form: *right*
- Morphological breakdown: Old English *riht* (straight, just, proper)
- Typical translations: right, correct, just, proper, true
- Usage: Covers a wide range of evaluative senses including factual correctness, moral rightness, and procedural propriety. Used predicatively (*that's right*) and attributively (*the right answer*). Productive: *rightly*, *rightness*, *rightful*, *rights*.
- [CORE]

---

**9. fake**
- Form: *fake*
- Morphological breakdown: origin uncertain; possibly from German *fegen* (to sweep, polish) via criminal slang
- Typical translations: fake, counterfeit, false, sham, fraudulent
- Usage: Applied to objects, persons, documents, and emotions to indicate that they are not what they appear or claim to be. Used as noun, verb, and adjective. Productive: *faker*, *fakery*, *faked*.
- [CORE]

---

**10. false**
- Form: *false*
- Morphological breakdown: Old English *fals*, from Latin *falsus* (deceived, feigned), past participle of *fallere* (to deceive)
- Typical translations: false, untrue, incorrect, counterfeit, deceitful
- Usage: Applied to propositions, beliefs, appearances, and persons to indicate untruth, deception, or inauthenticity. Covers both epistemic falsity and moral duplicity. Productive: *falsely*, *falseness*, *falsity*, *falsify*, *falsification*, *falsehood*.
- [CORE]

---

**11. falsehood**
- Form: *falsehood*
- Morphological breakdown: *false* + *-hood* (condition, state)
- Typical translations: falsehood, lie, untruth, deception
- Usage: Refers to a false statement or the condition of being false. Used in moral and legal contexts, often with connotations of deliberate deception. Distinguished from *error* by typically implying intent.
- [CORE]

---

**12. counterfeit**
- Form: *counterfeit*
- Morphological breakdown: Old French *contrefait*, from *contrefaire* (to imitate), from *contre-* + *faire* (to make)
- Typical translations: counterfeit, forged, fake, imitation
- Usage: Primarily applied to currency, documents, and goods made in fraudulent imitation of genuine items. Used as noun, verb, and adjective. Carries strong legal connotations of fraud. Productive: *counterfeiter*, *counterfeiting*.
- [CORE]

---

**13. spurious**
- Form: *spurious*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *spurius* (illegitimate, false)
- Typical translations: spurious, false, illegitimate, counterfeit, not genuine
- Usage: Applied to documents, arguments, claims, and objects to indicate that they are not genuine or are based on false premises. More formal and literary than *fake*. Productive: *spuriously*, *spuriousness*.
- [MARGINAL]

---

**14. veracious**
- Form: *veracious*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *verax* (truthful), from *verus* (true) + *-acious* (tending to)
- Typical translations: truthful, veracious, accurate
- Usage: Applied to persons and statements to indicate habitual truthfulness or factual accuracy. More formal and less common than *truthful*. Productive: *veraciously*, *veracity*.
- [MARGINAL]

---

**15. veracity**
- Form: *veracity*
- Morphological breakdown: Medieval Latin *veracitas*, from *verax* (truthful)
- Typical translations: truthfulness, veracity, accuracy, honesty
- Usage: Refers to the quality of being truthful or accurate, applied to persons, accounts, and statements. Commonly used in legal and journalistic contexts when assessing the reliability of testimony or reporting.
- [CORE]

---

**16. reliable**
- Form: *reliable*
- Morphological breakdown: *rely* (from Old French *relier*, to bind back) + *-able*
- Typical translations: reliable, dependable, trustworthy, credible
- Usage: Applied to persons, sources, instruments, and methods to indicate that they can be consistently trusted to perform correctly or provide accurate information. Productive: *reliability*, *reliably*, *unreliable*.
- [CORE]

---

**17. credible**
- Form: *credible*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *credibilis* (worthy of belief), from *credere* (to believe)
- Typical translations: credible, believable, plausible, trustworthy
- Usage: Applied to persons, accounts, and evidence to indicate that they are worthy of belief or trust. Used in legal contexts to assess witness testimony and in everyday contexts to evaluate claims. Productive: *credibility*, *credibly*, *incredible*, *incredibility*.
- [CORE]

---

**18. plausible**
- Form: *plausible*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *plausibilis* (deserving applause), from *plaudere* (to applaud)
- Typical translations: plausible, believable, seemingly reasonable, credible
- Usage: Applied to explanations, claims, and persons to indicate that they appear reasonable or probable, without asserting that they are actually true. Often carries a slight implication that the appearance may not match reality. Productive: *plausibility*, *plausibly*, *implausible*.
- [CORE]

---

**19. legitimate**
- Form: *legitimate*
- Morphological breakdown: Medieval Latin *legitimatus*, from *legitimus* (lawful), from *lex* (law)
- Typical translations: legitimate, lawful, valid, genuine, rightful
- Usage: Applied to claims, authority, procedures, and persons to indicate conformity with law, rules, or accepted standards. Also used to mean genuine or real in informal contexts. Productive: *legitimacy*, *legitimately*, *illegitimate*, *legitimize*, *legitimation*.
- [CORE]

---

**20. fraudulent**
- Form: *fraudulent*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *fraudulentus*, from *fraus* (fraud, deceit)
- Typical translations: fraudulent, deceitful, dishonest, false
- Usage: Applied to acts, documents, and representations made with intent to deceive for unlawful gain. Primarily a legal and formal term. Productive: *fraudulently*, *fraud*, *fraudulence*.
- [CORE]

---

**21. sincere**
- Form: *sincere*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *sincerus* (whole, pure, genuine); etymology debated
- Typical translations: sincere, genuine, honest, heartfelt
- Usage: Applied to persons, emotions, and expressions to indicate that they are genuinely felt or meant, without pretense or deception. Contrasts with *insincere* and *hypocritical*. Productive: *sincerely*, *sincerity*, *insincerity*, *insincere*.
- [CORE]

---

**22. bona fide**
- Form: *bona fide*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *bona fide* (in good faith), from *bonus* (good) + *fides* (faith)
- Typical translations: genuine, authentic, in good faith, legitimate
- Usage: Used as an adjective or adverb to indicate that something is genuine or done in good faith, without fraud or deception. Common in legal and formal contexts. Also used informally to mean simply *real* or *genuine*.
- [CORE]

---

**23. apocryphal**
- Form: *apocryphal*
- Morphological breakdown: Greek *apokryphos* (hidden, obscure), from *apo-* (away) + *kryptein* (to hide)
- Typical translations: apocryphal, of doubtful authenticity, legendary, unverified
- Usage: Applied to stories, sayings, and texts whose authenticity or historicity is doubtful, though they are widely circulated. Carries a connotation that the item is probably not genuine but has achieved cultural currency. Productive: *apocryphally*, *Apocrypha*.
- [MARGINAL]

---

**24. verisimilitude**
- Form: *verisimilitude*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *verisimilitudo*, from *verus* (true) + *similis* (like) + *-tudo* (abstract noun suffix)
- Typical translations: verisimilitude, appearance of truth, plausibility, lifelikeness
- Usage: Refers to the quality of appearing to be true or real, especially in literary and artistic contexts where a work convincingly represents reality. Also used in philosophy of science to describe how closely a theory approximates truth. Primarily a formal and academic term.
- [MARGINAL]

---

**25. sound**
- Form: *sound* (adjective)
- Morphological breakdown: Old English *sund* (healthy, whole)
- Typical translations: sound, valid, well-founded, reliable, correct
- Usage: Applied to arguments, reasoning, judgments, and advice to indicate that they are well-grounded, free from error, and based on solid principles. In formal logic, a *sound* argument is both valid and has true premises. Productive: *soundly*, *soundness*, *unsound*.
- [CORE]

**27. trustworthy**
- Form: *trustworthy*
- Morphological breakdown: *trust* (Old Norse *traust*, confidence) + *worthy* (deserving)
- Typical translations: trustworthy, reliable, dependable, honest
- Usage: Applied to persons, sources, and institutions to indicate that they can be relied upon to be honest and to act as expected. Commonly used in assessments of character and in evaluations of information sources. Productive: *trustworthiness*, *untrustworthy*.
- [CORE]

---

**28. fictitious**
- Form: *fictitious*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *ficticius* (artificial, feigned), from *fingere* (to shape, feign)
- Typical translations: fictitious, invented, imaginary, false, fabricated
- Usage: Applied to names, accounts, persons, and entities that are invented or fabricated rather than real. Used in legal contexts (e.g., *fictitious name*) and in literary contexts. Productive: *fictitiously*, *fictitiousness*.
- [CORE]

---

**29. fabricated**
- Form: *fabricated*
- Morphological breakdown: Latin *fabricatus*, past participle of *fabricare* (to construct), from *fabrica* (workshop); extended to mean invented or falsified
- Typical translations: fabricated, invented, made up, falsified, forged
- Usage: Applied to evidence, stories, and claims to indicate that they have been invented or falsified, typically with intent to deceive. Used in legal and journalistic contexts. Productive: *fabricate*, *fabrication*, *fabricator*.
- [CORE]

---

**30. rightful**
- Form: *rightful*
- Morphological breakdown: *right* (Old English *riht*) + *-ful* (having the quality of)
- Typical translations: rightful, legitimate, lawful, just, proper
- Usage: Applied to claims, ownership, and positions to indicate that they are held or exercised in accordance with right, law, or justice. Commonly used in legal and moral contexts to assert that something belongs to or is owed to someone by right. Productive: *rightfully*, *rightfulness*.
- [CORE]

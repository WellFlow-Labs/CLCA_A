# G3 — EMERGENT CROSS-LANGUAGE SEMANTIC DIMENSIONS

## Methodology Note

This analysis identifies recurring semantic distinctions visible across the eight languages based solely on patterns documented in F1–F3 tables and related evidence. Dimensions are inferred from:
- Lexical contrasts within languages (separate terms for related but distinct concepts)
- Semantic groupings identified in F3 tables
- Distributional patterns in F1 usage descriptions
- Constructional specializations noted in F2/F4 tables

## Identified Semantic Dimensions

### DIMENSION 1: PROPOSITIONAL CORRESPONDENCE vs. MATERIAL AUTHENTICITY

**Definition**: This dimension distinguishes whether evaluation targets the correspondence between statements/representations and facts (propositional domain) versus the genuineness/originality of physical objects, documents, or credentials (material domain). Languages may lexicalize this distinction through separate terms or handle both with a single lexeme that shows different distributional patterns.

**Representative Languages & Terms**:

- **Chinese**: 实 (shí) for factual correspondence ("事实" shìshí "fact") vs. 真 (zhēn) for material authenticity ("真品" zhēnpǐn "genuine article")
- **Greek**: αλήθεια (alítheia) for propositional truth vs. γνήσιος (gnísios)/αυθεντικός (afthentikós) for material genuineness
- **English**: true (primarily propositional) vs. genuine/authentic (primarily material)
- **Tagalog**: totoo (factual truth) vs. tunay (genuineness/authenticity)
- **Swahili**: kweli/ukweli (truth of statements) vs. halisi (genuineness of objects)
- **Hebrew**: אמת (emet) for truth vs. אמיתי (amiti) for genuineness (though derived from same root)

**Distribution / Notes**: **STRONGLY SUPPORTED** across 6/8 languages through clear lexical distinctions. Sanskrit सत्य (satya) and Quechua chiqan show less clear internal differentiation, covering both domains with single lexemes. The distinction appears to reflect different verification procedures: propositional truth verified through evidence/testimony, material authenticity through provenance/testing.

---

### DIMENSION 2: SIMPLE CORRECTNESS vs. PRECISION/ACCURACY

**Definition**: This dimension distinguishes binary judgments of correctness (right/wrong answers, correct/incorrect statements) from gradable assessments of precision, exactness, or fine-grained accuracy. The former involves categorical evaluation against standards; the latter involves degree of correspondence to detailed specifications.

**Representative Languages & Terms**:

- **Chinese**: 对/错 (duì/cuò) for binary correctness vs. 准确 (zhǔnquè) for precision/accuracy
- **Hebrew**: נכון (nakhon) for simple correctness vs. מדויק (meduyak) for precision
- **Greek**: σωστός (sostós) for correctness vs. ακριβής (akrivís) for precision/exactness
- **English**: correct/right vs. accurate/precise
- **Swahili**: sahihi (correct) vs. madhubuti (precise/reliable) or kamili (complete/accurate)

**Distribution / Notes**: **CLEARLY LEXICALIZED** in 5/8 languages (Chinese, Hebrew, Greek, English, Swahili). Tagalog shows less clear distinction (uses tumpak or borrowed eksakto for precision). Quechua uses allin broadly or hunt'a (complete) for thoroughness. Sanskrit uses यथार्थ (yathārtha) for correspondence but less clear precision vocabulary. The dimension appears more elaborated in languages with strong scientific/technical registers.

---

### DIMENSION 3: FACTUAL CORRESPONDENCE vs. MORAL/NORMATIVE RIGHTNESS

**Definition**: This dimension distinguishes evaluation of whether something corresponds to facts or reality (epistemic domain) from evaluation of whether something conforms to moral norms, justice, or proper conduct (moral/normative domain). Some languages maintain clear separation; others show significant conflation or overlap.

**Representative Languages & Terms**:

- **Chinese**: 正确 (zhèngquè) "factually correct" vs. 正义 (zhèngyì) "justice/righteousness" (separate compounds from same root 正)
- **Greek**: αλήθεια (alítheia) "truth" vs. δικαιοσύνη (dikaiosýni) "justice" (completely separate)
- **English**: true/correct vs. right/just (right shows polysemy across both domains)
- **Sanskrit**: सत्य (satya) "truth" vs. धर्म (dharma) "righteousness/duty" (separate)

**Conflated cases**:
- **Hebrew**: צדק (tsedek) covers both "being right/correct" and "justice/righteousness"
- **Swahili**: haki (from Arabic ḥaqq) covers "truth/correctness" and "rights/justice"

**Distribution / Notes**: **VARIABLE CROSS-LINGUISTICALLY**. Most languages (6/8) maintain lexical separation between factual and moral domains, but Hebrew and Swahili show significant conflation. English right shows polysemy spanning both. Quechua allin covers both domains without clear internal distinction. This suggests the factual/moral boundary is culturally variable.

---

### DIMENSION 4: SUBSTANTIALITY/CONCRETENESS vs. EMPTINESS/SUPERFICIALITY

**Definition**: This dimension evaluates whether something has genuine substance, solidity, or concrete reality versus being empty, hollow, merely superficial, or lacking real content. It applies to both physical objects and abstract qualities like arguments, promises, or displays.

**Representative Languages & Terms**:

- **Chinese**: 实 (shí) "solid, substantial, concrete" vs. 虚 (xū) "empty, hollow, false" (华而不实 "flashy but lacking substance")
- **Quechua**: hunt'a "full, complete, whole" vs. ch'usaq "empty, hollow, vain" (ch'usaq simi "empty words")
- **Sanskrit**: (less clearly lexicalized, but आभास ābhāsa "mere appearance" vs. substantive reality)
- **Greek**: ουσιαστικός (ousiastikós) "substantial, essential" vs. εικονικός (eikonikós) "virtual, apparent"

**Distribution / Notes**: **STRONGLY LEXICALIZED** in Chinese and Quechua. Greek shows the distinction through ουσία (ousía) "substance/essence" vocabulary. Other languages show weaker or less systematic lexicalization. Sanskrit treats this more through ontological categories (सत्/असत्) than dedicated substantiality terms. English, Hebrew, Tagalog, Swahili lack clear equivalent systematic distinction, though English substantial/superficial exists.

---

### DIMENSION 5: HONESTY/SINCERITY IN CHARACTER vs. FACTUAL ACCURACY OF STATEMENTS

**Definition**: This dimension distinguishes evaluation of persons' habitual truthfulness, sincerity, or honest character (dispositional/moral) from evaluation of whether specific statements correspond to facts (propositional/epistemic). The former involves character assessment; the latter involves verification.

**Representative Languages & Terms**:

- **Chinese**: 诚实 (chéngshí) "honest (person)" vs. 真实 (zhēnshí) "true (statement/situation)"
- **Hebrew**: כנה (kene) "honest, sincere" vs. אמת (emet) "truth (of statements)"
- **Greek**: ειλικρινής (eilikrinís) "sincere, honest" vs. αλήθεια (alítheia) "truth"
- **English**: honest/truthful (persons) vs. true (statements)
- **Tagalog**: tapat "sincere, honest, faithful" vs. totoo "true (factually)"
- **Swahili**: (uses halisi "genuine" or borrowed sinsero) vs. kweli "true"

**Distribution / Notes**: **CLEARLY LEXICALIZED** in 6/8 languages (Chinese, Hebrew, Greek, English, Tagalog, Swahili). Sanskrit uses सत्यवादिन् (satyavādin) "truth-speaker" (compound) rather than dedicated sincerity term. Quechua uses chiqan runa "truthful person" (compound) and chiqan sunqu "true heart" rather than dedicated lexeme. The distinction reflects different evaluation targets: character traits versus propositional content.

---

### DIMENSION 6: DECEPTION/LYING vs. SIMPLE ERROR/MISTAKE

**Definition**: This dimension distinguishes deliberate falsehood or deception (involving intent to mislead) from unintentional error or mistake (involving lack of knowledge or judgment failure). The former carries moral condemnation; the latter may be morally neutral.

**Representative Languages & Terms**:

- **Chinese**: 欺骗 (qīpiàn) "deceive" / 谎言 (huǎngyán) "lie" vs. 错误 (cuòwù) "error, mistake"
- **Hebrew**: שקר (sheker) "lie, falsehood" / רמאות (rama'ut) "deception" vs. טעות (ta'ut) "error, mistake"
- **Greek**: ψέμα (pséma) "lie" / απάτη (apáti) "deception" vs. λάθος (láthos) "error, mistake"
- **English**: lie/deceive vs. error/mistake
- **Tagalog**: kasinungalingan "lie" / daya "cheat, fraud" vs. pagkakamali "mistake, error"
- **Swahili**: uongo "lie" / -danganya "deceive" vs. makosa "mistakes, errors"
- **Quechua**: llulla "lie, deceptive" / llullachiy "cause to lie, deceive" vs. pantay "to err, make mistake"
- **Sanskrit**: मिथ्या (mithyā) "false, illusory" vs. भ्रम (bhrama) "error, mistaken cognition"

**Distribution / Notes**: **UNIVERSALLY LEXICALIZED** across all 8 languages. All languages maintain clear distinction between intentional deception (morally condemned) and unintentional error (morally neutral or less condemned). This suggests universal pragmatic importance of distinguishing deliberate from accidental falsehood.

---

### DIMENSION 7: VALIDITY/LEGITIMACY (INSTITUTIONAL) vs. FACTUAL TRUTH

**Definition**: This dimension distinguishes whether something has proper authorization, legal force, or institutional legitimacy (procedural/legal domain) from whether it corresponds to facts (epistemic domain). Validity concerns conformity to rules and proper authority; truth concerns correspondence to reality.

**Representative Languages & Terms**:

- **Hebrew**: תקף (takef) "valid (legally/logically)" vs. אמת (emet) "true (factually)"
- **Greek**: έγκυρος (énkyros) "valid, legitimate" vs. αλήθεια (alítheia) "truth"
- **English**: valid (legal/logical) vs. true (factual)
- **Sanskrit**: (less clearly lexicalized; uses प्रमाण pramāṇa for epistemic validity)
- **Swahili**: chanin "legitimate, valid, worthy" vs. kweli "true"
- **Tagalog**: (less clear; may use wasto "proper" or borrowed balido)

**Distribution / Notes**: **CLEARLY LEXICALIZED** in 4/8 languages (Hebrew, Greek, English, Swahili). Chinese uses 正当 (zhèngdàng) "legitimate" versus 真实 (zhēnshí) "true" but less systematically. Sanskrit treats validity through प्रमाण epistemological framework rather than legal/institutional terms. Tagalog and Quechua show less clear lexicalization (may use borrowed terms or general correctness vocabulary). The distinction appears more elaborated in languages with strong legal/administrative registers.

---

### DIMENSION 8: RELIABILITY/DEPENDABILITY vs. SINGLE-INSTANCE ACCURACY

**Definition**: This dimension distinguishes evaluation of consistent trustworthiness or dependability over time (track record, character) from evaluation of accuracy in single instances. Reliability involves predictability and consistency; accuracy involves correspondence in specific cases.

**Representative Languages & Terms**:

- **Chinese**: 可靠 (kěkào) "reliable, dependable" vs. 准确 (zhǔnquè) "accurate, precise"
- **Hebrew**: אמין (amin)/נאמן (ne'eman) "reliable, trustworthy" vs. נכון (nakhon) "correct (in instance)"
- **Greek**: αξιόπιστος (axiópistos) "reliable" vs. σωστός (sostós) "correct"
- **English**: reliable/trustworthy vs. correct/accurate
- **Swahili**: madhubuti "reliable, accurate, precise" (conflates both)
- **Sanskrit**: (uses प्रमाण pramāṇa for reliable epistemic means)

**Distribution / Notes**: **CLEARLY LEXICALIZED** in 4/8 languages (Chinese, Hebrew, Greek, English). Swahili madhubuti appears to conflate reliability and precision. Tagalog uses mapagkakatiwalaan (complex derivation) for trustworthiness. Quechua lacks dedicated reliability term (uses allin or circumlocution). Sanskrit treats reliability through epistemological framework (प्रमाण as reliable means of knowledge). The distinction reflects different temporal scopes: reliability is dispositional/habitual, accuracy is episodic.

---

### DIMENSION 9: CONVENTIONAL/PRACTICAL vs. ULTIMATE/ABSOLUTE TRUTH

**Definition**: This dimension distinguishes truth that holds at the level of everyday practical reality, conventional usage, or empirical observation from truth that holds at an ultimate, absolute, or metaphysical level. This is primarily a philosophical distinction about levels or domains of truth.

**Representative Languages & Terms**:

- **Sanskrit**: व्यवहार (vyavahāra) "conventional/practical reality" vs. परमार्थ (paramārtha) "ultimate/absolute truth" (with dedicated adverbials व्यवहारतः/परमार्थतः)
- **Chinese**: (some philosophical register distinctions: 理 lǐ "principle" vs. 事 shì "affairs", but not systematically lexicalized like Sanskrit)
- **Greek**: (philosophical vocabulary exists but no systematic two-truths framework)

**Distribution / Notes**: **STRONGLY LEXICALIZED** only in Sanskrit, where it structures major philosophical frameworks (Madhyamaka, Advaita Vedanta). Other languages lack systematic lexicalization of this distinction. Chinese shows some philosophical register variation. Greek, Hebrew, English, Tagalog, Swahili, Quechua do not show clear lexicalization. This appears to be a language-specific elaboration reflecting Sanskrit's role as philosophical metalanguage rather than a universal semantic dimension.

---

### DIMENSION 10: EPISTEMOLOGICAL VALIDITY (MEANS OF KNOWLEDGE) vs. PROPOSITIONAL TRUTH

**Definition**: This dimension distinguishes evaluation of the instruments, methods, or means by which knowledge is acquired (epistemological validity) from evaluation of the content of what is known (propositional truth). The former concerns how we know; the latter concerns what is true.

**Representative Languages & Terms**:

- **Sanskrit**: प्रमाण (pramāṇa) "valid means of knowledge" (perception, inference, testimony) vs. सत्य (satya) "truth (content)"
- **Hebrew**: (some vocabulary: מוכח mukhaḥ "proven", but no systematic pramāṇa equivalent)
- **Greek**: (philosophical terms exist but less systematic)
- **Swahili**: ushahidi "evidence, testimony" (means) vs. ukweli "truth" (content)

**Distribution / Notes**: **STRONGLY LEXICALIZED** only in Sanskrit with systematic epistemological framework (pramāṇa-śāstra). Other languages distinguish evidence/proof from truth but lack Sanskrit's systematic elaboration of valid knowledge sources. Hebrew, Greek, English, Swahili show evidence/testimony vocabulary but not systematic epistemological validity framework. Chinese, Tagalog, Quechua show even less elaboration. This appears language-specific to Sanskrit's philosophical tradition.

---

### DIMENSION 11: CLARITY/OBVIOUSNESS vs. HIDDENNESS/OBSCURITY

**Definition**: This dimension evaluates whether truth or facts are manifest, evident, and clearly visible versus hidden, concealed, or obscure. This concerns epistemic accessibility rather than truth-value itself—whether truth is easy or difficult to perceive/discover.

**Representative Languages & Terms**:

- **Hebrew**: ברור (barur) "clear, obvious" vs. (implied: hidden, unclear)
- **Greek**: ברור (barur) "clear, evident" vs. (concealment terms not in core truth vocabulary)
- **Swahili**: dhahiri "clear, evident, obvious" vs. (implied: hidden)
- **Tagalog**: lantád "exposed, open, evident" vs. lihim "secret, hidden, concealed"
- **Quechua**: suti "clear, evident, obvious" vs. (implied: hidden)
- **Sanskrit**: (uses प्रत्यक्ष pratyakṣa "directly perceptible" in epistemological framework)

**Distribution / Notes**: **MODERATELY SUPPORTED** across 5/8 languages. Tagalog uniquely shows clear lexical opposition (lantád/lihim) with extensive derivational families. Other languages have clarity terms but less systematic opposition with concealment in truth domain specifically. Chinese, English show metaphorical revelation constructions (truth coming to light) but less clear lexicalization of the clarity dimension itself. This dimension appears to concern epistemic accessibility rather than truth-value.

---

### DIMENSION 12: COMPLETENESS/WHOLENESS vs. INCOMPLETENESS/PARTIALITY

**Definition**: This dimension evaluates whether something is complete, whole, and lacking nothing essential versus incomplete, partial, or fragmentary. When applied to truth, it distinguishes "the whole truth" from partial truths or half-truths. When applied to objects or processes, it evaluates thoroughness.

**Representative Languages & Terms**:

- **Chinese**: 完整 (wánzhěng) "complete, whole" (though not in core P1 truth vocabulary)
- **Sanskrit**: कामिल (kāmil) "complete, perfect, whole, entire"
- **Greek**: (uses completeness vocabulary but not systematically in truth domain)
- **English**: complete/whole (applied to truth in "whole truth"), half-truth
- **Swahili**: kamili "complete, perfect, whole, entire, accurate"
- **Quechua**: hunt'a "full, complete, whole" vs. ch'ulla "single, incomplete, lacking pair"

**Distribution / Notes**: **MODERATELY SUPPORTED** across 5/8 languages. Quechua shows clearest lexical opposition (hunt'a/ch'ulla). Swahili kamili explicitly includes "accurate" among meanings, linking completeness to truth. English half-truth shows the dimension applied to truth domain. Chinese, Sanskrit, Greek have completeness vocabulary but less clearly integrated into core truth domain. Hebrew and Tagalog show less clear lexicalization of this dimension in truth contexts.

---

### DIMENSION 13: SINCERITY/AUTHENTICITY IN RELATIONSHIPS vs. FACTUAL TRUTH

**Definition**: This dimension distinguishes evaluation of genuineness in interpersonal relationships, emotions, or personal identity (relational/emotional authenticity) from factual correspondence of statements. The former concerns whether feelings, friendships, or self-presentation are genuine; the latter concerns whether claims match facts.

**Representative Languages & Terms**:

- **Chinese**: 真诚 (zhēnchéng) "sincere" / 真心 (zhēnxīn) "genuine intention" vs. 真实 (zhēnshí) "true, factual"
- **Hebrew**: נאמן (ne'eman) "faithful, loyal" vs. אמת (emet) "truth (factual)"
- **Greek**: ειλικρινής (eilikrinís) "sincere, honest" vs. αλήθεια (alítheia) "truth"
- **English**: sincere/genuine (in relationships) vs. true (factually)
- **Tagalog**: tunay na pag-ibig "true love" / tunay na kaibigan "true friend" vs. totoo (factual)
- **Swahili**: halisi applied to mapenzi "love" and urafiki "friendship" vs. kweli (factual)
- **Quechua**: chiqan sunqu "true heart" vs. chiqan (factual)

**Distribution / Notes**: **WIDELY ATTESTED** across 7/8 languages through dedicated lexemes or systematic collocations. All languages show ways to evaluate relational/emotional genuineness distinct from factual truth, though some (Quechua, Swahili) use the same root with different collocations rather than separate lexemes. This suggests universal pragmatic importance of evaluating interpersonal authenticity.

---

### DIMENSION 14: CERTAINTY/CONFIDENCE vs. DOUBT/UNCERTAINTY (EPISTEMIC STANCE)

**Definition**: This dimension marks the speaker's degree of confidence or certainty about claims, ranging from complete certainty to doubt or uncertainty. This concerns epistemic stance or attitude rather than truth-value itself—how sure the speaker is, not whether something is true.

**Representative Languages & Terms**:

- **Hebrew**: ודאי (vadai) "certain, sure" / בטוח (batuaḥ) "certain" vs. ספק (safek) "doubt"
- **Greek**: βέβαιος (vévaios) "certain, sure" vs. αμφίβολος (amfívolos) "doubtful, dubious"
- **English**: certain/sure vs. doubtful/uncertain
- **Swahili**: hakika "certain, sure, definite" vs. (shaka "doubt" implied in ukweli usio na shaka "undoubted truth")
- **Sanskrit**: (uses निश्चय niścaya "certainty" though not in core P1 data)
- **Quechua**: (obligatory evidential marking -mi/-si/-cha serves this function grammatically)

**Distribution / Notes**: **CLEARLY LEXICALIZED** in 4/8 languages (Hebrew, Greek, English, Swahili). Chinese shows less clear dedicated certainty vocabulary in truth domain. Tagalog uses sigurado (borrowed from Spanish). Quechua uniquely grammaticalizes this through obligatory evidential suffixes rather than lexical items. Sanskrit has certainty vocabulary but not prominently in core truth domain. This dimension appears to concern speaker's epistemic attitude rather than objective truth-value.

---

### DIMENSION 15: VERIFICATION/CONFIRMATION (PROCESS) vs. TRUTH (STATE)

**Definition**: This dimension distinguishes the active process of verifying, confirming, or establishing truth from the state of being true. The former involves evidential procedures and epistemic work; the latter is the result or state achieved.

**Representative Languages & Terms**:

- **Chinese**: 证实 (zhèngshí) "verify, confirm" vs. 真实 (zhēnshí) "true, real"
- **Hebrew**: אימת (imet) "verify" / בדק (badak) "check" vs. אמת (emet) "truth"
- **Sanskrit**: साधयति (sādhayati) "prove, establish" vs. सत्य (satya) "truth"
- **Greek**: βεβαιώνω (vevaióno) "confirm, verify" vs. αλήθεια (alítheia) "truth"
- **English**: verify/confirm/prove vs. true/truth
- **Tagalog**: patunayan "to prove, verify" vs. totoo "true"
- **Swahili**: thibitisha "to confirm, verify" vs. kweli "true"
- **Quechua**: chiqanchay "to verify, authenticate" vs. chiqan "true"

**Distribution / Notes**: **UNIVERSALLY DISTINGUISHED** across all 8 languages. All languages show dedicated vocabulary for verification processes distinct from truth-state terms. Six languages use causative morphology (Hebrew, Sanskrit, Greek, Tagalog, Swahili, Quechua); Chinese uses resultative compounds; English uses separate roots (verify from Latin). This suggests universal recognition that establishing truth is an active process distinct from truth itself.

---

### DIMENSION 16: APPEARANCE/SEEMING vs. REALITY/ACTUALITY

**Definition**: This dimension distinguishes how things appear, seem, or are represented from how they actually are in reality. It concerns the contrast between surface appearance and underlying reality, or between representation and actual state of affairs.

**Representative Languages & Terms**:

- **Chinese**: 表面 (biǎomiàn) "surface, appearance" vs. 实际 (shíjì) "actual, reality" (though not in core P1 truth vocabulary)
- **Sanskrit**: आभास (ābhāsa) "appearance, semblance" vs. सत्य (satya) "reality"
- **Greek**: εικονικός (eikonikós) "virtual, apparent" vs. πραγματικός (pragmatikós) "real, actual"
- **English**: seem/appear vs. be (really), virtual vs. real
- **Tagalog**: palabás "pretense, show, facade" vs. tunay "genuine"
- **Swahili**: (less clearly lexicalized in core truth vocabulary)
- **Quechua**: rikch'ay "to appear, to seem" vs. chiqan "true, real"

**Distribution / Notes**: **MODERATELY SUPPORTED** across 6/8 languages. Sanskrit shows this most clearly in philosophical contexts (आभास vs. reality). Greek shows clear lexicalization (εικονικός/πραγματικός). English, Tagalog, Quechua show the distinction through appearance verbs versus reality terms. Chinese and Swahili show less clear lexicalization in core truth vocabulary. Hebrew shows less systematic treatment. This dimension appears more elaborated in languages with philosophical traditions.

---

### DIMENSION 17: CONSISTENCY (INTERNAL COHERENCE) vs. CORRESPONDENCE (EXTERNAL MATCH)

**Definition**: This dimension distinguishes evaluation of internal consistency or coherence (whether parts fit together logically) from evaluation of correspondence to external reality (whether something matches facts or objects). The former is about logical/structural integrity; the latter is about external validation.

**Representative Languages & Terms**:

- **Chinese**: 一致 (yīzhì) "consistent, coherent" (言行一致 "words match deeds") vs. 符合 (fúhé) "correspond to, accord with" (符合事实 "accord with facts")
- **English**: consistent/coherent vs. correspond/match (to reality)
- **Sanskrit**: (treats through logical frameworks but less clearly lexicalized as dimension)
- **Greek**: (less clearly lexicalized in core truth vocabulary)

**Distribution / Notes**: **WEAKLY ATTESTED** across languages. Chinese shows clearest lexicalization through 一致 (internal consistency) versus 符合 (external correspondence). English distinguishes consistent from true/accurate. Other languages show less clear lexicalization of this dimension. Hebrew, Greek, Tagalog, Swahili, Quechua, Sanskrit do not show systematic distinction in core truth vocabulary. This may be a dimension more relevant to philosophical/logical discourse than everyday truth-evaluation.

---

### DIMENSION 18: PRETENSE/SIMULATION vs. GENUINENESS

**Definition**: This dimension evaluates whether behavior, emotions, or displays are genuine expressions of actual states versus deliberate pretense, acting, or simulation. It concerns the distinction between authentic self-expression and performed/simulated behavior.

**Representative Languages & Terms**:

- **Chinese**: 假装 (jiǎzhuāng) "pretend, feign" vs. 真诚 (zhēnchéng) "sincere, genuine"
- **Hebrew**: (less clearly lexicalized; uses general deception vocabulary)
- **Greek**: υποκρισία (ipokrisía) "hypocrisy, pretense" / υποκριτής (ipokritís) "hypocrite" vs. ειλικρινής (eilikrinís) "sincere"
- **English**: pretend/fake vs. genuine/sincere
- **Tagalog**: kunwari "pretend, feign" / palabás "pretense, show" vs. tunay "genuine"
- **Swahili**: (less clearly lexicalized in core vocabulary)
- **Quechua**: (less clearly lexicalized; uses llulla for deception generally)

**Distribution / Notes**: **MODERATELY SUPPORTED** across 5/8 languages. Tagalog shows richest vocabulary (kunwari, palabás, gawâ-gawâ). Greek υποκρισία specifically targets pretense/hypocrisy. Chinese, English show clear pretense vocabulary. Hebrew, Swahili, Quechua, Sanskrit show less systematic lexicalization of pretense as distinct from general deception. This dimension appears more elaborated in languages emphasizing behavioral authenticity.

---

### DIMENSION 19: RIGHTEOUSNESS/MORAL UPRIGHTNESS vs. FACTUAL CORRECTNESS

**Definition**: This dimension distinguishes moral virtue, righteousness, or proper conduct (ethical domain) from simple factual correctness or accuracy (epistemic domain). Some languages maintain clear separation; others show overlap or conflation.

**Representative Languages & Terms**:

- **Sanskrit**: धर्म (dharma) "righteousness, duty, proper conduct" vs. सत्य (satya) "truth (factual)"
- **Greek**: δικαιοσύνη (dikaiosýni) "justice, righteousness" vs. αλήθεια (alítheia) "truth"
- **English**: righteous/just vs. true/correct (though right shows polysemy)
- **Hebrew**: צדק (tsedek) conflates both domains
- **Swahili**: haki conflates both domains
- **Chinese**: 正义 (zhèngyì) "justice, righteousness" vs. 正确 (zhèngquè) "correct" (separate compounds from same root 正)

**Distribution / Notes**: **VARIABLE CROSS-LINGUISTICALLY**. Most languages (6/8) maintain lexical separation between moral and epistemic domains, but Hebrew צדק and Swahili haki show significant conflation. Chinese uses same root (正) in different compounds. English right shows polysemy. Quechua allin covers both without clear distinction. This overlaps with DIMENSION 3 but focuses specifically on moral virtue rather than general normativity.

---

### DIMENSION 20: EVIDENCE/PROOF (SUPPORT) vs. TRUTH (CLAIM)

**Definition**: This dimension distinguishes the evidence, proof, or testimony that supports claims (evidential basis) from the claims or truths themselves (propositional content). The former provides justification; the latter is what is justified.

**Representative Languages & Terms**:

- **Chinese**: 证据 (zhèngjù) "evidence, proof" vs. 事实 (shìshí) "fact, truth"
- **Hebrew**: ראיה (ra'aya) "evidence, proof" vs. אמת (emet) "truth"
- **Sanskrit**: हेतु (hetu) "reason, evidence" / प्रमाण (pramāṇa) "valid means" vs. सत्य (satya) "truth"
- **Greek**: απόδειξη (apódeixi) "proof" vs. αλήθεια (alítheia) "truth"
- **English**: evidence/proof vs. truth/fact
- **Tagalog**: katunayan "proof, evidence" vs. katotohanan "truth"
- **Swahili**: ushahidi "evidence, testimony" / hoja "argument, proof" vs. ukweli "truth"

**Distribution / Notes**: **UNIVERSALLY DISTINGUISHED** across all 8 languages. All languages maintain clear distinction between evidential support and propositional content. This suggests universal pragmatic importance of distinguishing what is claimed from what supports the claim. The distinction appears fundamental to argumentative and legal discourse across cultures.

### Notes on Dimensional Interactions and Overlaps

1. **Dimensions 3 and 19** (Factual vs. Moral/Normative Rightness and Righteousness vs. Correctness) show significant overlap. They may represent a single underlying dimension with variation in which aspects are lexicalized.

2. **Dimensions 1 and 13** (Propositional vs. Material and Relational Authenticity) both concern authenticity but in different domains (objects vs. relationships). They may reflect a general AUTHENTICITY dimension that applies across multiple domains.

### Cross-Cutting Observations

**Asymmetries Across Dimensions**:

- **Negative pole more elaborated**: Across multiple dimensions, the negative pole (lying, deception, error, fakeness) shows richer vocabulary than positive pole (truth-telling, honesty, correctness, genuineness). This appears in agentive nominalizations (liar vs. truth-teller) and general lexical elaboration.

- **Causative asymmetries**: Verification/confirmation (making true) more productive than falsification (making false) across languages, suggesting truth-establishment conceptualized as active process while falsity may be seen as default or passive state.

- **Moral vs. epistemic elaboration**: Languages vary in whether moral dimensions (honesty, righteousness, sincerity) are as elaborated as epistemic dimensions (accuracy, correspondence, validity). Sanskrit and Chinese show strong elaboration in both; Quechua shows less differentiation.

**Register and Domain Effects**:

- **Philosophical registers**: Sanskrit and Greek show most elaboration in philosophical dimensions (conventional/ultimate truth, epistemological validity, appearance/reality)
- **Legal/administrative registers**: Hebrew, Greek, English, Swahili show most elaboration in validity/legitimacy dimension
- **Technical/scientific registers**: Chinese, Hebrew, Greek, English show most elaboration in precision/accuracy dimension
- **Interpersonal/moral registers**: All languages show elaboration in honesty/sincerity and deception dimensions

This suggests semantic dimensions are partly universal (deception vs. error, evidence vs. claim) and partly register-dependent (precision in technical contexts, validity in legal contexts, ultimate truth in philosophical contexts).

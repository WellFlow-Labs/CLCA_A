# CLCA Protocol v3.2 — P1 CORE VOCABULARY COLLECTION
# Language: English (en)

---

**1. true**
- Form: true
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal meaning: OE *trēowe* "faithful, trustworthy"
- Typical English translations: (source language)

Used to predicate accuracy or correspondence of statements, representations, or beliefs to facts. Also applied to persons or objects to indicate genuineness or fidelity to type (e.g., *a true friend*, *true north*). Participates in derivation: *truth*, *truly*, *truism*, *untrue*.

**2. false**
- Form: false
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal meaning: L *falsus* "deceived, mistaken, feigned"
- Typical English translations: (source language)

Predicates inaccuracy, misrepresentation, or lack of correspondence to fact. Also used of objects or persons to indicate inauthenticity or deceptive appearance (e.g., *false teeth*, *false pretenses*). Derivational family: *falsity*, *falsify*, *falsification*, *falsely*.

**[CORE]**

**3. genuine**
- Form: genuine
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal meaning: L *genuinus* "native, innate" (possibly from *genu* "knee," referring to paternal acknowledgment)
- Typical English translations: (source language)

Applied to objects, persons, or emotions to indicate that they are what they purport to be, without adulteration or pretense. Contrasts with *fake*, *counterfeit*, and *spurious*. Derivational family: *genuinely*, *genuineness*.

**[CORE]**

**4. authentic**
- Form: authentic
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal meaning: Gk *authentikos* "original, primary, acting on one's own authority"
- Typical English translations: (source language)

Used of documents, artworks, persons, and experiences to indicate that they are original, unforged, or true to their claimed origin or nature. In philosophical and popular usage, also applied to persons acting in accordance with their own values rather than external pressure. Derivational family: *authenticity*, *authenticate*, *authentically*, *authentication*.

**[CORE]**

**5. accurate**
- Form: accurate
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal meaning: L *accuratus* "done with care," from *ad-* + *cura* "care"
- Typical English translations: (source language)

Predicates close correspondence between a representation, measurement, or description and the thing it represents. Commonly used in scientific, journalistic, and everyday evaluative contexts. Derivational family: *accuracy*, *accurately*, *inaccurate*, *inaccuracy*.

**[CORE]**

**6. correct**
- Form: correct
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal meaning: L *correctus*, past participle of *corrigere* "to set right"
- Typical English translations: (source language)

Applied to statements, answers, procedures, and conduct to indicate conformity with a standard, rule, or fact. Overlaps with *accurate* but more commonly used where a definite right answer or norm exists. Derivational family: *correctly*, *correctness*, *incorrect*, *correction*, *corrective*.

**[CORE]**

**7. valid**
- Form: valid
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal meaning: L *validus* "strong, effective," from *valere* "to be strong"
- Typical English translations: (source language)

In logic, predicates that an argument's conclusion follows necessarily from its premises. In legal and general usage, indicates that something is properly constituted, binding, or well-founded. Derivational family: *validity*, *validate*, *invalid*, *invalidate*, *validation*.

**[CORE]**

**8. sound**
- Form: sound (adj.)
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal meaning: OE *gesund* "healthy, whole"
- Typical English translations: (source language)

In logic, predicates that an argument is both valid and has true premises. In general usage, applied to reasoning, judgment, or structures to indicate reliability and freedom from flaw. Derivational family: *soundness*, *soundly*, *unsound*.

**[CORE]**

**9. reliable**
- Form: reliable
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal meaning: *rely* (from Fr *relier* "to bind") + *-able*
- Typical English translations: (source language)

Applied to persons, sources, instruments, or methods to indicate that they can be depended upon to perform consistently and accurately. Commonly used in evaluations of testimony, evidence, and character. Derivational family: *reliability*, *reliably*, *unreliable*, *unreliability*.

**[CORE]**

**10. credible**
- Form: credible
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal meaning: L *credibilis* "worthy of belief," from *credere* "to believe"
- Typical English translations: (source language)

Predicates that a person, claim, or source is worthy of belief, typically on the basis of plausibility or established trustworthiness. Distinct from *reliable* in focusing on believability rather than consistent performance. Derivational family: *credibility*, *credibly*, *incredible*, *incredibility*.

**11. fake**
- Form: fake
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal meaning: origin uncertain; possibly from German *fegen* "to sweep, polish" via criminal slang
- Typical English translations: (source language)

Applied to objects, persons, or statements that are presented as genuine but are not. Functions as adjective, noun, and verb. Derivational family: *faker*, *fakery*, *faked*.

**12. counterfeit**
- Form: counterfeit
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal meaning: OFr *contrefait* "made in imitation," from *contre-* + *faire* "to make"
- Typical English translations: (source language)

Primarily applied to currency, documents, and goods that are fraudulent imitations of genuine articles. Carries a stronger implication of deliberate deception than *fake* in many contexts. Derivational family: *counterfeiter*, *counterfeiting*.

**[CORE]**

**13. spurious**
- Form: spurious
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal meaning: L *spurius* "illegitimate, false"
- Typical English translations: (source language)

Applied to claims, arguments, documents, or objects that appear genuine or well-founded but are not. More formal and often used in academic, legal, or scholarly registers. Derivational family: *spuriously*, *spuriousness*.

**14. legitimate**
- Form: legitimate
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal meaning: ML *legitimatus*, from L *lex/legis* "law"
- Typical English translations: (source language)

Predicates conformity with law, rules, or accepted standards, and by extension genuine or rightful status. Used in legal, political, and general evaluative contexts. Derivational family: *legitimacy*, *legitimately*, *illegitimate*, *legitimize*, *legitimation*.

**15. veridical**
- Form: veridical
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal meaning: L *veridicus* "truth-telling," from *verus* "true" + *dicere* "to say"
- Typical English translations: (source language)

Used primarily in philosophical and psychological contexts to describe perceptions, reports, or representations that accurately correspond to reality. Contrasts with *illusory* or *hallucinatory* in perceptual contexts. Derivational family: *veridicality*, *veridically*.

**16. truthful**
- Form: truthful
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal meaning: *truth* (OE *trēowþ*) + *-ful*
- Typical English translations: (source language)

Applied to persons or statements to indicate a disposition or tendency toward telling the truth. Focuses on the agent's honesty rather than the correspondence of a specific proposition to fact. Derivational family: *truthfully*, *truthfulness*, *untruthful*.

**17. sincere**
- Form: sincere
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal meaning: L *sincerus* "whole, pure, genuine" (etymology disputed; possibly *semel* "once" + *cera* "wax," referring to unrepaired pottery)
- Typical English translations: (source language)

Predicates that a person's expressed feelings, beliefs, or intentions genuinely correspond to their inner states, without pretense or dissimulation. Distinct from *truthful* in focusing on the match between expression and inner state rather than between statement and external fact. Derivational family: *sincerity*, *sincerely*, *insincere*, *insincerity*.

**18. honest**

- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal meaning: OFr *honeste*, from L *honestus* "honorable," from *honor*
- Typical English translations: (source language)

Applied to persons, statements, and conduct to indicate freedom from deception, fraud, or concealment. Broader in scope than *truthful*, encompassing fairness and integrity in dealings as well as verbal accuracy. Derivational family: *honesty*, *honestly*, *dishonest*, *dishonesty*.

- Typical English translations: (source language)

Applied to translations, reproductions, accounts, and persons to indicate close adherence to an original, a commitment, or a relationship. In evaluative contexts, often predicates accuracy of representation relative to a source. Derivational family: *faithfully*, *faithfulness*, *unfaithful*, *unfaithfulness*.

- Typical English translations: (source language)

Predicates precise correspondence or conformity, often with an implication of leaving no margin for error or approximation. Used of measurements, reproductions, quotations, and descriptions. Derivational family: *exactly*, *exactness*, *exactitude*, *inexact*.

Predicates that a claim or statement is capable of being checked against evidence or observation. Commonly used in scientific, journalistic, and philosophical contexts. Derivational family: *verifiability*, *verify*, *verification*, *unverifiable*.

**22. bona fide**

- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal meaning: L "in good faith"
- Typical English translations: genuine, in good faith, authentic

Used as an adjective or adverb to indicate that something is genuine, without fraud or deception, or that an action is undertaken sincerely. Common in legal and formal registers but also in general use. Functions as a fixed expression with limited internal modification.

Applied to stories, sayings, or accounts that are widely circulated but of doubtful authenticity or authorship. Carries an implication that the item is probably not genuine, though it may be difficult to disprove. Derivational family: *apocryphally*, *apocrypha*.

Applied to arguments, claims, or reasoning that appear sound or well-founded on the surface but are actually flawed or misleading. Emphasizes the deceptive plausibility of the item being evaluated. Derivational family: *speciously*, *speciousness*.

**27. ersatz**

- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal meaning: German *Ersatz* "replacement, substitute"
- Typical English translations: (source language; borrowed)

Applied to objects or experiences that are inferior substitutes for the genuine article, often with a connotation of inadequacy or artificiality. Borrowed into English from German, primarily in the twentieth century. Derivational productivity in English is limited.

**28. pseudo-**
- Form: pseudo-
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal meaning: Gk *pseudēs* "false, lying"
- Typical English translations: (source language; productive prefix)

A productive prefix attached to nouns and adjectives to indicate that something falsely claims or resembles the category named (e.g., *pseudoscience*, *pseudo-intellectual*, *pseudonym*). Widely used in academic, scientific, and general registers. Not a free-standing lexical item but included as a highly productive bound form in this evaluative domain.

**29. plausible**
- Form: plausible
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal meaning: L *plausibilis* "deserving applause," from *plaudere* "to applaud"
- Typical English translations: (source language)

Predicates that a claim, explanation, or account seems reasonable or believable on the basis of available evidence or general knowledge, without asserting that it is actually true. Occupies a weaker epistemic position than *credible* in many uses. Derivational family: *plausibility*, *plausibly*, *implausible*, *implausibility*.

**30. canonical**
- Form: canonical
- Transliteration: N/A
- Literal meaning: Gk/L *canonicus* "according to rule," from *kanon* "rule, standard"
- Typical English translations: (source language)

Applied to texts, interpretations, versions, or examples that are recognized as authoritative, standard, or definitively representative of a category. Used in literary, religious, philosophical, and general academic contexts. Derivational family: *canonically*, *canon*, *canonize*, *canonization*.

# CLCA Protocol v3.2 — P1 CORE VOCABULARY COLLECTION
# Language: Swahili (sw)

---

**Note on script and transliteration:** Swahili is standardly written in the Latin alphabet; no separate transliteration column is required. Morphological breakdowns are provided where relevant.

---

**1. kweli**
- Form: kweli
- Literal meaning: truth, truthfulness (from Arabic *qawl*, "speech/saying")
- Typical English translations: truth, true, truly, really
- Swahili is a Bantu language with significant Arabic loanwords, and *kweli* is among the most frequent. It functions as both a noun ("ukweli" in its noun-class form) and an adverb/adjective, appearing in statements like *hii ni kweli* ("this is true"). It is used across everyday, moral, legal, and religious contexts.
- [CORE]

---

**2. ukweli**
- Form: ukweli
- Literal meaning: u- (abstract noun prefix, class 11/14) + *kweli* (truth)
- Typical English translations: truth, truthfulness, veracity, fact
- The nominalized form of *kweli*, *ukweli* refers to the abstract quality or state of being true. It appears in formal, philosophical, and legal discourse (*ukweli wa jambo*, "the truth of a matter") and is highly productive as a noun.
- [CORE]

---

**3. uongo**
- Form: uongo
- Literal meaning: u- (abstract noun prefix) + *-ongo* (falsehood, deception)
- Typical English translations: lie, falsehood, untruth, deception
- *Uongo* is the direct antonym of *ukweli* and is used to characterize statements, persons, or representations as false or deceptive. It appears in moral condemnation (*kusema uongo*, "to tell a lie") and in legal contexts.
- [CORE]

---

**4. halisi**
- Form: halisi
- Literal meaning: genuine, real, authentic (from Arabic *khāliṣ*, "pure, unmixed")
- Typical English translations: genuine, authentic, real, pure
- *Halisi* is used to evaluate objects, persons, or claims as authentic rather than counterfeit or imitative. It appears in commercial contexts (*dhahabu halisi*, "genuine gold") and in broader evaluative discourse about character or identity.
- [CORE]

---

**5. sahihi**
- Form: sahihi
- Literal meaning: correct, right, accurate (from Arabic *ṣaḥīḥ*, "sound, correct")
- Typical English translations: correct, accurate, right, exact, valid
- *Sahihi* is used to evaluate statements, calculations, documents, and conduct as accurate or correct. It is extremely common in educational, administrative, and everyday contexts (*jibu sahihi*, "correct answer"; *hati sahihi*, "valid document").
- [CORE]

---

**6. usahihi**
- Form: usahihi
- Literal meaning: u- (abstract noun prefix) + *sahihi* (correctness)
- Typical English translations: accuracy, correctness, precision, validity
- The abstract nominalization of *sahihi*, used to refer to the property of being accurate or correct. It appears in formal and technical discourse, including legal and scientific contexts (*usahihi wa taarifa*, "accuracy of a report").
- [CORE]

---

**7. bandia**
- Form: bandia
- Literal meaning: artificial, fake, counterfeit (origin debated; possibly from Arabic *band*, "made/manufactured")
- Typical English translations: fake, counterfeit, artificial, imitation, forged
- *Bandia* is used to characterize objects, documents, or representations as not genuine. It contrasts directly with *halisi* and appears in commercial, legal, and everyday evaluative contexts (*pesa bandia*, "counterfeit money"; *bidhaa bandia*, "fake goods").
- [CORE]

---

**8. haki**
- Form: haki
- Literal meaning: right, justice, truth (from Arabic *ḥaqq*, "right, truth, reality")
- Typical English translations: right, justice, truth, entitlement, fairness
- *Haki* covers a wide semantic range including moral rightness, legal entitlement, and truth. It is used in assertions (*haki kabisa*, "absolutely right/true"), moral discourse, and legal contexts (*haki za binadamu*, "human rights").
- [CORE]

---

**9. uhalisi**
- Form: uhalisi
- Literal meaning: u- (abstract noun prefix) + *halisi* (genuine/real)
- Typical English translations: reality, authenticity, genuineness, realism
- *Uhalisi* refers to the state or quality of being real or authentic. It is used in philosophical, artistic, and everyday contexts (*uhalisi wa hali*, "the reality of the situation") and is the basis for the literary/artistic term *uhalisia* (realism).
- [CORE]

---

**10. uhalisia**
- Form: uhalisia
- Literal meaning: u- (abstract noun prefix) + *halisi* + *-a* (nominalizing/adjectivizing suffix)
- Typical English translations: realism, authenticity (in aesthetic or philosophical sense)
- *Uhalisia* is used particularly in literary and artistic criticism to denote the quality of faithfully representing reality. It is somewhat more domain-specific than *uhalisi* but well attested in Swahili literary discourse.
- [MARGINAL]

---

**11. kweli kweli**
- Form: kweli kweli
- Literal meaning: truth truth (reduplication of *kweli*)
- Typical English translations: really, truly, genuinely, in truth
- Reduplication of *kweli* intensifies the assertion of truth or genuineness. It is used in spoken and written Swahili to emphasize that something is genuinely the case (*hii ni kweli kweli*, "this is really true"). Reduplication as an intensifying strategy is productive in Swahili.
- [CORE]

---

**12. dhahiri**
- Form: dhahiri
- Literal meaning: evident, manifest, clear (from Arabic *ẓāhir*, "apparent, manifest")
- Typical English translations: evident, obvious, manifest, clear, apparent
- *Dhahiri* is used to characterize something as plainly or manifestly the case, often in evaluative or argumentative contexts. It appears in both everyday speech and formal discourse (*ni dhahiri kwamba*, "it is evident that").
- [CORE]

---

**13. uaminifu**
- Form: uaminifu
- Literal meaning: u- (abstract noun prefix) + *amini* (to believe/trust) + *-fu* (adjectival/nominal suffix)
- Typical English translations: faithfulness, trustworthiness, reliability, fidelity, honesty
- *Uaminifu* refers to the quality of being trustworthy, faithful, or honest, applied to persons, representations, or conduct. It is used in moral, religious, and professional contexts (*uaminifu wa mfanyakazi*, "an employee's reliability").
- [CORE]

---

**14. amini**
- Form: amini
- Literal meaning: to believe, to trust (from Arabic *amana*, "to be trustworthy/faithful")
- Typical English translations: to believe, to trust, to have faith in; (as adjective) trustworthy, faithful
- *Amini* functions as a verb and, in some constructions, as an adjective. It is used in evaluating whether something is credible or whether a person is trustworthy (*siwezi kumwamini*, "I cannot trust him/her").
- [CORE]

---

**15. udanganyifu**
- Form: udanganyifu
- Literal meaning: u- (abstract noun prefix) + *danganya* (to deceive) + *-ifu* (nominal suffix)
- Typical English translations: deception, deceitfulness, fraud, dishonesty
- *Udanganyifu* refers to the quality or act of deceiving, used in moral and legal condemnation of false representations or conduct. It is productively derived from the verb *kudanganya* ("to deceive") and appears in formal and everyday discourse.
- [CORE]

---

**16. kudanganya**
- Form: kudanganya
- Literal meaning: ku- (infinitive prefix) + *danganya* (to deceive, to mislead)
- Typical English translations: to deceive, to lie, to mislead, to cheat
- *Kudanganya* is the infinitive form of the verb meaning to deceive or mislead. It is used to characterize acts of producing false impressions in others, in moral, legal, and everyday contexts (*alikudanganya*, "he/she deceived you").
- [CORE]

---

**17. ulaghai**
- Form: ulaghai
- Literal meaning: u- (abstract noun prefix) + *laghai* (fraud, trickery; from Arabic *laghā*, "vain/worthless speech")
- Typical English translations: fraud, trickery, swindling, deceit
- *Ulaghai* refers specifically to fraudulent or swindling conduct and is used in legal and moral contexts. It is somewhat more specific than *udanganyifu*, carrying connotations of deliberate material fraud (*kesi ya ulaghai*, "a fraud case").
- [CORE]

---

**18. haki ya kweli**
- Form: haki ya kweli
- Literal meaning: *haki* (right/truth) + *ya* (of) + *kweli* (truth)
- Typical English translations: the real truth, the genuine right, the true fact
- This fixed expression is used to emphasize that something is genuinely true or genuinely one's right, often in assertive or argumentative contexts. It combines two high-frequency evaluative terms and is attested in both spoken and written Swahili.
- [CORE]

---

**19. asili**
- Form: asili
- Literal meaning: origin, nature, essence (from Arabic *aṣl*, "root, origin, essence")
- Typical English translations: origin, nature, essence, original, native
- *Asili* is used to refer to the fundamental nature or original character of something or someone. It appears in evaluative contexts where authenticity or essential character is at issue (*asili yake ni nzuri*, "his/her nature is good"; *lugha ya asili*, "native/original language").
- [CORE]

---

**20. uasili**
- Form: uasili
- Literal meaning: u- (abstract noun prefix) + *asili* (origin/essence)
- Typical English translations: originality, nativeness, authenticity, essential nature
- *Uasili* is the abstract nominalization of *asili*, referring to the quality of being original or of one's essential nature. It is used in aesthetic and philosophical contexts, though somewhat less frequently than *asili* itself.
- [MARGINAL]

---

**21. bora**
- Form: bora
- Literal meaning: best, excellent, superior (from Arabic *bāri'*, "excellent, outstanding")
- Typical English translations: best, excellent, superior, ideal, better
- *Bora* is used in evaluative contexts to characterize something as of high quality or as the best option. It appears in comparative and superlative constructions (*bora zaidi*, "even better") and in aesthetic and practical evaluation.
- [CORE]

---

**22. kasoro**
- Form: kasoro
- Literal meaning: defect, flaw, shortcoming (from Arabic *qaṣūr*, "deficiency, shortcoming")
- Typical English translations: defect, flaw, fault, shortcoming, imperfection
- *Kasoro* is used to identify a deficiency or flaw in an object, person, or representation. It appears in quality assessment, legal, and moral contexts (*bidhaa yenye kasoro*, "a defective product").
- [CORE]

---

**23. makosa**
- Form: makosa
- Literal meaning: ma- (plural noun prefix, class 6) + *kosa* (error, mistake, fault)
- Typical English translations: errors, mistakes, faults, wrongs
- *Makosa* (plural of *kosa*) refers to errors, mistakes, or moral wrongs. It is used in evaluating conduct, statements, or work as incorrect or blameworthy (*kufanya makosa*, "to make mistakes/errors"; *makosa ya kisheria*, "legal offenses").
- [CORE]

---

**24. kosa**
- Form: kosa
- Literal meaning: error, mistake, fault, offense (singular of *makosa*)
- Typical English translations: error, mistake, fault, wrong, offense
- *Kosa* is the singular form, used in both everyday and formal evaluative contexts. It can refer to factual errors, moral wrongs, or legal offenses (*kosa kubwa*, "a serious mistake/offense").
- [CORE]

---

**25. mfano**
- Form: mfano
- Literal meaning: m- (noun class 3 prefix) + *fano* (likeness, resemblance; cf. *fanana*, "to resemble")
- Typical English translations: example, likeness, resemblance, model, parable
- *Mfano* is used in contexts of resemblance and representation, including evaluations of how closely something resembles something else. It also appears in aesthetic and didactic contexts (*mfano mzuri*, "a good example/likeness").
- [CORE]

---

**26. fanana**
- Form: fanana
- Literal meaning: to resemble, to be alike (verb; related to *mfano*)
- Typical English translations: to resemble, to be similar, to be alike, to match
- *Fanana* is used to evaluate the degree of resemblance between two things or persons. It appears in aesthetic, legal (e.g., identifying persons or objects), and everyday contexts (*vinafanana sana*, "they resemble each other closely").
- [CORE]

---

**27. tofauti**
- Form: tofauti
- Literal meaning: difference, different (from Arabic *tafāwut*, "difference, disparity")
- Typical English translations: difference, different, distinct, discrepancy
- *Tofauti* is used to evaluate whether two things differ, including in contexts where accuracy or resemblance is at issue. It appears in comparative evaluations and in identifying discrepancies (*kuna tofauti*, "there is a difference/discrepancy").
- [CORE]

---

**28. thibitisha**
- Form: thibitisha
- Literal meaning: to confirm, to verify, to prove (causative of *thibiti*; from Arabic *thabbata*, "to establish firmly")
- Typical English translations: to confirm, to verify, to prove, to authenticate, to validate
- *Thibitisha* is used in contexts of verification and confirmation of claims, documents, or facts. It is productive in legal, administrative, and everyday contexts (*thibitisha utambulisho wako*, "verify your identity").
- [CORE]

---

**29. uthibitisho**
- Form: uthibitisho
- Literal meaning: u- (abstract noun prefix) + *thibitisha* (to confirm/verify) + *-o* (nominalizing suffix)
- Typical English translations: confirmation, verification, proof, authentication, validation
- *Uthibitisho* is the nominalization of *thibitisha*, referring to the act or result of confirming or verifying. It appears in legal, administrative, and formal contexts (*uthibitisho wa hati*, "authentication of a document").
- [CORE]

---

**30. uongo mtupu**
- Form: uongo mtupu
- Literal meaning: *uongo* (falsehood) + *mtupu* (pure, mere, nothing but)
- Typical English translations: pure falsehood, outright lie, nothing but lies
- This fixed expression intensifies the characterization of something as entirely false. *Mtupu* functions as an intensifier meaning "nothing but" or "sheer," and the combination is attested in spoken and written Swahili in moral and argumentative contexts.
- [CORE]
